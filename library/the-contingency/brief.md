## Premise

A few generations into a future that ran out of room. The United States still exists — there is still a president, still a flag, still an apparatus that calls itself a government — but the country is a husk maintaining pockets of high technology over a continent stripped of nearly everything else. The resources are gone. The grandeur is gone. What remains is advanced and threadbare at once: a state that can still fold space — there is a gate, a platform, a working teleport — but can barely feed the people who operate it.

The story opens on the night of a state dinner. The president is hosting a send-off for **the Contingency**: a small force of highly trained marines being sent, one-way, through the gate — to the only place humanity has found that might let it survive. The dinner is the tell. The most powerful man left in America hosts a banquet that looks like a mid-tier company function; the decline is rendered in the catering and the table settings before a word is spoken about it. During his speech the president reads the marines' names and ranks aloud, and one by one they step to a nondescript platform at his right — a brief wash of light, and they are gone. The roll call is a vanishing. The reader understands quickly that no one comes back.

**Dr. Imani Reyes** (working name), in her fifties, is the xenobiologist who has spent months failing to understand the one thing that has ever come the *other* way through the gate: a creature, caged and veiled in a yard behind the banquet hall. It is bestial, not of Earth, and — she is increasingly, unprovably certain — intelligent. Nothing sedates it. Every attempt to study it by force has yielded nothing. She is the one who told the president, plainly, that they cannot learn what it is, which is why the marines are going in blind, and why she has the run of the hall and the president's ear. Once, the creature made a sound directly at her that cost her part of her hearing and has never fully given it back. She has never been able to decide whether that was an attack or the only word it had. Tonight another contingent ships into the place it came from, and her time to prove anything is the length of a dinner.

**Corporal Eli Brandt** (working name), enlisted, is one of the marines in the Contingency — and in the same small unit as the president's son, who has put his own name on a list he was never required to join. Brandt believes. In a dead country, with the gate the only door left, he has decided this is the last real chance the species has, and he intends to be the point of the spear. His thread is the road to the platform: the waiting, the drill, the gallows humor of men who know it is one-way, and the unreadable fact of the president's son standing in the same formation, choosing the same light.

The two never meet. Reyes never goes through the gate; Brandt never leaves it. They share one night and one ceremony, and nothing else. The book is what each of them carries toward the platform — and, when the light takes Brandt and his thread simply ends, what Reyes is left holding in the silence after.

## Length and shape

- Target length: ~40,000 words
- Number of chapters: 16

The chapters alternate POV — Reyes, Brandt, Reyes, Brandt — across narrative time, **until the gate.** Brandt's final chapter ends on the flash of the platform, and there is no Brandt chapter after it. The chapters that remain are Reyes alone. The alternation is the book's clock, and its breaking is the book's grief: one of the two voices simply stops, mid-book, and never returns. The plotter chooses which POV opens; once chosen, the alternation holds until it severs. Across ~16 chapters this falls roughly nine Reyes / seven Brandt — the asymmetry is the point, not a flaw to balance out.

Chapter lengths vary with the beat (roughly 1,800–4,000 words). The quiet day-frame chapters run shorter; the send-off night runs longer.

**Structural frame.** A four-part axis around the send-off, asymmetric at the close:

- **Day-before** — each man in his world as it was before the Contingency was named. Reyes at her work with the caged thing on an ordinary day; Brandt in the ordinary texture of an enlisted life not yet chosen for the gate. The prose observes; it does not foreshadow.
- **Approach** — Reyes's last failing attempts to prove what the creature is, against the clock of the dinner; Brandt's waiting, drill, and unit in the days before they ship, the president's son among them.
- **The send-off night** — the banquet, the roll call, the vanishings. Reyes's chapter is the spine of the original image: she drifts from the hall, follows a sound to the veiled cage, sees the thing whole, and the scream. Brandt's chapter is the formation and the step to the platform — and the flash that ends his thread.
- **Day-after (Reyes only)** — the dead morning. The empty platform, the next list already forming, what is left when the light has taken everyone it was going to take.

The plotter has discretion within this frame; the day-before openings, the severance at the gate, and the Reyes-only close are non-negotiable.

## Voice

Third-person limited past, bound tightly to whichever POV the chapter belongs to. The two registers must be felt as distinct within the first paragraph of any chapter.

- **Reyes's chapters**: spare, precise — the interiority of a scientist whose life's work is a failure of comprehension. The lab's vocabulary in stride (containment, assay, the specimen) without lecturing the reader. Her dread and grief reach her in the language of method: what she has tried, what has not worked, what she cannot rule out. Restrained literary prose, near the register of *the cold signal*, but turned from skepticism toward the particular helplessness of a person who is certain of something she cannot prove. The hearing the creature took from her is a recurring sensory thread — the muffling, the ring, voices arriving as if underwater — available as texture and motif, not stamped onto every chapter.
- **Brandt's chapters**: faster, coarser — the enlisted grunt's voice, a register this library has never carried. Profane but never cartoon; gallows humor as the medium men use to carry fear; in-group jargon and the rhythm of people living in close quarters under orders. Dialect and profanity in idiom and cadence, not in phonetic misspelling. Crucially, this voice is **earnest underneath** — Brandt believes, and the belief gives the banter its gravity. This is not a wink to the reader. The humor is how frightened men cope; it never signals that the book is in on a joke.

Across both: restrained prose in a period of its own. Action when action calls — but the book's only "action" is a scream and a vanishing; there is no combat on the page, ever. Reflection clipped. The story trusts the reader to feel what is not said.

## Audience and register

- **Target audience age range**: adult literary
- **Voice pace**: medium — Reyes's chapters breathe; Brandt's tighten; neither sprawls
- **Reading-grade target**: college
- **POV count intent**: two co-leads, strict alternation that severs at the gate
- **Chapter-ending convention**: mixed — resolved beats and mid-action both, no manufactured cliffhangers. Brandt's chapters may end on the cut; the book closes on a literary fade, Reyes alone.

## Exemplar passages

Voice anchors (named for the architect — paste actual openings here if you want sharper register):

- **Joe Haldeman, *The Forever War*** — soldiers sent through a gate to fight what they cannot understand, where the ache is the impossibility of return, not the combat. The spine of this book's dread. The most important exemplar.
- **Kazuo Ishiguro, *Never Let Me Go*** — an institution that sends its people to a fate, told in flat, devastating restraint, the horror assembled slowly beneath euphemism. The model for Reyes's register and for how the one-way truth is withheld.
- **Michael Shaara, *The Killer Angels*** — restrained third-person-limited per chapter, military vocabulary in stride, interiority earned and brief. The model for soldiers rendered without inflating the prose. Anchor for Brandt's chapters.
- **Peter Watts, *Blindsight*** (texture only) — an alien intelligence that is genuinely, structurally incomprehensible; the horror is epistemic. Anchor for how the creature resists being read — without Watts's density.

Unef's first starships had been possessed of a kind of spidery, delicate beauty. With them as a reference, we could see that the letters were a good hundred meters tall. That didn't mean there was going to be plenty of elbow-room. In its belly, the ship held six large tachyondrive fighters and fifty robot drones. The infantry was tucked off in a corner. War is the province of friction, Chuck von Clausewitz said; I had a feeling we were going to put him to the test.

Charlie had beaten me to the lounge and to the privilege of being first to evaluate the quality of Masaryk II's coffee.

"Rhinoceros bile," he said.

"At least it isn't soya," I said, taking a first cautious sip. Decided I might be longing for soya in a week.

The officers' lounge was a cubicle about three meters by four, metal floor and walls, with a coffee machine and a library readout.

## Constraints

- **The camera never follows the light.** No scene ever takes place on the far side of the gate. No alien planet, no expedition, no combat. Brandt's thread ends at the platform; what waits through the gate is never shown and never narrated. This is the book's central discipline — the equivalent of *the cold signal* never describing the signal.
- **No on-page combat anywhere.** The military apparatus is texture and metaphor, not action. The only violence the reader witnesses is the creature's scream.
- **The one-way truth is never explained.** Why the gate sends but nothing returns; how the creature came to be on Earth; what the destination is — none of it is resolved, by any character or by the prose. Reyes entertains several irreconcilable readings (it is *from* there; it is what people *become* there; it is the place's *answer* to being entered; it is what came *back* the last time they sent a contingent) and settles on none. The dread is real and unrelieved; there is no reveal. Treat it like the body on the breakwater in *coldwater reach*.
- **The creature's nature and intelligence stay unresolved.** We get Reyes's conviction that it is intelligent and the evidence she cannot make conclusive — never an answer. The prose neither confirms nor denies what it is. It is never given a point of view, a language, or a translated thought.
- **The president's son is never a POV character.** He is seen only from below — through Brandt in the formation, glimpsed by Reyes across the hall. His reasons for putting his name on the list are never explained from inside. The father reads the son's name aloud before he understands what he is reading; that beat is the emotional axis of the send-off and must not be over-explained.
- **The president is never a POV character, and is played straight.** He is a real, diminished man — an office reduced to symbol, presiding over a banquet that can barely be catered — not a satirical figure, not a clone, not a joke. Seen from below, like the senior officers in the historical books. His grief is rendered without being milked.
- **Earnest register only. No satire.** The propaganda of hope is never winked at. The diminished grandeur is rendered as fact and felt as loss, never played for irony. If a passage starts to nudge the reader to feel superior to the characters' hope, it has failed.
- **No "fate of humanity" narration.** The stakes are the largest imaginable, which is exactly why the prose must never announce them. The characters do not make speeches about the species; the reader is never told what the night means. The significance lives in the catering, the roll call, the empty platform — never in declaration.
- **Decline is rendered through texture, never exposition.** How dead the Earth is, how far the country has fallen — the reader assembles it from the dinner, the rationed grandeur, the threadbare apparatus surrounding world-folding technology. No infodumps, no expository history of the collapse. The same restraint the historical books use for period: the world is the air the characters breathe, not a lecture.
- **The two POVs never meet.** Reyes and Brandt share one night and one ceremony and never see, speak to, or know of each other. The send-off is the only thing they have in common.
- **The marine voice must not drift into generic military SF.** No gear fetishism, no "the rifle kicked against his shoulder," no banter that exists only to sound cool. The enlisted register is grounded in real military culture (see Research scope) and earns its texture from how people actually wait, drill, and cope — not from genre furniture.
- **Brandt believes.** His drive is conviction — that the Contingency is humanity's last real chance and that being on it is worth the one-way cost. The belief is sincere and is never undercut by the narration as naïveté. The book may let the reader fear he is wrong; it never lets the prose sneer at him for hoping.
- **The half-deafness is a motif, not a mechanic.** Use it where the chapter wants it — the muffled hall, the ring, the sense of meaning arriving just below the threshold of hearing. Do not stamp it onto every Reyes chapter; the restraint in `PHILOSOPHY.md` applies.
- **The ending is what Reyes is left holding.** No coda jumping past the day-after. The book closes on the dead morning after the send-off, in Reyes's POV, with the platform empty and the next list forming. Whatever she carries out of that night is what the reader gets. At least one thread — the truth, the creature, or her own choice about what to do with what she believes — stays deliberately unresolved.

## Research scope

Including this section enables the researcher. Keep it sharp: this is a largely invented world, and the researcher is here for one job — to ground the **real-world register anchors** the invented setting borrows, so the prose (Brandt's especially) reads true instead of generic. Do not research the invented future; research the real things it stands on.

```
- Enlisted military culture, contemporary US: rank structure and how a small special-mission unit is organized; the daily texture of drill, waiting, and close-quarters life; the in-group speech register and gallows humor of enlisted personnel — captured for idiom and rhythm, not for dialect spelling.
- The psychology of one-way and high-casualty missions: historical analogues for units sent on operations understood to be unsurvivable or non-returning (forlorn hopes, special-attack units, long-duration isolated postings). How people actually prepare, talk, and cope when they know the odds — grounded, not romanticized.
- The texture of shipping out: the processing, the ceremony, the last hours before a unit deploys. What a real send-off feels like from inside the ranks.
- High-containment study of a dangerous, intractable specimen: how real high-containment biology and animal-cognition research are actually conducted — vocabulary, procedure, and the limits of force-based study — to ground Reyes's failed work.
- Institutional ceremony under resource collapse: how declining states and shrinking institutions actually perform diminished grandeur — the real texture of a once-great office reduced. Used to ground the banquet.
```
