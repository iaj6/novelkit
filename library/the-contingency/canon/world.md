# World — *The Contingency*

## Period and Geography

Near-future United States, several generations after a resource collapse that was not a sudden catastrophe but an attrition — the granaries running lower, the fuel getting harder, the coasts and interiors thinning out until what remains is a country that functions at pockets. The continental geography is still named — still America — but large stretches are emptied out or abandoned. The government occupies a smaller footprint than it once did. The capital still has its ceremonial rooms; they are just harder to staff and harder to supply.

No year is given. No date is referenced in the prose. The world is the air the characters breathe, assembled from what they eat, what they can't get, and what the forms they sign actually say.

## The Technology Paradox

The defining condition of this world is that it retains high technology at its tips while the underlying civilization hollows out beneath them. The gate is real: a platform that folds space to one location, somewhere that may sustain life. It works. The country built it and operates it and cannot explain half of what it found on the way to building it. What it cannot do is feed the people who run it at the level they once expected. The servers and the facility are maintained; the catering is not.

This is not dystopian satire. The reader is never invited to feel clever about the irony. The decline is rendered as loss — felt, not announced.

## The Send-Off Compound

A federal compound in or near the former capital. The banquet hall is a real room: proper linens on the head tables, printed name cards (not calligraphed) at the tables further back. One branch of the honor guard. The military band is a quartet. The food is adequate and visibly procured rather than prepared: a fish course that is competent, not composed; a meat course that runs short before the back tables. Speeches and toasts are delivered from the same plain lectern — there is no separate ceremonial stand.

The platform — the gate's sending surface — stands at the president's right, elevated slightly on a gray slab. It is nondescript equipment: gray, functional, not decorated. The wash of light when it activates is brief. The vanishing is quieter than people expect.

## The Containment Yard

Behind the banquet hall, off a service corridor through a heavy door: the creature's enclosure. Not a formal BSL-4 clean room — the creature does not fit the biological safety categories that exist — but improvised high-containment: chain-link, poured concrete, an outer perimeter and an inner barrier. A heavy cloth veils the inner space; it has stayed in place since the second time they removed it, when things broke. Reyes works at the outer perimeter most nights. She has been inside the inner barrier twice and will not go again. The equipment brought to bear over eleven months has been substantial. It has yielded nothing the protocols can accommodate.

## The Marine Compound

On the same federal grounds, in buildings that were probably supply warehouses a decade ago: bunks arranged in configurations that suggest improvisation, shared bathrooms, a staging area with palletized gear under tarps and administrative lines that move slowly. Six weeks of restricted movement. An accelerated drill schedule. The waiting that military life is mostly made of, compressed into whatever time is left before the list ships out. The president's son is in the same compound. He bunks in the same general area. He drills at the same hours.

## Mood and Register

The word *apocalypse* is never used. The mood is the brief's register: earnest near-future speculative fiction in a literary register, told in the manner of institutions that are still going through their forms even as the forms mean less than they used to. The grandeur of the send-off ritual is real. The diminishment is also real. Both are held simultaneously and without comment. The reader assembles the scope of the decline from texture — the empty back tables, the one honor guard branch, the creature caged in a yard that no one in protocol ever anticipated — and is never told what it means.
