# Chapter 5 — Chain of Title

**POV**: Ellen Vance (third-person limited, past tense)  
**Summary**: Ellen visits the county clerk's office and pulls the deed and probate records for the Verrill Street property; she finds Della Markey's name and learns she died in the house in February 1983; she asks about coroner's records.  
**Clock**: Ellen's clock — May, present day  
**Chapter shape**: single scene (with brief transit)  
**Ending mode**: turn  
**Target words**: ~2,800  

---

## Setting / Time

The county clerk's office in the Greenbrier County courthouse, Lewisburg, West Virginia (or the fictional county's equivalent — the county seat near Renick). A public building with institutional light, file boxes, a counter. May, present day. Ellen has driven in from Renick. The records room is not dramatic.

---

## Chapter Shape and Tone

Single scene with transit framing (the drive there, the drive back). Ellen is in her professional habitat — a records room, a file, a counter, a clerk — and the prose stays close to her working methodology. She proceeds through the deed search the way she would proceed through a deposition: in order, noting page numbers, bracketing what matters. The emotional content is in what she finds, not in how she reacts to finding it.

---

## Goal

Find out who owned the house before her and what happened to them; get the provenance.

---

## Obstacle

The deed chain is readily available — the county has digitized deed records — but the probate records are harder to navigate, and what she finds raises a new question rather than answering the one she came in with.

---

## Action Beats

1. **Drive to the county seat**: Brief transit scene — Ellen on Route 219 or equivalent, the hills greening after spring. She has her notebook on the passenger seat. Professional mode: she is going to pull a file.

2. **At the counter**: She gives the property address; the clerk — mid-forties, a county employee of long tenure — pulls up the deed records on the computer terminal. The deed chain is there: Ellen's recent purchase (transferred from the estate of the prior owner); the prior transfer (from the county to a buyer in the early 1990s, after years of the estate sitting); before that: the deed in Della Markey's name, inherited from Harold Markey, dated approximately 1978.

3. **Harold Markey**: The deed before Della's shows the house purchased by Harold Markey in the late 1940s or 1950s. The clerk confirms: Harold Markey is in the probate index too; she can pull the estate file. She does.

4. **Harold Markey's estate file**: He died in 1979 or 1980; his estate included the house, which passed to his daughter Della Markey, as sole heir listed. There is a note in the probate record about a son — Cale Markey — and some notation about the settlement (Ellen reads only the surface at this point; the note about Cale is there but she doesn't pursue it yet).

5. **Della Markey's estate**: The clerk pulls Della's estate file. Della Markey, died intestate, February 1983. The file notes the cause of death as accidental. Ellen copies: the name, the date, the address. The estate was complicated — the brother is listed as potential heir; there's a notation that he could not be reached or did not respond; the estate sat for years before the county sold the property. (Ellen files the brother's name — Cale — without pursuing it yet; it's part of the estate record.)

6. **Ellen's first reaction (internal, professional)**: She does not think: a ghost. She thinks: a woman died in this house and nobody has the complete record. That is what the files say. The deed and the probate give the shell; the death certificate and the inquest file would give the story. The stenographic instinct: this transcript is incomplete.

7. **The digitization question**: She asks the clerk: how far back do your coroner's records go? The clerk says: we have them; they're upstairs, in the coroner's office; they haven't been digitized. She can request them, but they're paper files. The clerk adds — offhand, not alarmed: "We're working through the older records now. Anything from the early eighties, I'd say come back soon if you want originals — we'll have them scanned by fall probably."

8. **Drive back**: Brief. Ellen with her notebook, the deed copies on the passenger seat. Della Markey. February 1983. The house on Verrill Street. The house is there when she gets back.

---

## Outcome / Turn

Ellen now knows: Della Markey died in the house she bought; the death was recorded as accidental; the records exist but are weeks from being scanned. She has a name. She has a date. The house's back-room cold has a person's name now, even if Ellen does not yet let herself frame it that way. The proceeding has a deponent.

---

## New Question / Pressure Raised

What does the full death record say? (The inquest file is upstairs at the courthouse and is still on paper. Ellen has a deadline — not arbitrary urgency but the real urgency of the digitization schedule. She needs to go back.)

---

## Membrane Crossing

**Vehicle**: The deed and probate records. Specifically: Della Markey's name on the chain of title, and the fact of her death in February 1983, cross from the documentary record into Ellen's knowledge. Ellen can now connect the house to a specific person and a specific death. Della is no longer anonymous; the house is no longer a house with a faulty latch; the proceeding has a name attached.

---

## Belief Movement (Epistemic Ledger)

- **Ellen**: suspects (something happened in this house) → **suspects** (now she has a name and a date; her suspicion is grounded in documentary fact; but she has not yet read the inquest file and has not yet formed a belief about whether anyone came to the door). The epistemic movement here is: suspicion becomes specific. She is not wrong-believing yet; she is not sure of anything yet; she is pursuing the record.
- **@reader**: suspicious → suspects P (knowing now that a woman died in the house in February 1983 during a blizzard, the reader begins to connect this to Della's stopped night; the two timelines are now explicitly linked).

---

## Continuity Notes

- **New facts established**: The house's deed chain documented; Harold Markey owned the house from approximately the late 1940s; Della inherited it approximately 1978 after Harold died (1979–80); Della died intestate in February 1983; the estate listed Cale Markey as potential heir; Cale did not respond or could not be reached; the estate sat for years before the county sold the property; the coroner's records from 1983 are paper files at the county courthouse, weeks from being digitized.
- **Voice anchors**: Third-person limited, past tense. The professional register is at home here — the deed search is exactly the kind of procedure Ellen knows; she moves through it with the efficiency of someone who has navigated administrative records rooms for depositions. No emotional commentary; what she discovers is allowed to sit without being interpreted aloud.
- **Mood**: Procedural, focused. The turn at the end of the chapter is quiet: Ellen drives home with Della Markey's name on a notepad. The house is there when she gets back. She does not need to say what this means. It means what the record says it means.
