# Chapter 3 — The Third Step

**POV**: Ellen Vance (third-person limited, past tense)  
**Summary**: Ellen makes the house habitable and learns its physical character; she goes into Renick and gets a first sense of the town; she decides, without quite deciding, to learn the history of the house.  
**Clock**: Ellen's clock — April–May, present day; the first week or two in the house  
**Chapter shape**: multi-scene  
**Ending mode**: resolved-beat  
**Target words**: ~2,200  
**Note**: ⊘ STALLING CHAPTER — no documentary discovery of the death; Ellen settles in and notices. This chapter accomplishes: the house as a physical presence; Renick as a specific place; and Ellen's professional instinct toward provenance, which will drive the documentary search that follows. Without this stall, the leap to deed records in chapter 5 would feel rushed.

---

## Setting / Time

The house on Verrill Street and the town of Renick, West Virginia. Late April or early May. The town: Meade Street with the former plant building visible from the north end, a hardware store, a resale shop. The Sandy River below the ridge, brown with spring silt.

---

## Chapter Shape and Tone

Multi-scene: domestic work in the house; a trip into town; return. The tone is Ellen's professional register applied to domesticity — she inventories the house the way she inventories a deposition room, with the same expectation that a record will emerge. The chapter is quiet; nothing dramatic happens. Its function is to establish Ellen's habits and to let the house's presence accumulate through specific physical observation rather than atmospheric declaration.

---

## Goal

Make the house functional; get a sense of the town; determine what she can find out about the house's prior occupants.

---

## Obstacle

The house resists easy understanding — not hostile, just particular. The third step keeps catching her slightly off-balance. The back room remains cold even as the weather warms. She cannot fully account for either.

---

## Action Beats

1. **Domestic work**: Ellen measures rooms, makes a proper list of repairs, tests the heat registers. The staircase: she learns the third step. It flexes underfoot in a way that is slightly wrong — not dangerously, but enough to catch her each time, even after she knows it's there. She notes it, as she notes everything.

2. **The back room revisited**: She goes in during daylight this time. She examines it properly: the walls, the ceiling, the closet (empty — a few old nails, a shelf). The cold in the room is not draft; the window is intact and sealed adequately for an old house. She opens and closes it, notes nothing, goes back out. The door, she notices, has a latch that catches cleanly on the plate. By every mechanical test, it should stay closed. She closes it.

3. **The desk with the maker's mark**: She opens the desk's inner drawer to check the mark again: *Renick Furniture Works.* She looks the name up on her phone: the company's Wikipedia page is two paragraphs, listing the founding date, the furniture lines, and the plant closure in the early 1990s. The furniture plant is the town's history. The desk is in her house.

4. **Hardware store visit**: She drives to the hardware store on Meade Street. She buys what she needs. The man at the counter — older, has been here since the plant was running — asks where she's come from. She says: she bought the house on Verrill Street. He says: that house has been empty for a while. He does not say anything more specific. She does not ask.

5. **Walking Renick**: She walks the main strip. The plant building: large, brick, the company name fading from the south face. A resale shop with old furniture in the window — she looks in; at least two pieces carry labels that match the maker's mark on her desk. She goes in, turns over a chair to look at the bottom: *Renick Furniture Works, Est. 1908.* The chair is priced at twelve dollars. She buys it, carries it to the car.

6. **The professional instinct**: Back at the house, she sits at the desk with the chair across from her. She is thinking: she knows who made the furniture; she does not know who lived with it. The deed search is the next logical step — who owned the house, when, and what happened to them. She has done deed searches before, peripherally; you learn the procedure when you work enough property depositions.

7. **Evening**: The house in the early evening. The staircase, the third step; the landing; the back room door closed. The cold edge in the hall that is not the season's cold. She notes it. She moves on. 

---

## Outcome / Turn

Ellen has decided — not dramatically, just procedurally — that she is going to find out who lived in this house. The decision is professional in character: she wants the provenance. The chapter closes with this resolved: not the mystery (she has no mystery yet), but her intention to pursue the record.

---

## New Question / Pressure Raised

Who owned this house before her, and what is the history of the back room's cold? Ellen has begun to ask the first question; she does not yet know it is connected to the second.

---

## Membrane Crossing

**Vehicle**: The physical house — the third step, the back-room cold, the maker's mark on the desk. These are present in both timelines. Della knew the third step; Della knew the back room; the plant made the desk. The crossing here is not informational but atmospheric: the house itself carries its history, and Ellen is beginning to read it as a record. No documentary crossing yet.

## Continuity Notes

- **New facts established**: Ellen shops at the hardware store on Meade Street; the Renick Furniture Works plant building is on the north end of Meade Street, its name fading on the brick; at least some old furniture in local resale carries the maker's mark; Ellen buys a twelve-dollar chair from the resale shop; Ellen has decided to do a deed search.
- **Voice anchors**: Third-person limited, past tense. The professional register applied to domesticity — the maker's mark is noted; the chair is examined the way a deposition exhibit is turned over to read the stamping. No spiritual or emotional language about the house. The cold in the back room is a notation.
- **Mood**: Settling in, quiet, deliberate. The stalling chapter works because Ellen herself is not alarmed — she is organizing. The reader may feel more than she does, but the prose follows her, not their feeling.
