# Chapter 9 — Before the Scanner

**POV**: Ellen Vance (third-person limited, past tense)  
**Summary**: Ellen returns to the county records office with the full afternoon to read the inquest file; she works through the coroner's report and the physician's statement again and reaches the witness statements — and is stopped, mid-reading, by closing time; she will return again.  
**Clock**: Ellen's clock — late May or early June, present day  
**Chapter shape**: single scene  
**Ending mode**: mid-action-cut  
**Target words**: ~2,800  

---

## Setting / Time

The county records reading room, courthouse. A return visit — Ellen has booked more time this visit and arrived early. Late morning through early afternoon. The records clerk — the same one — has the file ready.

---

## Chapter Shape and Tone

Single scene. Ellen working through a long file, methodically. The chapter's rhythm is reading: she is doing what court reporters do, which is to receive a record in order and make sense of it. The chapter extends the procedural texture without adding new revelations — the content of the coroner's report and the physician's statement are retread from chapter 7, now read more carefully — and arrives at the witness statements at the end of the reading session, where it cuts off before the crucial statement (Mabel Duff's) is reached.

---

## Goal

Read the complete inquest file; specifically, get to the witness statements.

---

## Obstacle

The file is denser than expected; the methodology requires reading in order; the day is shorter than the file. She reaches the witness statements at 3:40 PM and the records room closes at 4:00 PM.

---

## Action Beats

1. **Setup**: Ellen at the records room; the file is brought out again. The digitization update: the records clerk says the scanning crew is on schedule; the 1980s files are probably eight to ten weeks out now. "So you've got time," the clerk says, "but not tons of it." Ellen notes this. Eight to ten weeks.

2. **Working methodology**: She has a notepad and a photocopy of chapter 7's photographs. She is not re-reading from scratch; she is working forward from the physician's statement, which she only partially absorbed last time. She reads the pathologist's report more carefully this time:
   - The time of death (estimated from body temperature on discovery): sometime between 10:00 PM and 4:00 AM on the night of February 11–12, 1983
   - The manner of death: hypothermia
   - The body temperature on discovery: approximately 79°F (severe hypothermia)
   - A note about the pattern of cold: she had likely been in mild hypothermia for several hours before cognitive and motor impairment set in.

3. **Ellen's interior note (professional)**: She knows what this means without being told — the mild stage is the one where decisions are still possible. A stenographic note, almost: the record shows she was capable of decision until a certain point; the record does not say what decisions she made.

4. **The inquest proceedings**: She reads the brief transcript of the inquest itself — more fragmentary than a deposition, conducted in an era before everything was recorded. Witness statements summarized rather than verbatim. The coroner's questions. The jury's questions. The verdict.

5. **Finding the witness list**: She reaches the section of the file containing the individual witness statements. There are three. She reads the index:
   - Statement of Lester Caulfield, utility worker, who responded to the call on February 12
   - Statement of Deputy Glenn Harman, who secured the scene
   - Statement of Mabel Duff, neighbor, Verrill Street

6. **Lester Caulfield's statement**: She reads this first. He found the house cold; no power; he called for the deputy after seeing the body through the kitchen window. Routine emergency response. Nothing revelatory.

7. **Deputy Harman's statement**: The scene description again, more detailed. Forced entry through the kitchen window. The kitchen: the table, the lamp (burned out; oil exhausted), the ledger open on the table, the body on the floor. He mentions the ledger in passing — the coroner noted it; it was part of the inventory of the house contents. The ledger was *left in the house with the rest of the property* when the estate was never fully administered.

   Ellen notes this: the ledger. Left in the house. She marks the page.

8. **The clock**: She looks at the clock on the wall: 3:42 PM. One more witness statement.

9. **Beginning Mabel Duff's statement**: She turns to Mabel Duff's statement. She gets three lines in:

   *"I saw no visitors to the Markey house on the evening of the 10th or during the daylight hours of the 11th. I knew Miss Markey to be a private person and I was not in the habit of—"*

   The records clerk appears in the doorway. Closing at four, she can have five more minutes.

10. **Cut off**: Ellen photographs the remaining pages quickly. She will read them next visit. She thanks the clerk.

---

## Outcome / Turn

Ellen has the file's interior geography now. The deputy's mention of the ledger is the chapter's significant discovery: *the ledger was left in the house.* The ledger exists; it was there when the body was found; it has been in the house for forty years. And she has been in that house.

And: she has started Mabel Duff's statement but not finished it. The chapter cuts here, mid-reading.

---

## New Question / Pressure Raised

Two questions now: (1) Where is the ledger? (Ellen has been in the house; she has not found a ledger. This puts her in motion toward the back room.) (2) What does the rest of Mabel Duff's statement say?

---

## Membrane Crossing

**Vehicle**: The deputy's passing mention of the ledger in the scene description. Ellen now knows the ledger was in the house when Della was found; it was inventoried; it was left with the property. The ledger — Record B's container — is now on Ellen's radar, even though she hasn't found it yet. The object that crosses the membrane here is the ledger itself, referenced in the file, located (in principle) in the house she lives in.
