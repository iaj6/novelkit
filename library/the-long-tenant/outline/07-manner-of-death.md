# Chapter 7 — Manner of Death

**POV**: Ellen Vance (third-person limited, past tense)  
**Summary**: Ellen goes to the county records office and reads the coroner's inquest file on Della Markey's death — the death certificate and coroner's investigation report; she is cut off before the witness statements; she returns to the house with the official account.  
**Clock**: Ellen's clock — May, present day  
**Chapter shape**: single scene (document-heavy), with brief transit  
**Ending mode**: resolved-beat  
**Target words**: ~3,200  

---

## Setting / Time

The county records office — a room above or adjacent to the county clerk's office in the courthouse. A reading room: a table, two chairs, institutional fluorescent light, a window that looks out over the courthouse parking lot. May, morning. Ellen has driven in from Renick.

---

## Chapter Shape and Tone

Single scene with transit bookends. The chapter is the reading of a file — not a dramatic revelation but a professional encounter with administrative documents. Ellen works through the coroner's file in order, the way she would work through deposition exhibits. The prose stays close to her process: she notes, she brackets, she photographs. The file gives the mechanism of Della's death; it does not give the story. The chapter's resolved-beat ending is the professional sub-arc of reading the official account — but the file is incomplete in a way Ellen cannot yet name.

---

## Goal

Read the full inquest file for Della Markey; get the official account of the death.

---

## Obstacle

The file gives the mechanism (the storm, the stove, hypothermia) but leaves Ellen with a gap she cannot quite identify. The file is a record of the scene and the physical cause; it does not answer why Della did not leave the house. There is nothing in the coroner's report about why a forty-seven-year-old woman sat in a failing house in a blizzard and died there when the road — according to the report — was not yet impassable at the time of her death.

---

## Action Beats

1. **Arrival**: Ellen at the courthouse; she signs in at the records office and requests the Markey inquest file, 1983. The records staff is efficient; they have the file in a cardboard folder. She is shown to the reading room. The file is thicker than she expected.

2. **The death certificate — first document**: She opens to it. Standard form: *Della Mae Markey, 37, Verrill Street, Renick, Greenbrier County, West Virginia. Date of death: February 12, 1983. Immediate cause of death: hypothermia. Manner of death: ACCIDENT. Attending: [coroner's signature]. Date filed: February 17, 1983.*

   Ellen reads it the way she reads any document: in order, marking what she doesn't yet have context for. *37 years old.* The age is quieter than she expected. She photographs the certificate.

3. **The death certificate as transcript**: Ellen's interior note — the death certificate is the transcript's cover page. It has the ruling and none of the testimony. She wants the testimony.

4. **The coroner's investigation report**: She reads through. The scene: county deputies responded on the morning of February 12, when a neighbor reported not seeing signs of life since the storm began. They found the front door locked; gained entry through a window. Della was found in the kitchen, on the floor, wrapped in a wool blanket, near the cold stove. Temperature in the house: approximately 36°F when deputies arrived. No sign of forced entry. No evidence of trauma. The stove: cold, no mechanical defect; the chimney exterior-wall construction consistent with draft reversal in extreme cold.

5. **Noting the gap**: Ellen pauses at the stove description. *No mechanical defect.* The stove didn't fail. The stove did exactly what it does in those conditions. The difference is noted in the file but not framed — the report does not ask why she didn't leave. The professional instinct kicks in: a transcript records what was said; this file records what was found. The choice — if there was a choice — is not on the page.

6. **The physician's report**: She reads the pathologist's cause-of-death opinion: hypothermia consistent with prolonged exposure to cold in an inadequately heated environment. No alcohol. No drugs. No underlying conditions that would have accelerated vulnerability. She was healthy; she was cold; she died. The report does not say why she stayed.

7. **The inquest verdict**: *Accidental death. The jury finds: Della Mae Markey died on or about February 11–12, 1983, as a result of hypothermia brought on by exposure to cold following the failure of her heating source during the storm of February 9–12, 1983. No evidence of foul play. Verdict: accidental death.* Ellen photographs this page.

8. **Approaching the witness statements**: She turns to the next section of the file: witness statements. She can see there are at least three. She begins to read the first — the neighbor who called in the check, then the deputy's scene report — and then:

9. **Cut off**: The records clerk appears in the doorway. "We're closing in fifteen minutes." Ellen looks at her watch; she has been here for two hours. She marks her place — she has not yet read the statements from neighbors who may have been watching the house. She asks if she can photograph the remaining pages. The clerk confirms: yes, if she does it quickly.

   She photographs the remaining pages without reading them — she'll read them properly next time. She thanks the clerk and packs up.

10. **Drive back**: Brief. Ellen on Route 219, the photocopies of the inquest file on the passenger seat. The stove that didn't fail. The woman who was found on the floor near it. The witness statements she hasn't read yet.

---

## Outcome / Turn

Ellen has the official account. The file is a complete administrative record: the certificate, the investigation report, the pathologist's opinion, the inquest verdict. The proceeding concluded. And it leaves a gap she can feel but cannot yet name — the file records what happened and not why; the testimony of the witnesses is still unread.

---

## New Question / Pressure Raised

What did the witnesses say? (The neighbor statements are in the file but Ellen photographed without reading them; she'll read them on the return visit. The file is complete as a verdict; it may not be complete as a record of what people knew.)

She now has the official account: the stove, the storm, hypothermia, accidental death. The file uses the phrase "no mechanical defect" for the stove — a phrase that tells us what the record says and does not say. Ellen notes the gap without naming it.
