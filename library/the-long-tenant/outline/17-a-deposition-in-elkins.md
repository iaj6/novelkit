# Chapter 17 — A Deposition in Elkins

**POV**: Ellen Vance (third-person limited, past tense)  
**Summary**: Ellen drives west to Elkins for a deposition — her first contract in weeks; the work goes as the work goes; she returns to Renick in the evening and finds the back-room door ajar again; she closes it.  
**Clock**: Ellen's clock — late June, present day  
**Chapter shape**: multi-scene  
**Ending mode**: elliptical  
**Target words**: ~2,400  
**Note**: ⊘ STALLING CHAPTER — no new documentary discovery; no advance on P. This chapter accomplishes: (1) the work world — the trade that is going from under her — is shown directly, in its professional reality, as contrast to the house investigation; (2) the house's continuing behavior (the back-room door) is confirmed as ongoing even when Ellen is away; (3) the question she has written in her notebook (*Did Cale come back?*) is allowed to sit without being immediately answered; (4) a stall in the late-middle section provides the dead air the book needs between chapter 15's investigative energy and the convergence beginning in chapter 19.

---

## Setting / Time

The road west from Renick to Elkins, West Virginia (approximately 80 miles); a law firm's conference room in Elkins; then the road east; then the house at Verrill Street in the evening.

---

## Chapter Shape and Tone

Multi-scene: the drive, the deposition, the drive back, the return. The deposition is shown in real procedural detail — Ellen at the stenotype machine, chording in real time, the attorneys and the deponent, the off-the-record breaks. This is her professional life; it is shown from inside, without sentimentality. The contrast with the house investigation is structural: in the deposition room, the record is made by a machine in real time; in the house on Verrill Street, the record was made by hand in a ledger; both are the same impulse. Ellen does not think this.

---

## Goal

Do the deposition work; get paid; return to Renick.

---

## Obstacle

The deposition is not difficult, but it is the first contract in several weeks, and it is one contract — the work is thin. During a stretch of testimony, the back room intrudes into Ellen's attention; she pushes it aside; the proceeding continues.

---

## Action Beats

1. **The drive west**: Ellen on Route 33, the hills and the valleys and the stretch of highway that runs along a river bottom before climbing. The stenotype machine in its case in the back seat. She has her notebook but she has left it on the desk; she didn't bring the Markey notes. She is going to do a deposition, not investigate a death.

2. **Arriving in Elkins**: A small city; a law firm on the main street, third floor. She is early; she sets up the machine, tests the CAT connection, checks her brief dictionary (the steno briefs she has built over twenty years, the coded syllables that let her chord a full word in a single stroke). This is the familiar ritual.

3. **The deponent arrives**: A worker's compensation case — a man in his forties with a back injury, suing his former employer. His attorney. The defendant's attorney. Ellen at the machine.

4. **The deposition, in procedural detail**: She chords. The testimony moves at pace — the attorneys are experienced; they stay out of each other's way; the deponent is careful and not particularly articulate but clear. Ellen tracks every word: the objections to form (she marks them), the off-the-record break at 11:40 AM, the return on the record, the read-back when the deponent's attorney asks her to re-read a question. She re-reads: the exact words, in order, at a pace. This is what she does.

5. **The intrusion**: During a sustained stretch of testimony — the deponent's description of a worksite — something from the house intrudes into Ellen's processing. Not a thought exactly; more like the way a room sounds different when something in it has changed. The back room. She pushes it aside. She chords. The proceeding continues.

6. **Professional loss, visible**: At the lunch break, one of the attorneys says something — offhand, not cruel — about how he usually just uses digital audio now; his firm has started using it for straightforward cases. "We keep a stenographer for anything complex or contested, but..." He trails off. He is not being unkind; he is just talking.

   Ellen says: yes, that's the direction things are going. She doesn't elaborate. The break is fifteen minutes.

7. **End of the deposition**: The deposition runs until 2:45 PM. The attorneys shake hands. Ellen packs the machine. The rough draft is already done; the CAT produced it in real time on the monitor. She'll clean and certify tonight.

8. **Drive back east**: The road home. She passes through the county at sunset. Route 219, the ridges softening in the long light. She is thinking about nothing in particular — the deposition, the CAT draft she needs to clean. She comes over the ridge and the Sandy River is below and the town is there.

9. **At the house**: She parks; she goes in. She goes upstairs. She is carrying the machine.

   The back room door is ajar.

   She has been in Elkins all day. She left the house at seven AM with every door closed. The back-room door is ajar.

   She sets down the stenotype case. She closes the door. She does not go in.

10. **Closing**: She goes back downstairs. She makes coffee. She opens the notebook and the question is there on the page: *Did Cale come back?* She closes the notebook. The house is quiet.
