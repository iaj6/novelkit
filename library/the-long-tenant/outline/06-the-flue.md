# Chapter 6 — The Flue

**POV**: Della Markey (first-person, present tense)  
**Summary**: Della makes a sustained attempt to relight the stove; the attempt fails; she uses her last matches; she sits on the floor near the dead stove and acknowledges the cold.  
**Clock**: Della's clock — stopped night of February 11, 1983; approximately 9:00–9:20 PM  
**Chapter shape**: bottle  
**Ending mode**: literary-fade  
**Target words**: ~2,000  

---

## Setting / Time

The kitchen of the Verrill Street house. The temperature has continued dropping — the thermometer now reads forty-two or forty-one degrees. The oil lamp is burning. Outside: the blizzard, the wind on the exterior wall. The stove is cold.

---

## Chapter Shape and Tone

Bottle: the kitchen, Della, the stove. The chapter is a sustained physical sequence — Della's last serious attempt to restore the fire — rendered with the precision of someone who knows exactly what she is doing and why it isn't working. No panic; no drama. Just the physical facts of a wood stove in a cold chimney in a severe storm. The chapter deepens the reader's understanding of the specific mechanism of Della's death without naming it as death. It ends on the cold as fact, without comment.

---

## Goal

Get the stove lit again; restore heat to the house.

---

## Obstacle

The chimney is too cold. The flue gas chills before it reaches the top; draft reverses; any fire she starts is starved and dies.

---

## Action Beats

1. **First attempt**: Della crumples a page of newspaper and sets it in the firebox with a few sticks from the kindling box. She lights a match; the paper catches; it burns briefly and goes out. The cold air from the reversed flue kills the flame. The draft reverses on contact with the chimney's chill.

2. **The diagnosis, restated in her words**: She knows this failure. Her father explained it: a cold chimney needs priming — you need to get warmth into the flue first before the fire can draw. You light a torch of paper and hold it up into the firebox as far as it will go; if the chimney takes, you'll feel the draft shift.

3. **Torch-paper method**: She tears more pages from an old newspaper she has in the pantry; she rolls them into a tight cone, lights it, and holds it as far into the firebox as she can. She holds it. She can feel — or thinks she can feel — a slight shift in the air. She holds the torch until it burns down toward her fingers, then sets it on the ash and quickly adds kindling. The fire takes. She holds her breath.

4. **The brief success**: The fire burns for perhaps three minutes. She can feel, faintly, the warmth from the firebox — not heating the kitchen, but present. She puts the one piece of cord wood on top of the kindling, carefully, so as not to smother it. The fire holds.

5. **The failure**: And then: the cold air from above, feeding back down. The draft reverses again. The firebox fills with smoke rather than heat; the fire smothers under the descending cold. She opens the firebox door to breathe the smoke out. The fire is out.

6. **The count**: She has two matches left. She looks at them. She could try again. She knows already what would happen: the same result. The chimney is too cold; the storm is not letting up; without a sustained high-temperature source to prime the flue, the draft will not restore itself tonight. She puts the matches back in the tin.

7. **On the floor**: She sits down on the kitchen floor near the stove. Not at the table — on the floor, her back against the wall, the blanket pulled around her. The floor is colder than the table. She knows this and sits there anyway. There is something in sitting on the floor that is its own kind of assessment: this is what I have, this is where I am.

8. **The cold named**: The cold is in the house now. Not the storm's cold — the house's own cold, the cold that builds when the heat source dies and there is nothing to replace it. She knows what this is. She has the blanket. She has the lamp. She has the one piece of cord wood that she is not burning because there is nothing for it to accomplish.

---

## Outcome / Turn

The fire is out and it is not coming back tonight. Della has used all but two of her matches. The cord wood is being held back. She is sitting on the floor in the kitchen. The cold is in the house.

---

## New Question / Pressure Raised

How long does she have? (The reader now understands the physical mechanism; the mild hypothermia stage — when decisions are still possible — has a window; the chapter does not state this, but the reader who has been paying attention knows it.)

## Membrane Crossing

**Vehicle**: The stove (the same stove Ellen now owns). The specific failure mode described by Della — the exterior-wall chimney losing draft in extreme cold — is the mechanism that the coroner's report will later attribute as the cause of the failed heating. Ellen will encounter the stove as a cold, intact object whose mechanism Della has just described. The shared object carries information even before Ellen finds the documentary record.
