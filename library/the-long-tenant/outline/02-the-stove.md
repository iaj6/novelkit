# Chapter 2 — The Stove

**POV**: Della Markey (first-person, present tense)  
**Summary**: Della is in the kitchen of the Verrill Street house; the stove has already failed; she takes inventory of what she has and records it; the kept sentence appears for the first time.  
**Clock**: Della's clock — stopped night of February 11, 1983; approximately 8:30–9:00 PM  
**Chapter shape**: bottle  
**Ending mode**: declarative-close  
**Target words**: ~2,000  

---

## Setting / Time

The kitchen of the Verrill Street house, February 11, 1983. The power has been out since before dark. The oil lamp is lit on the kitchen table. Outside, the blizzard — not howling, a sustained pressure on the walls. The stove has gone cold; the house is cooling.

---

## Chapter Shape and Tone

Bottle: the kitchen, Della, the failed stove, the ledger. First-person present tense, short sentences closing on physical objects. This chapter establishes Della's register immediately — the flat diction, the millwright's household vocabulary, the precision of a woman who keeps the record. It is not frightening; it is managing. The reader meets Della at her most competent, doing what she knows, before the chapter gives them what she does not yet know.

---

## Goal

Take stock of the situation: the stove that won't draw, the wood supply, the oil in the lamp, the temperature in the house.

---

## Obstacle

The stove is dead. The chimney has lost draft in the extreme cold; the flue is cold; the fire cannot be re-established tonight. She has one piece of cord wood left and a few hours of oil in the lamp. The storm shows no sign of ending.

---

## Action Beats

1. **Opening at the stove**: Della's hand on the damper handle; the damper's resistance. The stove is cold — not merely banked, cold. She opens the firebox and confirms it: the ash is settled; the fire is out.

2. **The chimney diagnosis**: She does not panic; she knows why. Exterior-wall chimney. Her father explained it: when the outdoor temperature drops far enough in sustained cold, the flue gas chills before it reaches the top; draft reverses; cold air feeds back down into the firebox. This is what has happened. She knows this as a physical fact, not a catastrophe.

3. **Inventory of what she has**: She goes through the house systematically — the way her father went through the mill at the end of a shift, checking what holds. The cord wood basket: one piece left. The kindling box: empty. Matches on the shelf by the stove: twelve remaining. Lamp oil in the tin on the shelf: enough for several hours.

4. **The oil lamp**: She trims the wick — she knows how; her father showed her — and the lamp burns steady. The circle of light it gives is the circle of light she has for the evening. Outside that circle, the kitchen is already darker than it should be. The thermometer on the wall near the window reads fifty-one degrees Fahrenheit.

5. **The ledger**: She sits at the table with the household account book open. She records: the time (8:42 PM by the travel clock on the mantle, visible from the kitchen door), the temperature, the stove status, the oil level, the storm outside. The ledger is kept in columns: date, description, figure if applicable. Her father's method, and now hers.

6. **Record A appears**: At the bottom of the entry she writes — or has written; it is already there, as if it is always already there — **"Nobody came to that door."** She does not explain it. It is what she knows. It is what the entry requires.

7. **Sitting with the fact**: She sits for a moment after writing it. Not grief; not fear. It is simply what the night is. The storm is outside; the stove is cold; nobody came to that door. She has the lamp and the wool blanket she has carried down from upstairs and the one piece of cord wood she is not burning yet.

8. **End of the chapter**: She pulls the blanket around her shoulders; she stays at the kitchen table with the lamp. The circle of light. The house beyond it is already going cold at the edges.

---

## Outcome / Turn

Della is managing. She knows what she has; she has recorded it; she has set the kept sentence on the page. The reader knows the sentence is there and will have to decide what it means. The chapter closes on competence and cold, without distress — which makes it more disturbing than distress would.

---

## New Question / Pressure Raised

What is "Nobody came to that door" doing in a ledger entry about a stove failure? (The reader notices; Della does not elaborate.)

- **New facts introduced**: The power has been out since before dark; the stove has lost draft in the extreme cold; the house has one piece of cord wood remaining; the oil lamp is on the kitchen table; the travel clock is visible from the kitchen door; the thermometer reads 51°F; the ledger is in active use; the kept sentence first appears.
- **Voice anchors**: First-person, present tense from the first word. Short sentences. Flat diction. No metaphor Della wouldn't reach for naturally. The technical vocabulary of the stove and chimney belongs to her (damper, flue, firebox, draft, cord wood, kindling). No lyricism. The power of the kept sentence is in its flatness.
- **Mood**: Competent, managed, matter-of-fact. The cold is a problem being solved, not a horror. This is where Della is most legible — the millwright's daughter, keeping the record.
