# Reader Guidance — The Long Tenant

## Intended Audience

Adult readers of literary fiction — the readership for Sarah Waters, Ali Smith, Hilary Mantel, Marilynne Robinson. Not horror readers expecting supernatural mechanism; not thriller readers expecting plot-driven resolution. Readers who will sit with a stopped clock and a stopped belief and a house that does what it does without explanation, and who will find meaning in the verbatim document rather than the jump. College-educated, comfortable with formal experimentation (the two-tense, two-person, two-register architecture), patient with the pace of the record.

## Brief-Adherence Checks to Perform

The Reader phase must answer these questions explicitly, in addition to general craft review:

1. **Is the register split immediate and maintained?** Does the first sentence of every Della chapter establish first-person present tense? Does every Ellen chapter establish third-person past tense? Does any chapter blur into a third voice — a literary average that belongs to neither POV? (This is the central quality check; any register bleed is a high-severity finding.)

2. **Is the verbatim-record rule honored?** Do all in-chapter quotations of Record A (`kept-sentence`) and Record B (`markey-ledger-1983`) match the canonical text exactly? Flag any divergence, even a single word.

3. **Is the membrane rule honored?** Does any chapter have Ellen seeing Della as a figure, hearing her words, or perceiving her as a person? Does any chapter have Della hearing Ellen as speech or reading Ellen's thoughts? Any direct contact across the membrane is a structural failure.

4. **Is the supernatural never explained?** Does any chapter — in narration, in dialogue, in any character's interiority — propose a mechanism for how the haunting works? Does any character claim to understand what Della is? Any explanation of the mechanism is a finding.

5. **Is the false crossing fair and the payoff honest?** When Ellen wrong-believes ¬P after reading Mabel Duff's statement, is the error genuinely available to a careful reader — is the statement plausibly read as confirming nobody came? And when the ledger arrives, does it actually disprove the inference (not just the surface belief)? Both sides must be fair-play.

6. **Are the stalling chapters defended, not thinned?** The deliberately stalling chapters are structural requirements, not pacing failures. If a chapter makes no epistemic progress but successfully renders the texture of a haunting's dead air, it is doing its job. Flag stalling chapters only if they also fail on texture — if they are both epistemically and atmospherically inert.

7. **Is the ending-mode variety enforced?** No two consecutive chapters should close in the same mode. If a run of three or more consecutive chapters close the same way, flag it.

8. **Is Della's flatness preserved?** Does any Della chapter contain a sentence that could belong to a literary narrator — a beautiful metaphor, a reflective distance, a lyricism — that a millwright's daughter keeping a ledger in 1983 would not produce? Flatness is the register; departures from it are findings.

9. **Is the death resolved fairly?** By the close, can the reader assemble the complete account of Della's last night from what is on the page? If any piece of the spine (the knocking, the hour, the ledger entry, Della's choice) is not rendered explicitly somewhere in the text, flag it.

10. **Is the unresolved ending preserved?** Della's final chapter must close on a concrete physical action mid-gesture — on the object, not on a feeling or a fate. If any chapter after this explains what happened to Della, flags it. The question of her fate is deliberately left in shadow.

## What "Good" Looks Like for This Book

Good Della chapters read like the dead narrator sections of Ali Smith's *How to Be Both*: present tense, first person, the world perceived partially and from inside a specific frozen moment, the prose unable to step outside its own time. Flat. Forensic. Each chapter adds one more concrete grip on the night — a new object named, a new physical fact admitted — without elaborating what was already known.

Good Ellen chapters read like the opening sections of Sarah Waters' *The Little Stranger*: a professional observer, restrained, in a house that is doing something he cannot account for and will not name, with a prose rhythm that builds dread out of procedure and anomaly. The stenographic vocabulary should be consistent and specific; Ellen's trade should be present in how she perceives and records, not just in what she does for a living.

Good use of the two canonical documents: Record A accumulates weight across Della's chapters by being returned to without elaboration — the reader should feel it settle deeper with each recurrence. Record B's arrival in Ellen's chapters should feel like the click of a last piece into place: not a shock, but the exact word in the exact context making a new meaning that was always there to be made.

## What to Flag Explicitly if the Manuscript Drifts

**If either POV drifts toward a literary average** — longer, more reflective sentences; abstract interiority; beautiful prose that doesn't belong to a court reporter's professional restraint or a millwright's daughter's flat vocabulary — name it explicitly. Do not let craft excellence in the wrong register pass as success. "This is well-written but it sounds like a literary narrator, not Ellen Vance / Della Markey" is the correct finding.

**If Della's chapters become lyrical or ruminative** — if she begins to reflect on her life broadly, if she makes metaphors that belong to a literary consciousness, if she narrates her death with elegiac beauty — flag it immediately. Della's power comes from her flatness. Elegy is the wrong mode for this voice.

**If Ellen's chapters explain the house** — if the prose, through Ellen's interiority or the narrative stance, offers a causal or metaphysical interpretation of the haunting — flag it. The house is not explained. The reader is trusted.

**If the convergence feels mechanical** — if every chapter advances the mystery at the same pace, without the stalling chapters' dead air, without the false crossing's apparent resolution — flag the missing texture. A haunting that advances on a metronome is not a haunting.

**If the unresolved ending is resolved** — if any chapter after Della's final chapter says, in any register, what happened to her — flag it as a constraint violation.

## What to Applaud

**The register split held under pressure**: If the two voices remain genuinely distinct across all 24 chapters — especially in the middle chapters where the alternation has been going long enough to tempt drift — name this explicitly. The register split is the book's primary technical achievement; if it works, it must be defended.

**The stalling chapters earning their place**: If a deliberately inert chapter (neither POV advancing the truth) successfully renders the texture of suspended time — a haunting's dead air, a woman sitting with what she cannot yet know — applaud it. Defend it against any editorial pressure to add plot movement.

**The documents doing the work**: If the two canonical records (the kept sentence, the ledger entry) carry the book's meaning without the prose ever explaining that meaning — if the A/B side-by-side lands without a paragraph telling the reader what they have just understood — name this as the book's central achievement and defend it against any pressure to add interpretive scaffolding.

**Della's flatness**: If Della's voice maintains its unliterary plainness across all eleven chapters — if it never reaches for beauty, never steps outside 1983, never explains itself — applaud this specifically. Flat is hard. Consistently flat, chapter after chapter, in a book that is also trying to be emotionally resonant, is the register achievement the book requires.
