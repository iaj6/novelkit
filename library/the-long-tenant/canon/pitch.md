# Pitch — The Long Tenant

Forty years ago, Della Markey froze to death in a narrow house on Verrill Street in Renick, West Virginia, during the February 1983 blizzard. The county recorded an accident: a storm, the power out, a stove that failed, a woman who lived alone. Della has never recorded it as an accident. She is still in the house, and she is certain of one thing, and she is wrong about it.

Ellen Vance, forty-four, a freelance court reporter whose trade is being dissolved a contract at a time by digital audio and voice-to-text, buys the house because it is the cheapest sound house in Renick and Renick is cheap. She arrives precise, underemployed, and angry in a way she will not name. She does not arrive looking for a ghost. She arrives with twenty years of training in the discipline of the exact word — the guarantee that *these were the words*, not the gist, not the comfortable paraphrase — and she begins, without meaning to, to notice the house: a door found open that she left closed, a cold with an edge to it, the sense of a second attention in the rooms.

Behind the one door that will not stay as she leaves it, she finds what no digital system ever kept: a household ledger in a stranger's hand, forty years old, still legible.

The kept sentence and the ledger entry are the same five words. They mean opposite things. The woman whose whole trade was the guarantee of the exact word is the first person in the house with the habits and the reason to read one against the other.

This is a contemporary literary ghost story told from both sides of the membrane between the living and the dead — one present-day POV moving forward through spring and summer, one anchored forty years back inside a single stopped winter night. The engine is asymmetric knowledge about a single death, and the moment each wrong belief corrects.
