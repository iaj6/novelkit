# World — The Long Tenant

## Renick, West Virginia

A fictional town on the Sandy River, in the limestone valley country of central West Virginia — modeled on the historical Renick community in Greenbrier County. A furniture manufacturing town: a plant on the riverbank that made hardwood furniture (desks, chairs, case goods) from the timber country around it, employing much of the county through the mid-twentieth century. The plant closed in stages through the 1980s and 1990s. What remained is cheaper than most places and quieter than most places want to be.

The main plant building is still there, converted to storage, its name fading off the brick. Maker's marks still appear on furniture across the county — on pieces built by people who are now old or dead, a company name burned into wood that outlasted the company. The county courthouse is not grand. The hills are present but they are hills with history that does not care about visitors.

**The Sandy River** is not romantic. It backs up in spring and smells like silt in summer. It flooded once, in living memory, and took out part of the east end of the commercial block. The marks are still on the old brick on Meade Street.

The people still in Renick in the present day are there by choice or by immobility. Housing is cheap: a sound house on Verrill Street is within reach of someone whose income has contracted. The county is mid-digitization of its 1970s–1990s paper records. Some files are still physical — weeks from the scanner — held in the records room of the county courthouse.

## The House at Verrill Street

A narrow, two-and-a-half-story worker's house built in the late nineteenth century — the architecture of a mill town, close to the street, tall-ceilinged rooms, wood floors that flex underfoot. Tall and narrow: the characteristic shape. Della grew up in it; her father owned it; she inherited it; she died in it in February 1983. Ellen Vance buys it approximately forty-two years later.

The back room on the upper floor is the room Della has kept shut. It has the ledger. It has the one door that will not stay as Ellen leaves it.

**The house's presence is not explained.** A door found open that was left closed. A cold in certain rooms that is not the season's cold. The sense of a second attention in the space. The prose renders these as fact and does not adjudicate them. No lore is offered. No medium is consulted.

**Sensory anchors — present day (Ellen's chapters):**
- The smell of old wood and something that might be cold even in summer warmth
- Floor registers that wheeze when a window opens somewhere else in the house
- The desk in the front room — heavy hardwood, a maker's mark burned into the inner drawer edge: *Renick Furniture Works* — that came with the house and belongs to no one now
- The staircase's third step, which is never quite where Ellen expects it
- The back room's specific quality of cold and stillness: a room held in reserve

**Sensory anchors — February 11, 1983 (Della's chapters):**
- The stove damper's resistance when the draft reverses and cold begins to feed back down the flue
- The oil lamp on the kitchen table, the small circle of light it gives that does not warm
- Wind on an uninsulated exterior wall — not howling but constant, a sustained pressure
- The specific weight of a wool blanket in deep cold, which is not enough
- The travel clock on the mantle: wound, accurate, ticking through everything that stops
- The sound the latch makes; the sound the silence makes after knocking stops

## Two Clocks

The book runs two clocks simultaneously and they do not meet.

**Ellen's clock** — present day, approximately spring through summer. She moves forward in real time: she buys the house, occupies it, begins to notice what the house does, begins to research its history, assembles a record of Della's death. Her chapters breathe and build.

**Della's clock** — February 11, 1983, which does not advance. Her present tense is the hours between approximately nine-thirty in the evening and somewhere past midnight — the hours the stove failed and the house went cold. She re-enters the same stopped hour from new angles with each chapter. The clock does not move; her knowledge of that night accretes. She can only be here, in this night, in this cold. She does not reflect on the past from a distance because there is no distance.

## Historical Anchor: The February 1983 Blizzard

Della dies during the Megalopolitan Blizzard, February 9–12, 1983 — a well-documented storm that deposited 35 inches of snow at Glengary, West Virginia, left 34,000+ homes in Charleston and Pineville without power, and reached deep into the Appalachian interior. This is the documented historical anchor for Della's last night: the storm, the power out before dark, the roads impassable, the stove losing draft in the extreme cold, the knocking that came and stopped.

The house has an exterior-wall chimney. When the outdoor temperature drops far enough in sustained extreme cold, the flue gas loses its temperature before reaching the top; draft reverses; cold air feeds back down into the firebox. Re-establishing draft in a fully cold chimney requires sustained effort. Once the stove is cold, in those conditions, it may not come back.

## Renick in 1983

The furniture plant was still operating but contracting: the early-1980s recession drove unemployment nationally to 10.8% (November 1982); manufacturing lost 1.7 million jobs between 1979 and 1982. For a woman who worked in the plant's office on what was still there, the thinning was already present. Wood heat was standard for rural West Virginia — 8.2% of American homes burned wood by 1981; for rural Appalachian households it was often the only source. When Della's stove fails, nothing replaces it.

## Renick Today

Post-industrial in the specific sense: the thing the town made is still visible in the objects around it, but the making is over. Housing prices reflect the depopulation. The county is digitizing its old paper files; the coroner's records from the early 1980s are in the queue. The urgency that a paper record can still be physically touched and read — before it becomes a database entry or is destroyed — is the real, documented urgency of what Ellen is racing.
