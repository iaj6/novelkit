# Style Guide — The Long Tenant

## The Register Split

The book's authority depends on the two POVs never sounding like the same writer in different moods. The difference must be felt in the first sentence of any chapter, without a header. **The test**: cover the chapter POV tag and read the opening line; the register tells you immediately whose chapter it is.

---

## Register 1 — Ellen's chapters (Third-person limited, past tense)

The prose of a professional listener whose trade is the exact word.

**Person and tense**: Third-person limited, past tense. Always. The prose does not slip into present tense for immediacy and does not go omniscient for effect.

**Vocabulary and lexical field**: The record, *on the record*, *off the record*, *read it back*, *mark it*, *Exhibit N*, *certified transcript*, *stricken from the record*, *the deponent*, *rough draft*, *objection*, *for the record*, *the stenotype*, *chording*, *read-back*, *scopist*. This vocabulary stays Ellen's and does not appear in Della's chapters or in neutral narration.

**Rhythm**: Medium length sentences, deliberate — closer to 15–22 words average than to either extreme. The sentence has thought behind it; it builds in careful clauses, the way a transcript formats testimony. Not long; not ornate; not spare. The prose mirrors her professional discipline: it gathers, it orders, it does not editorialize.

**Narrative distance**: Close but not intimate. Ellen observes her own interiority with the same disciplined distance she applies to testimony — she notes her reactions, she does not elaborate them. She does not explain the house; she records what she cannot account for and moves on.

**The uncanny**: The uncanny registers as a professional anomaly — the equivalent of an inaudible passage in the transcript. It gets noted, marked parenthetically, and the proceeding continues. The prose never converts Ellen into a believer. It never decides for the reader what the house is.

**Exemplar anchor**: Sarah Waters, *The Little Stranger* — the skeptical, procedural observer who never quite concedes the supernatural; dread built from a house and a record; the model for Ellen's chapters and for holding the uncanny without naming it. Also Marilynne Robinson, *Housekeeping* — domestic interiority, a house as a pressure on a life, patience without austerity; anchor for Ellen's chapters' willingness to breathe.

---

## Register 2 — Della's chapters (First-person, present tense)

The voice of a stopped clock. Her now is winter 1983 and does not move.

**Person and tense**: First-person, present tense. Always. The tense is not a stylistic choice — it is her imprisonment. She can only be here, in this night, in this cold. There is no reflective distance from the past because for her there is no past that is not this.

**Vocabulary and lexical field**: *The stove, the damper, the flue, the draft, the latch, the door, the ledger, the cold, cord wood, kindling, the column of figures, the clock, the shift, the plant, the paycheck, the deed, the account book*. The vocabulary comes from the millwright's household and the mill town and the physical world of a house in winter. No word that belongs to a literary consciousness. No metaphor she wouldn't reach for naturally.

**Rhythm**: Shorter sentences that close on physical objects — 8–14 words average. Della's chapters advance not by elaborating what she already knows but by accreting one more concrete datum of that night: a new object re-gripped, a new sense-datum admitted. The circling is forensic, not lyrical. It is the mirror of Ellen pulling records.

**What her chapters do not do**: They do not lyricize her condition, romanticize her death, or name the emotion the night contains. The power of the kept sentence is that it is banal; her chapters should be banal in the same register — the same flatness a ledger entry has. The feeling lives in the named objects, not in the narrator standing back to gloss them.

**Exemplar anchors**: Ali Smith, *How to Be Both* — a dead first-person-present narrator perceiving the present partially and out of their own time; the closest structural model for Della's chapters and for the two-clock architecture of the whole book. Hilary Mantel, *Beyond Black* — the dead as mundane, unromantic fact; no mysticism, no rules of the afterlife; anchor for Della's flat diction and for the book's restraint about mechanism.

---

## The Membrane

The two voices must never converge. If a sentence from Della's chapter could, with minor adjustment, appear in Ellen's, one of the voices has drifted. The separator is not just tone but vocabulary, person, tense, and the kind of observation each character is capable of.

Ellen is limited by what the record contains; she observes her own experience at one remove. Della is limited by what the night contained; she is inside it, always present tense, always this hour.

---

## The Two Canonical Documents

**Record A — The kept sentence**: `"Nobody came to that door."` — Della's recurring phrase, met early in her chapters, returning across her circling. It is the mercy she made. It is flat, declarative, without qualification. It does not gain weight through repetition by being explained; it gains weight through context.

**Record B — The ledger entry**: The text of Della's final ledger entry, recovered from the back room late in Ellen's chapters. When set beside Record A, it inverts A's meaning without changing a word of A. Both records must be read via `read_record` and reproduced verbatim when re-quoted in any chapter; the surrounding prose frame is the drafter's to vary, but the document text is fixed.

## Failure Modes

The AI-author patterns most tempting in this story's register. The drafter should actively avoid these; the compression pass should cut them.

- **Post-scene subtext naming** — the prose states what a scene was "really about" after the scene ends. Especially dangerous in Ellen's chapters, where the procedural register creates pressure to then interpret the document she just found. The document must stand without the prose explaining its significance to the reader.
- **Thesis restatement** — an abstract sentence restates the book's themes (the verbatim record, the mercy of wrong belief) at the end of a scene or chapter. The thematic content lives in the concrete objects, never in the prose stepping back to name it.
- **Emotional structural analysis** — a character's feeling is dramatized and then its architecture is explained. Both POVs resist this: Ellen because she is professionally non-interpretive; Della because her emotional vocabulary is limited and flat.
- **Register bleed** — Della's prose acquires literary self-consciousness or metaphor that belongs to Ellen's register, or vice versa; the two voices average toward each other toward a third literary average that belongs to neither. This is the book's central failure mode — it dissolves the structural premise.
- **Redundant transition / re-summary** — throat-clearing that recaps what just happened before moving on; particularly dangerous in Della's circling chapters, where each return must re-enter the night fresh rather than reviewing the previous entry's ground.
- **Sentence-after-the-sentence** — an image lands cleanly, and then the following sentence glosses it. Tempting in both registers; in Della's flat-diction chapters it is especially destructive because it introduces a reflective distance her voice cannot sustain.

---

## Lean-Into Patterns

Patterns the drafter and editors should NOT treat as failures, because this register depends on them.

- **The flat declarative close** — Della's chapters close on a physical object named without comment. This is the register working, not a flat ending. Do not soften or elaborate.
- **The profession leaking into observation** — Ellen's stenographic vocabulary appearing in how she perceives the house (as a record, as testimony, as something that cannot be appealed) is a feature, not a stylistic tic. Let it be the consistent color of her chapters.
- **Deliberate stalling** — a chapter in which neither POV advances significantly toward the truth is not a structural failure; it is a haunting's dead air. Two or three such chapters are planned; the reader pass should defend them rather than flag them as low-tension.
- **The "X was X" fold** — sparingly, in Della's chapters, where the dead present tense creates a fixity the prose can occasionally name without explaining. "The cold was the cold. The clock was the clock." Used rarely; it is the register's own cadence in this voice.
