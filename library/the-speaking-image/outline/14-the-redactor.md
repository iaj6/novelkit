**Beat 2 — Ordinary Time script: "The Redactor" (B2; Document 52)**
The main document. Full B2 register. Structure:

*Hook (approximately 80–100 words):* Opens with a mystery that isn't — a story from the field. In 1948 scholars found the Dead Sea Scrolls; what they also found, eventually, was the problem of the people who copied them. Who were they? What did they change? "We spend careers trying to answer that question. Not because the copies are wrong — they're often excellent. But because the copy is also a choice, and a choice is a kind of author."

*Frame (approximately 60–80 words):* This episode is about redaction criticism — the method for finding the editor hidden inside the text. Sefton is setting it up: "An editor who adds nothing but arrangement is still an author. In this field we call that a redactor. And we spend careers trying to catch one at it."

**[PINNED LINE — must appear verbatim:]**
> "An editor who adds nothing but arrangement is still an author. In this field we call that a redactor, and we spend careers trying to catch one at it."

*Body (approximately 400–500 words):* The method explained for a general audience:
- Redaction criticism asks: what choices did the compiler make? What was included, what was left out, what was placed first? These choices reveal purpose even when the compiler claims to be a neutral editor.
- The classic case: the Gospels. The four evangelists are not transcribers — they are editors of a shared source tradition, and their editorial choices reveal their theological purposes. Q (the hypothetical source both Matthew and Luke drew on) would tell us very little on its own; it is the *arrangement* that speaks.
- Sefton gives an accessible example: two people given the same pile of photographs from a party will produce very different photo albums. Both are "accurate." Neither is neutral. The selection and order are authorial acts.
- The key question for the field: "Once you know what to look for, you start seeing redactors everywhere. The question is always: who arranged this, and what did they want the arrangement to say?"
- She applies this to digital contexts briefly — "This comes up in algorithms, in recommender systems, in the way a search engine decides what to surface first" — naturalizing the concept for her audience without making it alarming.

*Sponsor read (approximately 80–100 words):* Played completely straight. A different sponsor from §7 (to vary the chapter texture). Perhaps a subscription academic database or a writing tool. Professional, brisk.

*Closing (approximately 80–100 words):* Returns to the opening mystery. The Dead Sea Scrolls copyists: we have their work, we have traces of their choices, and through those choices we know something about who they were. "The copy outlasted the copyist. Which is usually how it goes." Points to the next episode, or invites community questions.
