# Brief

## Premise

Tess is fifteen and three months into her apprenticeship to Mara Weft, the hedge-witch who keeps the well charms and the cattle wards working in the village of Drey. Mara is broke. The Weavers' Guild in Asterhall — three weeks of road away — has offered an unusual delivery contract: a sealed leather bag, paid in advance, no questions asked, hand it to the named recipient. The fee covers Mara's debts. Mara is fifty-eight and the road would kill her. So Tess goes.

She doesn't go alone. The contract pays for four: someone to carry, someone to fight, someone who knows the city at the far end, and the courier of record. Mara picks who Tess goes with from the three other people in Drey who can afford to leave for six weeks:

- **Brann**, seventeen, the blacksmith's son. Quiet, oversized, took the job because his older sister's debt requires it.
- **Iri**, sixteen, half-elven, three years a stranger in Drey. Can do real magic — the kind Tess has only read about in her master's books. Tess is intimidated by her.
- **Pip**, fourteen, who turned up in Drey two summers ago and never explained why. Street kid from Asterhall. The only one who has actually been to the city they're going to.

Three weeks on the road. Six encounters Tess didn't plan for. A job that turns out to be bigger than the contract said. Three companions she doesn't quite trust at the start and doesn't quite want to leave at the end. The bag, when it arrives in Asterhall, opens onto a problem none of them can afford to carry — and Tess has to decide what to do with what she now knows.

## Length and shape

- Target length: ~42,000 words
- Number of chapters: 12

Structure is **episodic road-quest**. Each chapter is roughly one encounter or one milestone of the journey: the departure, the road, an ambush, a stop at a town, a stretch of strange country, a confrontation with rivals, a near-disaster, arrival in Asterhall, the delivery, the discovery, the decision. The final two chapters resolve the journey arc and set up the next book.

## Voice

Third-person limited past. **Single POV: Tess** for every chapter. Other party members are vivid through what they say and do; their interiority is Tess's inference. We never enter Brann, Iri, or Pip's heads directly.

Voice is fast, dialogue-forward, contemporary-coded. Sentences move. Banter is constant — it's how the four characterize each other and how the reader hears who they are. Action sequences are physical and concrete. Romance is subplot, not main plot.

## Audience and register

- **Target audience age range**: 13–15 / YA
- **Voice pace**: fast
- **Reading-grade target**: 7th–8th grade
- **POV count intent**: one anchor protagonist (Tess); the other three are seen entirely through her
- **Chapter-ending convention**: cliffhangers and mid-action endings dominate; literary fades are forbidden

## Constraints

- **Single POV throughout.** Tess and only Tess. Other characters are external. No omniscient narrator moments. No four-POV ensemble drift.
- **All four party members are teenagers.** Tess 15, Brann 17, Iri 16, Pip 14. Their ages matter to how they act, how they treat each other, and how the world treats them.
- **Adult NPCs exist and matter** (Mara the master, contacts on the road, the recipient in Asterhall) but they appear briefly and through Tess's lens.
- **Episodic chapter structure.** Each of the 12 chapters is one named encounter or milestone. Chapter titles should announce the encounter ("Departure", "The Wagon Road", "The Innkeeper at Long Crossing", "The Ambush", "The Pavers' Country", "Asterhall", etc.). The chapter feels like one beat, not multiple.
- **Every chapter ends on a cliffhanger or mid-action.** No exceptions. No literary fades. Chapter endings should feel like interruptions — the reader turns the page involuntarily.
- **Real action set pieces in chapters 3, 6, and 9.** Specifically: a roadside ambush by deserters or bandits; a confrontation with a rival party who also wants the bag; a near-disaster crossing or accident. These chapters must deliver physical action, not interiority around action.
- **Brewing romance subplot — Tess and Brann.** Slow burn. Their relationship arc across the book: stranger → reluctant ally → noticing each other → one kiss (or one explicit avoided-kiss moment) in chapter 10 or 11. Not main plot. No on-page sex. YA-appropriate scale.
- **A second emotional thread — Tess and Iri.** Not romantic. Tess's intimidation of Iri's real magic resolves into something like friendship or alliance by the end. The thread is about magical legitimacy and what it means to know more than you're allowed to use.
- **Pip is the comic engine but is competent.** His jokes are character, not deflection. He knows the city. He sometimes saves the party in ways the others can't.
- **The bag's contents matter and are specific.** Not a generic MacGuffin. When Tess discovers what it is (chapter 11 or 12), the discovery should re-cast some earlier encounters in a new light. The reader should feel "oh — *that* is why X happened."
- **Open ending.** Chapter 12 ends with Tess making a decision the consequences of which we don't see. The next-book hook. The reader closes the book wondering what she'll do, not knowing.
- **No DM-coded prose.** No die-rolls, no mechanics references, no fourth-wall breaks about fantasy genre tropes. This is a novel.
- **Magic has costs.** When Tess or Iri uses magic, it costs them something concrete — fatigue, time, materials. Not a free resource. The cost should be visible in the action.
- **The world is a working medieval fantasy.** Wagons, taverns, guild contracts, road-toll booths, market towns, a city of seven thousand. Standard but rendered specifically. The world is the setting, not the subject.

## Genre touchpoints (for register)

The register the book wants to live in: classic-but-modern YA party fantasy. Closest comps for voice and pacing: Leigh Bardugo's *Six of Crows* for ensemble voice and tight chapter structure; Naomi Novik's Scholomance for the bold-assertion-then-escalation cadence and the dark-and-funny-at-the-same-time balance; Tomi Adeyemi's *Children of Blood and Bone* for the immediate dropping-into-action openings. Not Tolkien-style high fantasy. Not adult literary fantasy. Not grimdark. Definitely not romance-forward (the romance is real but stays in its subplot lane).
