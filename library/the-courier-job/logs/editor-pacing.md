# Editor — Pacing Pass
## Macro arc assessment

**Overall shape.** The twelve-chapter arc is well-designed on paper: a two-chapter setup, a four-chapter rising block with an action chapter every third chapter (3, 6, 9), a two-chapter turn, a two-chapter climax, and a single-chapter coda. The cliffhanger sequencing (Revelation / Recognition / Mid-action, never repeating consecutively) is disciplined. All five threads are active in nearly every chapter. The retroactive clue system — seeding "testimony" and the enforcement badge so ch 11 re-casts everything — is the book's structural backbone and it is correctly built.

**Where the arc sails.** The enforcement-network thread (Thread 5) is the book's most reliable engine: it escalates in near-every chapter, plants cleanly, pays off devastatingly in ch 11. The Mara thread (Thread 3) is the book's emotional center of gravity and its staging is nearly perfect — the gap between "what Mara said" and "what Mara knew" widens at exactly the right intervals. The action chapters (3, 6, 9) are appropriately heavier (~4,000 words) against the travel chapters (~3,000–3,200), and the party competence table distributes credit correctly across all four characters.

**Midbook sag: Chapters 7–8.** This is the book's principal structural risk. After the mid-action cut ending ch 6, Thread 5 (the enforcement network — the book's external pressure engine) does not advance again until ch 9. That is two consecutive chapters — approximately 6,500 words — in which the antagonist force is invisible. Chapters 7 and 8 do legitimate work: the Tess/Iri thread turns in ch 7 (Iri's fingertip correction, the geometry speech), and the Pip/junction beat in ch 8 is Thread 4's most important pre-Asterhall moment. But both chapters together advance only internal and character threads. External pressure falls to its lowest point in the book right here, in the exact centre of the middle third. Ch 7 is a single-scene chapter with no antagonist presence and ch 8 is the book's shortest chapter (3,000 words). The emotional temperature drops across both and that drop is unearned — there is no deliberate lull signalled in the outline. This is the area most likely to produce a "why isn't anything happening" reader response and deserves the most attention in revision.

**Ch 11 risks compression at peak payload.** The Advocate's Court chapter at ~3,800 words has to carry: Pip's hidden letter clearing the gate, the walk to Sable Orr's office, the bag opening, seven depositions, Mara's name on the sixth scroll, the full re-casting of every attack across ten chapters, AND the deadline ultimatum. Threads 2, 3, 4, and 5 all resolve or near-resolve here. That is the heaviest informational and emotional load in the book — and it is not the longest chapter. The Ambush (ch 3) and the ford crossing (ch 9) are each longer (~4,000 words) yet carry smaller payloads. If ch 11 is written at pace, the revelations may feel fast rather than earned. This chapter wants to breathe.

**Repeated beat: "Iri pays the magic cost."** This exact beat lands in each of the three action chapters — ch 3 (pallor, shaking, Pip's shoulder), ch 6 (depleted, goes down, accepts help), ch 9 (burns herself dry, powers through). The escalation is intentional and serves Thread 2, but by the third instance the reader will know the shape before it arrives. Ch 9's version must be distinctly more costly or differently witnessed than the first two, or it reads as formula rather than escalation.

**Ch 4 and Ch 8 as back-to-back buffer chapters.** Each is the aftermath chapter following an action chapter (ch 3 → ch 4; ch 6 → ch 7 → ch 8). Ch 4 is the third-shortest chapter (3,000 words) doing decompression work. Ch 8 is the shortest chapter (3,000 words) doing travel work. Individually each is defensible; structurally they represent the two weakest thread-load chapters in the book and they are positioned at the same relative point in each act (just after the action peak). This creates a rhythm — action, decompress, breathe, compress — that risks becoming perceptible and deadening.

**Coda balance is correct.** Ch 12's 3,600 words is the right weight for an open-ended emotional landing. The unsent letter is the right image. The book does not overstay.

**Priority flags for per-chapter review:** Ch 7 and Ch 8 (midbook sag zone); Ch 11 (compression at peak payload); Ch 9's Iri beat (third instance of the magic-cost pattern).


## 08-two-days-out.md

**Small fix applied:** Cut the Pip/Iri ginger-rationing dialogue exchange (~10 lines, from `"You've been holding onto ginger," Iri said.` through `She ate the ginger. Pip ate his piece and looked east.`). The exchange advanced zero threads and re-asserted Pip's performed comic voice immediately before the chapter's climactic beat — his dropped mask at the fire. The single sentence describing him distributing ginger is kept as a camp-detail; the comedy exchange was the redundant element. The section break now leads directly from the camp meal into the dusk/Asterhall scene.

**Larger issue — Brann's forge-to-ward transition (document only):** In the Tess/Brann afternoon walk scene, the conversation breaks without connective tissue:

> "I was going to ask my father about the forge," Brann said. "Working my own commissions. He wouldn't object — he doesn't need both of us on the same workflow anyway." The pace didn't change. "Before the debt thing."
>
> "The ward," Brann said. "The one you laid in the Pavers' Country."

Nothing bridges these two Brann lines. The jump from the forge/debt disclosure to "The ward" reads either as an accidental cut or as an intentional associative leap that is too elliptical to track. If intentional (Brann's logic: I was thinking about the future → I was thinking about this road → I saw something real on this road), a single action beat between the lines — a pause, a glance, anything — would make the pivot legible as deliberate rather than broken. If it is a cut error, the missing material likely ran: Tess's response to "Before the debt thing," which would have prompted the redirect. Either way, as written, the reader loses the thread of the conversation for two beats and has to rebuild it. Actionable fix: add one beat (a sentence of action or road description) between "Before the debt thing." and "The ward," Brann said." — or confirm the ellipsis is intended and mark it with a dash or whitespace to signal the gap is structural.

## 11-the-advocates-court.md

**Small fix applied:** Compressed the Day 9 entry in the re-casting sequence. Removed "asking about sealed goods in transit" (implied by what follows) and "The corner man who came in and never ordered" (weakest sub-detail in the entire re-casting — briefly planted in ch 5 but carrying no emotional charge here). The entry now reads: *"Day nine. The Crossed Keys at Long Crossing. Rossa. The enforcement wing seal inlaid in her lodger-ledger. The rider who left before dawn with their names and route east."* This removes the most catalogue-feeling sub-sequence in a passage that otherwise earns its inventory structure.

**Larger issue — re-casting connective tissue (document only):** The re-casting reconstructs the operation in reverse chronological order (Day 6 → Day 9 → Day 11 → Day 3 → before Day 3 → bridge/ford/checkpoint), which is psychologically right — Tess moving from the most viscerally memorable back to the earliest. But the joints that make the non-linearity legible — "And further back:" and "And before that:" — are doing their work too visibly. In a passage that should feel like involuntary revelation, two explicit signposts of the backward structure remind the reader of the technique rather than the feeling. Revision options: (a) drop both connectives and let the non-linear order speak for itself — readers who are paying attention will feel the backward movement; (b) replace with subtler pivots that keep the logic without labeling it ("Farther back than that."; "Before that even."). Either approach makes the passage feel more like Tess's unspooling mind and less like a structured retrospective.

**Larger issue — emotional processing after Mara's name (document only):** The macro arc assessment flagged that ch 11 carries the heaviest emotional payload in the book and risks feeling compressed. The chapter handles most of this well — the re-casting and ultimatum sections both breathe. The one place where compression remains real is the ~220-word processing paragraph between "Mara Weft," Sable said. "Of Drey."* and the re-casting section. For the book's deepest revelation — that Mara knowingly sent her fifteen-year-old apprentice into an enforcement operation, and called it trust — 220 words of interiority may be thin. The paragraph is high quality; the question is whether it is long enough. The cold-hands return at the close of the ultimatum section ("Tess's hands had gone back to cold") signals that the reveal is still active, which is the right structural choice. But in revision, a sentence or two of additional texture in the processing paragraph — something that makes legible the specific shape of the betrayal question (protection or condescension?) — would slow the revelation to the speed it deserves before the re-casting takes over.
