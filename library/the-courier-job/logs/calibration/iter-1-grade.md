# Calibration grade — iteration 1

## Compared sample against brief anchors

**Exemplar anchors used:**
- Bardugo (*Six of Crows*): declarative compression, image-first, setup-and-payoff that reveals character. Short sentences. Dialogue does double duty.
- Novik (*Scholomance*): bold-assertion-then-escalation. Commits to an outrageously flat claim in sentence one and earns it.
- Adeyemi (*Children of Blood and Bone*): drop directly into action or dialogue, no preamble.

Cross-register target: punchy opener, banter as the chapter's bloodstream, fast and funny and sometimes real, **every chapter ends on a cliffhanger or mid-action beat**.

---

## What the sample does well

- **Strong bold declarative opener.** "The bag was heavier than it looked." — Novik mode, correct. Commits immediately to a physical-and-emotional fact. The repetition in paragraph two ("Heavier than it looked.") earns its echo without feeling labored.
- **Tight POV discipline.** Mara is entirely external throughout: her actions, her words, her pauses. The sample never slips into her perspective. Tess infers Mara through behavior ("Mara let it sit") without stating interiority.
- **Sentence-length distribution is appropriate.** Short declaratives dominate (3–8 words). A few mid-length compounds for rhythm. No 40-word periodic sentences. Compression is genuine.
- **Dialogue density is solid for a two-person scene.** Roughly 50% of the prose is dialogue; the exchange is load-bearing (contract terms, the other three party members, the warning about Iri) and doesn't feel padded.

---

## What the sample drifts on

1. **The chapter ending is a literary fade — a hard rule violation.**
   > *"Tess took her kit and went."*
   
   This is quiet, resolved, and final. It is the textbook literary fade: character acts, scene closes, mood lands. The brief says "Every chapter ends on a cliffhanger or mid-action beat. No exceptions." The guidance says "Literary fade — a quiet, resolved, reflective ending — is **prohibited in every chapter**." Even a departure scene must end on unresolved tension: something Mara said that cannot be walked back, a detail Tess noticed that doesn't yet mean anything, a door opening onto something unexpected. "And went" ends it; the reader is not pulled forward.

2. **Post-scene subtext naming — a named anti-default is present.**
   > *"These were the terms: Mara said the thing that mattered once, in exactly the words she meant, and that was enough."*
   
   This sentence states what the scene was emotionally about. It names the Mara-Tess relational dynamic and explains the subtext that the prior dialogue already conveyed. The style guide explicitly flags this as a failure mode: "Post-scene subtext naming — ending a chapter or scene with a sentence that states what the scene was emotionally about." The sample does it here and it deflates the compression that the rest of the dialogue built.

3. **Adult literary register in the farewell beat.**
   > *"She was not a woman who took your hand — hadn't been, in three months, in any situation that might have called for it. She took Tess's hand now."*
   
   This is beautifully written — and that is the problem. The emotional construction ("not a woman who... hadn't been... in any situation that might have called for it") is adult literary fiction register: the delayed subordinate clause, the interlocking negation, the meaning carried in structure. Compare to Bardugo's Inej passages or Novik's El Naouri observations, which hit emotional weight through compressed directness, not through syntactic architecture. For a 13-15 year-old reading this, the emotional move is real but the construction reads above register. YA at this level renders the same beat through physical specificity and less syntactic machinery.

4. **No contemporary-coded voice anywhere in the sample.**
   The brief calls for "contemporary-coded" characters who "sound like teenagers, not like medieval peasants." Modern idiom is "acceptable and expected." The sample's dialogue is entirely period-appropriate and professional-toned ("Sealed delivery, named recipient. You open it, the contract voids."). Mara and Tess both speak like adults in a formal-register fantasy world. There is no moment where a word or phrase anchors the contemporary-coded register that Bardugo and Novik both sustain. Even in an exposition-heavy departure scene, Tess's interior voice should carry at least one contemporary-sounding beat.

## Anti-defaults check

1. **No extended interiority opening.** ✓ — The opener is object/action, not interior monologue.
2. **No long interior observation blocks.** ✓ — Tess's internal observations are 2–3 sentences at most before returning to external action or dialogue.
3. **No multi-POV drift.** ✓ — Entirely Tess's lens. No moment of inhabiting Mara.
4. **No restrained action climax.** N/A — departure scene; not an action chapter.
5. **"Do not gloss the banter."** ✗ — "These were the terms..." glosses the relational subtext of the preceding dialogue exchange. Named failure mode, present in sample.
6. **No worldbuilding opening.** ✓ — Opens in media res with object and physical fact.
7. **No "she noted / she registered / she found herself thinking."** ✓ — Absent. Tess observes through action throughout.

## Revision approach

Two sections of `canon/agent-guidance/drafter.md` need tightening:

**Chapter-Ending Conventions** — The guidance is clear but abstract. The sample still produced a literary fade even under that guidance. The revision should add a concrete worked example showing how even a departure or farewell scene must end on unresolved tension, not resolution — demonstrating the difference between "Tess took her kit and went" (prohibited) and an ending that cuts before the resolution.

**Anti-Defaults #5 and the post-scene subtext naming failure** — The guidance names this failure mode but the sample still produced it. Add a before/after example that mirrors the pattern the sample used (a sentence naming the relational/emotional dynamic of the scene).

**Voice Register — contemporary-coding** — The existing guidance describes it but gives no contrast example. Add a brief before/after showing the difference between adult-literary emotional construction and YA-register construction for the same beat, so the drafter has a concrete target for the farewell/emotional moments.
