# Calibration grade — iteration 2

## Compared sample against brief anchors

The brief anchors this book in three exemplars:

- **Bardugo (Six of Crows ch. 1)**: declarative compression, absurdist image-first setup, punchy short sentences, setup-and-payoff that reveals character. *"Joost had two problems: the moon and his mustache."*
- **Novik (Scholomance)**: bold-assertion-then-escalation — first sentence commits to a specific, slightly outrageous, entirely honest position, and the chapter earns it. *"I decided that Orion needed to die after the second time he saved my life."*
- **Adeyemi (Children of Blood and Bone)**: immediate drop into action or dialogue, no preamble whatsoever. *"'Pick me.'"*

The brief specifies: 13–15 / YA audience, fast pace, 7th–8th grade reading level, contemporary-coded voice, cliffhanger chapter endings without exception.

The sample opens on *"The bag was heavier than it looked."* — a valid bold-declarative physical opener. The scene is grounded in specifics (cold stone floor through socks, candle on the table, sealed wax, kit bag and its straps), dialogue tags are spare, and single POV is maintained throughout. These are real strengths.

Where the sample drifts: (a) two or three key interior observations slide into adult-literary syntactic construction rather than the self-conscious, physically anchored teenage register the brief requires, and (b) the chapter ending lands as a quiet, resolved, literary fade — an explicitly named prohibition in the guidance.

---

## What the sample does well

- **Valid opener**: *"The bag was heavier than it looked."* commits immediately to a physical specific. No preamble, no worldbuilding delay, no re-summary of the situation. The reader is inside the scene before the second sentence.
- **Physical specificity throughout**: cold floor through socks, dawn in thin lines through the shutters, wax seal in candlelight, dried willow bark and salt-cold. The world enters through a character's senses, not through the narrator explaining it.
- **POV discipline**: Mara is entirely external. Her interiority never appears. Tess's read of Mara is framed as inference, not fact — *"Mara's voice was the same voice she used for most things"* stays on the observable surface.
- **Spare dialogue tags**: *"said"* only; action beats used as tags (*"Mara said, setting a candle on the table"*). No interpretive tags. No adverbs. No glossing what the dialogue means.

---

## What the sample drifts on

- **Chapter ending is a literary fade — explicit rule violation**: The sample ends on:

  > *Tess waited for the rest of it.*
  > *There wasn't any.*

  This is a quiet, emotionally resolved cut. The reader understands the moment completely. There is no unresolved question, no arriving threat, no revelation held open. The guidance's explicit contrast example (Tess already down the road, realizing Mara never said who the named recipient was) lands on an open question that compels the page-turn. *"There wasn't any"* lands instead on understood silence — a literary fade by the book's own definitions. The guidance forbids this in every chapter without exception.

- **Adult-literary register in emotional-observation passages**: Two passages drift meaningfully from YA-contemporary into adult-literary syntactic construction:

  > *"The way she said* real magic *landed the way it always did — like a fact, not a judgment, which somehow made it worse."*

  The *"like a fact, not a judgment, which somehow made it worse"* construction — paired negation followed by a syntactic pivot — is an adult-literary signature. It renders an emotion through syntactic analysis. The YA register for the same beat would render it through a physical signal or a self-conscious behavioral reaction (the guidance example: *"Tess didn't know what to do with her face"*). A fifteen-year-old would notice the sting of it; she wouldn't complete the philosophical parsing.

  > *"Mara's voice was the same voice she used for most things — steady, not unkind, not particularly interested in what Tess thought about it."*

  *"Steady, not unkind, not particularly interested in what Tess thought about it"* — three-point restrained analysis delivered with adult composure. A fifteen-year-old narrator noticing this same thing would likely register it more bluntly and more physically.

- **Post-scene subtext naming (the "real magic" gloss)**: The italicized *real magic* in Mara's speech already does its work — the reader hears the distinction Mara is drawing. The sentence that follows (*"landed the way it always did — like a fact, not a judgment, which somehow made it worse"*) names what was already implied. The guidance explicitly prohibits this: "Do not gloss the banter — and do not name the subtext of a scene after it happens." The gloss undoes what the italics accomplished.

- **Dialogue density falls short of the 6+ line target**: The longest run of uninterrupted dialogue is three lines (*"Don't pick at it." / "I wasn't going to pick at it." / "You're touching it."*). The guidance targets at least one passage of 6+ consecutive lines of pure dialogue per chapter.

## Anti-defaults check

1. **Do not open chapters on extended interiority** — PASS. Opener is physical; the first extended interior beat doesn't arrive until the third paragraph.
2. **Do not let interior observation run long** — PARTIAL PASS. The guild-mark paragraph runs four interior sentences (*"A mark she didn't recognize exactly..."* through *"She would have needed another thirty seconds to really look."*). Borderline.
3. **Do not write the ensemble as four POVs** — N/A. Only Mara and Tess are present; Mara is fully external. PASS.
4. **Do not build to restrained climaxes** — N/A for this scene type.
5. **Do not gloss the banter / name subtext** — FAIL. The *real magic* gloss names what the italics already communicated.
6. **Do not open with worldbuilding** — PASS.
7. **Do not use "she noted," "she registered," etc.** — PASS. No labeled-cognition tags present.
8. **Do not write emotional beats in adult-literary register** — FAIL. Two instances (see above).
9. **Chapter ending must be cliffhanger/mid-action** — FAIL. Literary fade.

Three anti-defaults violated, two of them explicit named rules in the guidance.

---

## Revision approach

The existing guidance is well-structured and the core instructions are sound. Two sections need concrete tightening:

**Chapter-ending section:** The guidance already has a "departure-scene trap" with a correctly constructed example. The sample still landed in the trap — which means the trap description needs a second named variant. Add a *"silence trap"* warning: when a scene ends on quiet understood weight, the reader feels resolved rather than impelled. The exact ending from this sample (*"Tess waited for the rest of it. / There wasn't any."*) should be given as a named negative example so the drafter can see precisely why it reads as a literary fade.

**Anti-default #8 (adult-literary emotional beats):** The guidance's one contrast pair is good but may not be enough. Add a second named failure pattern: the *"paired negation + pivot"* construction — *"like X, not Y, which somehow made it Z"* — as a specific adult-literary signature to avoid. In YA register, the same emotional content comes through a physical signal or a brief self-conscious behavioral reaction, not syntactic analysis. Naming this specific construction means the drafter can catch it before it reaches the sample.

Decision: REVISE
