# Drafter Guidance — *The Sealed Bag* (working title)

---

## Voice Register

The target register is classic-but-modern YA party fantasy: fast, banter-forward, physical, occasionally funny at the same moment it's serious. Three exemplar cadences from the brief:

- **Bardugo (Six of Crows)**: declarative compression, image-first, the setup-and-payoff joke that reveals character. *"Joost had two problems: the moon and his mustache."* Short sentences. Dialogue that does double duty. World emerges through what characters do, not through what the narrator explains.
- **Novik (Scholomance)**: bold-assertion-then-escalation. The narrator commits to an outrageously flat claim in the first sentence and then the chapter earns it. *"I decided that Orion needed to die after the second time he saved my life."* Tess's voice should do this: opener sentences that commit immediately to a specific, often slightly absurd, entirely honest position.
- **Adeyemi (Children of Blood and Bone)**: drop directly into action or dialogue, no preamble. The world exists because someone is moving through it, not because the narrator introduced it.

The cross of all three: punchy opener, banter as the chapter's bloodstream, physical action rendered concretely, the occasional moment where a joke stops being a joke.

---

## Sentence-Length Expectations

- **Average sentence length**: 12–18 words
- **Maximum acceptable sentence length**: 30 words; anything longer is a warning sign — break it unless the rhythm demands it
- **Short-sentence rhythm**: runs of 3–4 short declaratives (3–8 words each) are the natural beat; vary with a mid-length compound sentence, not with a 45-word periodic
- **In action sequences**: shorter. Drop to 5–10 word sentences. Fragments are acceptable when the action is fast.

---

## Chapter-Opening Conventions

The first paragraph of a chapter must contain either dialogue or concrete physical action, or a bold declarative interior statement that commits to something specific. Do not open on:

- A paragraph of setting description before a person appears
- An abstract observation about the journey, the road, the world
- A re-summary or recap of the previous chapter

Valid openers:
1. **Bold declarative** (Novik mode): *"The innkeeper was lying. Tess had known it from the moment he said 'unfortunately.'"*
2. **Action drop** (Adeyemi mode): *"The first arrow hit the cart wheel before anyone heard the bow."*
3. **Banter cold-open** (Bardugo mode): *"'I've been thinking,' said Pip. 'Don't,' said Brann."*

The setting enters through the character moving through it. Within the first two paragraphs, the reader should know: who, where (roughly), and what is happening right now.

---

## Chapter-Ending Conventions

**Every chapter ends on a cliffhanger or mid-action beat. No exceptions.**

Target distribution for 12 chapters (not in runs — distribute deliberately):
- **5–6 chapters**: threat arrives or becomes undeniable; cut at the moment of recognition before any response
- **3–4 chapters**: revelation lands and is not answered; cut before the response or decision
- **2–3 chapters**: action cuts at peak tension (chapter stops mid-event, next chapter continues)

Literary fade — a quiet, resolved, reflective ending — is **prohibited in every chapter**. Even the final chapter (an open decision-point) should end on the weight of Tess's choice, not on a mood paragraph.

The last sentence of a chapter can be very short. Two or five words. Trust the cut.

---

## POV Strategy

**Single POV: Tess, every chapter.** This is non-negotiable. Other characters' interiority does not appear on the page. What Brann feels, what Iri is thinking, what Pip knows that he's not saying — all of it comes through Tess's observation and inference, framed as her read: *"Tess thought Iri was angry, or something adjacent to it"*, not *"Iri was angry."*

Tess processes fast and notices details. Her inference can be wrong. When it is wrong, the chapter's events reveal the error without announcement.

No omniscient narrator moments. No "meanwhile, elsewhere." If something happens that Tess doesn't witness, the reader doesn't get it until Tess does.

---

## Dialogue Density

High. Target: **at least one passage of 6+ lines of pure dialogue per chapter** (a banter exchange or argument or information scene). In some chapters, 60–70% of the prose may be dialogue and this is correct.

Dialogue functions:
- **Character revelation**: who these four are emerges through banter, not through Tess describing them
- **Plot load-bearing**: information the party needs is delivered through dialogue, not through narration
- **Comic engine**: Pip's jokes, Brann's deadpan, Iri's literalism, Tess's dry observations

Dialogue tags: spare. "Said" and "asked." Action beats as tags ("Pip spread his hands."). No adverb tags. No interpretive tags ("as if to say," "which meant"). The line carries its own meaning.

---

## Comic Timing and Tonal Balance

The register is **funny and serious at the same time, not alternating between them**. The best moments are when Pip's joke lands and then is immediately complicated by what happens next. Or when Brann's deadpan delivery makes something funnier than it has any right to be.

Comic structure to use:
- **Setup-pause-punchline in dialogue**: Pip says something, Brann's response is the punchline delivered without inflection
- **Tess's dry observation**: she notices something absurd about the situation and names it flatly ("The ambushers had chosen a terrible spot; there was nowhere to stand without ankle-deep mud")
- **Iri's accidental literalism**: she says the precise, correct thing that is also slightly wrong for the situation in a way nobody expected

Tonal balance: humor does not undercut genuine danger or emotional stakes. The jokes do not make light of the real cost. When someone is scared, Tess can still be dry about it — but the fear is real.

---

## Anti-Defaults — What the Drafter Must Actively Avoid in This Register

The literary attractor will try to pull the prose toward extended interiority, restrained emotional register, and meditative chapter structure. For this book, that is a failure state. Specifically:

1. **Do not open chapters on extended interiority.** Do not let Tess sit in her own thoughts for more than 3–4 sentences without a sense break or external action.
2. **Do not let interior observation run long.** If Tess's thoughts have been the subject for more than a paragraph, something external must happen. Cut to dialogue or action.
3. **Do not write the ensemble as four POVs taking turns.** Tess is the single lens. Resist the pull to briefly inhabit Brann's perspective "just for a moment."
4. **Do not build to restrained climaxes.** Action chapters (3, 6, 9) must deliver physical confrontation, not interiority around it. The fight is the scene. The thinking happens after.
5. **Do not gloss the banter.** If Pip makes a joke that reveals something, let it. Do not add a sentence explaining what it revealed.
6. **Do not open with worldbuilding.** The world is the backdrop; the chapter is the event. Start in the event.
7. **Do not use "she noted," "she registered," "she found herself thinking."** These are labeled cognition. Tess observes through action. If she notices something, have her notice it — don't announce that she's noticing.
