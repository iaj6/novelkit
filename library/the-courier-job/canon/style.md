# Style

## POV and Tense

Third-person limited past tense. **Single POV: Tess, every chapter, no exceptions.** Other characters' interiority does not exist on the page. What Brann feels, what Iri is thinking, what Pip knows — all of it reaches the reader through Tess's observation and inference. No omniscient narrator moments. No "meanwhile, elsewhere" cuts. No four-POV ensemble drift.

When the narration has insight about another character, it is framed as Tess's read, not as fact: "Tess thought Iri was angry, or something adjacent to it" rather than "Iri was angry."

---

## Sentence Rhythm

Short to medium sentences dominate. **Average sentence length: 12–18 words.** The ceiling is approximately 30 words; anything longer should earn its length through rhythm, not information density. When in doubt, cut the sentence in half.

The Bardugo exemplar is the rhythm anchor: *"Joost had two problems: the moon and his mustache."* Declarative, compressed, image-first. The sentence makes a statement and stops. The next sentence makes a different statement.

Variety in rhythm comes from mixing short punchy sentences (3–8 words) with mid-length compound sentences. Long sentences are rare and reserved for deliberate pacing effects — a slow stretch of road, a moment of held tension. They should not appear in action sequences or dialogue-heavy scenes.

---

## Dialogue Density

High. **Banter is constant.** Dialogue is the primary vehicle for characterization — who these four are emerges through what they say to each other, not through Tess's description of them. In a chapter's first few pages, dialogue should be present within the first two or three paragraphs.

Dialogue tags are spare: "said," "asked," the occasional action beat as a tag. Interpretive tags ("as if to say," "which meant") are not permitted. Adverb-heavy tags ("she said bitterly," "he asked sarcastically") should be replaced with the line reading the way it's supposed to or with a brief action beat.

A page that is 70% dialogue is not thin for this register. It may be doing exactly the right thing.

---

## Chapter Openings

**Immediate.** Drop into action or dialogue on the first line.

Three valid opening modes:
1. **Bold declarative interior** (the Novik cadence): a flat, committed statement from Tess's perspective that commits to something interesting — *"The innkeeper was lying, and Tess had known it from the moment he said the word 'unfortunately'."*
2. **Dropped into action** (the Adeyemi mode): a line of dialogue or a physical action, no preamble — *"Pick me."* / *"The first arrow hit the wagon wheel before any of them heard the bow."*
3. **Punchy image-first** (the Bardugo mode): a compressed, specific image or situation-statement — *"They were three days from Long Crossing and Pip had already told the same story twice."*

**What does not work:** A paragraph of setting description before any person appears. An abstract observation about the journey. A re-summary of what happened in the previous chapter. The chapter earns its setting by putting a character in motion through it.

---

## Chapter Endings

**Every chapter ends on a cliffhanger or mid-action beat. No exceptions.**

Literary fade — ending on mood, reflection, or a quiet resolved moment — is **not permitted** in any chapter of this book.

Permitted ending modes:
- A threat arrives or becomes undeniable (cut at the moment of recognition)
- A revelation lands and is not answered (cut before the response)
- Action cuts at peak tension (the chapter stops mid-event, the next chapter picks it up)
- A line of dialogue that cannot be walked back (the last line is someone saying something that changes the situation)

The last line of a chapter can be very short. A two-word or five-word final line that cuts the scene at peak tension is a craft choice, not an abrupt stop.

---

## Narrative Distance

Close but not suffocating. Tess's observations filter everything, but the prose does not stay inside her skull for more than 3–4 sentences before something external happens.

Target balance: approximately **60% external** (action, dialogue, world) / **40% internal** (Tess's inference, assessment, feeling). When interiority runs long, find the next action and go to it.

Interior observation is rendered as perception and inference, not as labeled emotion: not *"Tess felt afraid"* but *"Her hands had gone cold, which was inconvenient."* Show the physical signal; let the reader name the feeling.

---

## Tonal Register

Contemporary-coded YA. Characters sound like teenagers, not like medieval peasants. Modern idiom is acceptable and expected. The world is fantasy-medieval in setting but not in voice.

The tone is **fast-and-funny-and-sometimes-real**. The humor is constant (Pip is always cracking jokes, Tess is dry, Brann is deadpan, Iri is accidentally funny through excessive precision) but does not undercut genuine stakes. The best moments in the book are when a joke stops being a joke. When Pip makes a cutting observation that turns out to matter. When Brann says one thing and it covers three things he didn't say.

---

## Action Sequences

Physical and concrete. Specificity over abstraction: not "there was a fight" but "Brann caught the man's wrist, turned it, and the knife went into the mud." Magic in action sequences has visible cost — Iri's working should be described in terms of what it does to her, not just what it does to the world. No slow-motion interiority during action; Tess processes fast, in fragments, in the sequence the events happen.

---

## Failure Modes

The AI-author patterns most tempting in this story's register. The drafter should actively avoid these; the compression pass should cut them.

- **Sentence-after-the-sentence** — a punchy line lands, then the next sentence explains or glosses it. This kills the Bardugo-style compression immediately. This register lives and dies on lines that land without annotation.
- **Meta-commentary on cognition** — "She noticed," "she registered," "she realized she was thinking about." This labels the observation and loses it. Tess observes through action and inference, not through labeled cognition.
- **Post-scene subtext naming** — ending a chapter or scene with a sentence that states what the scene was emotionally about ("And that was the moment Tess understood they were a team"). The cliffhanger contract fights this, but it is tempting; cut it every time.
- **Dialogue glosses** — tags that interpret rather than ground: *"he said, as if he meant more than that."* The banter carries its own weight. It does not need translation.
- **Redundant transition / re-summary** — opening a chapter with a paragraph recapping the previous chapter or establishing that the party is still on the road. Each chapter is a new named encounter. Open inside it, not before it.

---

## Lean-Into Patterns

Patterns the drafter and editors should NOT treat as failures, because this register depends on them.

- **Bold declarative interior statement** — Tess opening a scene or chapter with a flat, committed assertion. This is voice, not over-articulation; it is the Novik cadence. Use it freely.
- **High dialogue-to-prose ratio** — a page that is 70% or more dialogue is not thin; it may be doing exactly the right work. Banter is the primary characterization engine.
- **Short chapter-ending lines** — a two-word or five-word last line that cuts the scene at peak tension is a deliberate craft choice.
- **Deadpan without annotation** — Brann's jokes and Iri's literalism should land without an explanation that they were funny. Trust the line.
