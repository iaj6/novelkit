# Reader Guidance — *The Sealed Bag* (working title)

---

## Intended Audience

Teen readers, ages 13–15, who read YA fantasy and specifically enjoy party-dynamic stories: multiple distinct characters with different abilities who have to work together despite not particularly choosing to. Readers who have likely read Six of Crows, The Scholomance, or similar ensemble-in-motion fantasy. They expect:

- A protagonist they can anchor to across 12 chapters
- A cast they can tell apart through voice and action, not description
- Chapters that end on a note that forces them to turn the page
- Action that is physically concrete, not abstract
- Humor that is consistent and characterful, not random
- A romantic subplot that is real but not overwhelming
- An ending that is satisfying but sets up the next book

This is not adult literary fantasy. It is not grimdark. It is not romance-forward. It is not high-concept worldbuilding fantasy where the magic system is the point. The party is the point. The journey is the structure. The decision at the end is the subject.

---

## Brief-Adherence Checks to Perform

For each chapter, the Reader must answer these yes/no questions explicitly:

1. **Would a 13–15-year-old finish this chapter?** (Not just "is it well-written" — is it fast enough, does it start in the action, does the ending pull them forward?)
2. **Is Tess the sole POV?** No scene from Brann, Iri, or Pip's perspective — even briefly, even for a paragraph. Check for omniscient narrator slip ("across the room, Iri was thinking that—").
3. **Does the chapter start with dialogue, concrete action, or a bold declarative interior?** No chapter should open with a setting paragraph or a re-summary of the previous chapter.
4. **Does the chapter end on a cliffhanger or mid-action beat?** No exceptions. "Literary fade" (quiet, resolved, mood-based) is not permitted.
5. **Is the dialogue-to-prose ratio appropriate?** Each chapter should have substantial dialogue (at least 40% of the text, and most chapters should run higher). Long stretches of pure narration are a warning sign.
6. **Are the three action set pieces (ch 3, 6, 9) delivering physical action, not interiority around action?** The ambush, the rival confrontation, the near-disaster must be physical and concrete.
7. **Is the romance subplot (Tess/Brann) staying in its lane?** It should be present but not dominating any chapter where it isn't the named focus. It should peak in ch 10 or 11 and not resolve.
8. **Is the Tess/Iri thread advancing?** Track where Tess's intimidation of Iri softens and by what specific event. It must be earned through action, not declared.
9. **Does ch 11's discovery re-cast the ambush and rival party as targeted?** The retroactive re-casting must be specific: what clue was already present that now reads differently?
10. **Does ch 12 end open?** Tess makes a decision we don't see the consequences of. Do not accept a chapter 12 that resolves the discovery cleanly or tells us what Tess decides.

## What "Good" Looks Like for This Book

Good chapter 1 reads like the opening of Six of Crows — you're in the middle of something specific, you have a quick read on at least one character, and the situation is complicated before the end of the first page. The banter between Tess and Mara should do more character work than any description of either of them.

Good action chapters (3, 6, 9) are physical and move fast. You feel where everyone is in the space. The magic that gets used costs the person using it something visible. The reader is scared, then it's over, and the chapter ends before the party has fully processed what just happened.

Good quieter chapters are not slow — they are banter-forward chapters where the party relationship advances through what people say to each other, and the chapter ends on something that changes the stakes. The party trust-building must be visible in behavioral changes, not in speech about feelings.

Good final chapters (11, 12) land the emotional threads (Tess/Brann, Tess/Iri, the discovery about Mara) without lingering too long on any of them. The discovery in ch 11 should feel inevitable in retrospect. The decision in ch 12 should feel like Tess's — specific to who she is — not like a generic YA empowerment moment.

## What to Flag Explicitly If the Manuscript Drifts

These are the specific drift patterns to call out even when the prose is technically skilled:

1. **Literary register drift** — If the prose has shifted toward long sentences, abstract interiority, restrained climax, and meditative chapter structure, flag it explicitly. A beautifully written chapter in the wrong register is a failure for this book. Name it: "This chapter is reading as literary adult fantasy. It needs to be faster, more dialogue-forward, and the chapter ending must be a cliffhanger."

2. **POV leak** — If any sentence presents another character's thoughts as fact rather than Tess's inference, flag it. Even one sentence of Brann's interiority is a POV violation. Name the line.

3. **Resolved chapter endings** — If a chapter ends on a mood paragraph, a reflection, or a quiet beat where the immediate danger has passed, flag it. "This chapter ending is a literary fade; it must be replaced with a cliffhanger or mid-action cut."

4. **Extended interiority without external event** — If Tess's thoughts run for more than 4–6 sentences without an external action or dialogue beat, flag the passage. Tess's interiority is valid; long unbroken interiority blocks are not.

5. **Ensemble drift** — If the party is starting to feel like a four-POV ensemble (the narrative giving each character equal interior weight as if the reader is inhabiting all four of them), flag it. Brann, Iri, and Pip are vivid externally; they are not co-leads.

6. **Bag-as-MacGuffin** — If the sealed bag is functioning as an abstract motivating object rather than a specific one (if the discovery in ch 11 doesn't re-cast specific earlier events), flag it. The depositions, Mara's name among them, and the guild's extortion operation must be specific.

7. **Romance overtaking subplot lane** — If the Tess/Brann thread is becoming the chapter's primary event in chapters where it isn't the named focus, flag it. The romance is real and it should be felt — but it should be visible in the margin of chapters, not the center.

## What to Applaud

These are things the Reader should specifically defend in the "what to leave alone" section if the manuscript hits them well:

1. **High dialogue-to-prose ratio** — Do not recommend adding more narration or description if a chapter is running 60–70% dialogue. That is correct for this register. The banter is the chapter.
2. **Short chapter-ending lines** — A two-word or five-word final line that cuts the scene cold is a craft choice, not a weakness. Defend it.
3. **Pip's jokes that stop being jokes** — When a Pip punchline lands and then is immediately complicated by what follows, or when his banter accidentally reveals something real, do not flag it as tonal inconsistency. That is exactly right.
4. **Iri's accidental literalism** — When Iri says something precise and correct that is also slightly wrong for the emotional temperature of the scene, do not flag it as out-of-character. It is her character. Defend it.
5. **Magic costs that interrupt action** — When a magical working leaves Iri shaking or Tess stranded without her kit, and this creates a problem for the chapter's action, do not flag it as pacing damage. The cost is the point. Defend it.
