# 12 November 1984

She is on the last two lines of the October column when the knock comes.

Stela Ionescu, from the personnel section: a maintenance request across the desk, the boiler-room work order for the second heating pipe joint, which has been leaking since October and bleeds heat from the second-floor corridor before it reaches the administrative wing. The description of the repair is specific — sealing the joint with a wool-and-resin compound, three hours of work after the Sunday shift. The date is correct. The series number matches the maintenance register. Ilinca picks up her pen.

She has known Stela by the corridor and the elevator and the bathroom sink at the end of the hall: eleven years of proximity without occasion for more. Stela has a quiet manner and flat shoes and keeps her coat buttoned in the building until the heating comes on, which in November means until six, which in the administrative wing means until somewhat after that.

While Ilinca signs, Stela says — as a separate sentence, outside the transaction — that her friend from the bloc committee was called in this morning. About the Petrescu matter. Someone had been asking about a piece of paper that was going around the district. Something about what to do when a person was ill.

Ilinca hands the form back.

Stela says: the person asking had a copy with him, and he had read the ninth line aloud from it. The exact wording, as her friend had told her: *If 7 or 8, take her in in the morning.*

She says she thought Ilinca would want to know, given that everyone in the building is talking about it.

Ilinca says thank you. Stela goes, pulling the door not quite shut behind her. A two-centimeter gap of corridor light. Ilinca watches it for a moment. Then she gets up and closes the door properly.

She goes back to her desk. She sets her pen on the blotter. She does not pick it up.

---

The 1983 ledger is on the second shelf between the quarterly labour plan compliance records and the annual balance submission. The brown tape at the top of the spine has held since February. It is a Ministry of Finance form-book, four hundred pages, a state document she is required to produce on demand.

She takes it down from the shelf and carries it to her desk. She sets it flat with the spine uppermost and holds it by the boards, opening the spine at the top, fitting two fingers along the gap between the pages and the board where the paper sits. The paper is folded twice in one direction and once across, a small square. She takes it out by the corner.

She opens it flat on the desk between the ledger and the lamp. She does not touch it again.

The paper has been folded and refolded many times in the fourteen months since she wrote it in October of last year. The creases have gone slightly furry — the fiber pressing up at each fold line into a faint nap — and the outer edge has taken on a soft, handled quality. The ink is still sharp. She used the blue-black she keeps for fair copies. The page shows everything she has asked of it.

She reads from the beginning.

---

1. Keep her warm and lying down. Someone stays with her the whole night.
2. Small sips of water, often. Nothing else by mouth for six hours.
3. Count the cloths. Write the number and the hour on the paper by the bed.
4. If she sleeps, wake her every hour and speak to her until she answers you properly.
5. No visitors. No telephone. If anyone asks, she has the influenza.
6. Burn nothing until she is well. Wash and keep.
7. Two cloths in one hour is too many.
8. A fever that will not come down by morning is too long.
   [ 7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why.
     Do not wait for the morning. ]

---

She reads the bracket three times.

She drew it in October of last year because she understood, before she finished writing, that the ninth instruction was not the same kind of thing as the other eight. A numbered list is a sequence. Lines one through eight are a sequence: what to do first, what comes next, what follows. The bracket is not a step in a sequence. It is a different order of information entirely — not what comes after eight, but what overrides everything above it when the conditions are met. In fourteen years of bookkeeping she has used brackets for the same reason every time: to mark what operates on the column, not what continues it. An item inside a bracket is not the next entry. It is the instruction that changes what the other entries mean.

*Do not wait for the morning.*

She reads the phrase once more. Then she looks away from the document without looking at anything in the room.

She has been given the exact wording of the ninth line as it exists in another copy. *If 7 or 8, take her in in the morning.* She holds the two texts beside each other and reads the difference.

The bracket: *take her in. Do not wait for the morning.*

The numbered line: *take her in in the morning.*

Both contain the word *morning*. In the bracket, *morning* is the limit — the threshold you do not wait for. In the numbered line, *morning* is the time — it is when you go. The bracket says: the sequence has ended; when condition 7 or 8 is true, go now. The numbered line says: when condition 7 or 8 is true, go in the morning.

A bracket given a number has become a step in a list. The word *now* is gone from it. What is left is a sequential instruction with a time attached: *morning.* The woman who made that copy was being tidy. She saw nine pieces of information and she numbered them one through nine, because that is what a careful copyist does with nine pieces of information and a numbered list on the page in front of her. The hand that has just written *8.* has the *9.* ready.

Ilinca counts down the page. Eight numbered lines. Then the bracket.

She counts again. Eight. Then the bracket.

---

She puts the two accounts together and runs the entry that reconciles them.

The master is here, in front of her — eight numbered lines and a bracket, exactly as she wrote it. The three copies she made from the master in the thirteen months since October 1983 are the second generation. She has traced two of those three branches.

The N2 branch goes from Georgeta to Livia: dead end, closed, no Miliția contact, no further transmission. Clean.

The N3 branch goes through her cousin to Elena Florescu: confirmed clean on Day 5, no outside approach, no contact.

The N4 branch — the cellar copy, Strada Crișan, the third of November of last year, twenty minutes, by the light of the door gap — she has not confirmed directly, because confirming it directly means identifying herself in the chain. She has no route to N4 that does not require a name.

But she made N4. She holds the memory of making it the same way she holds a column: in order, line by line. The cellar: the smell of coal dust, the cold at the door, the paper on her knee. The pencil moving down the page. Eight numbered lines, written in sequence — one through eight, each checked as she went. And then the bracket: two strokes and a corner, the corner at the bottom of the last stroke. The text inside the bracket, written inside it, not below it, inside. She checked the copy once after finishing. She is almost certain the bracket was there when she handed it over.

If the N2 branch is clean and the N3 branch is clean, and if N4 is clean — and she is almost certain — then the mutation entered in a generation after her second generation. Someone downstream received a copy with a bracket at the bottom and copied it as a numbered step, because that is what the hand does when it has been moving down a numbered list and arrives at the bottom of the page with the number nine ready. The mutation entered there. Not in anything she made.

This is the direction the entries point.

She knows the wording of the mutation now exactly. She has added one node to her accounting: the fact of the mutant line, confirmed, the exact words of it, the distance between those words and the words in front of her on the desk. Her count is five.

---

She refolds N1 along the furry creases. Once in one direction, once across. The paper makes a soft sound that is the sound of paper that has made this fold many times before. She tucks it into the ledger spine, fitting it along the gap between the pages and the board until it seats at the outer edge. She carries the ledger to the second shelf and sets it between the quarterly labour plan compliance records and the annual balance submission, spine flush with the edge of the shelf.

She goes back to her desk. She picks up the pen.

But before she takes the October column from where she left it, she does what she always does when two accounts that cannot both be correct must be held together: she sets them in the same column and reads what is there.

She knows lines one through eight. She wrote them. She holds each of them complete and exact.

She has been given line nine verbatim — read aloud by a man she has never met, repeated by a woman she has known for eleven years by proximity, delivered to her desk at a quarter past four in the afternoon. She sets both texts in the same column and she reads:

---

1. Keep her warm and lying down. Someone stays with her the whole night.
2. Small sips of water, often. Nothing else by mouth for six hours.
3. Count the cloths. Write the number and the hour on the paper by the bed.
4. If she sleeps, wake her every hour and speak to her until she answers you properly.
5. No visitors. No telephone. If anyone asks, she has the influenza.
6. Burn nothing until she is well. Wash and keep.
7. Two cloths in one hour is too many.
8. A fever that will not come down by morning is too long.
9. If 7 or 8, take her in in the morning.

---

Lines one through eight: hers. Correct.

Line nine: not hers. The error is in the being careful.

She puts her pen to the October column and begins.

The heat comes on at twelve minutes past six: the knock of the joint at the baseboard corner, and then the dull expansion of the pipe along the wall. She does not look up. The figures run to the end of the page. She checks the column twice. Both times it totals correctly.

She caps her pen and sets it in the groove at the right side of the desk. She takes her coat from the hook behind the door and turns off the lamp. The office goes dark except for the yellow line of corridor light under the door. She steps through, closes the door, tries the handle once.

N1 is in the ledger spine. The ledger is on the second shelf. The mutation entered somewhere downstream — in a copy she did not make, in a hand she cannot name, in a generation she has not yet reached.

She walks.
