# 20 noiembrie

The laundry on Strada Mihai occupied the ground floor of the first bloc at the end of the street, where the street bent south and the buildings ran close to the river. The original window frames had been removed when the laundry took over the space; in their place were long horizontal panes of industrial glass that condensed all day and ran gray water down the inside of the sill in thin, continuous channels. From outside, the glass was opaque with moisture. From inside, the light came through diffusely, without shadows, only a general brightness that made the steam visible as it moved between the wash lines.

Munteanu arrived at seven forty-five. The morning delivery rush began at eight. He knew this because he had confirmed it on Thursday, the week he first opened the Petrescu file, before he had any particular reason to come here.

He gave his name at the intake counter, which was a solid wooden shelf running along the right wall, covered in receipt books and a mechanical adding machine and two rubber stamps in their stamp pads. The woman behind the counter was not Damian, Viorica — she was younger, managing the intake receipts and the telephone and the door simultaneously, with the expression of someone accustomed to all three arriving at once. She called to a young woman in a rubber apron near the first wash line, who put down what she was carrying and went to look.

The laundry was a long room — thirty metres at least, possibly more. The ceiling was high enough to accommodate the lines strung from wall to wall in four rows, with institutional cottons and work shirts hanging in stages from dry to damp, so that the back of the room smelled different from the front: the front end smelled of wet and industrial soap and the mineral scale building up inside the caldrons; the back smelled of something on its way to being clean. Along the left wall three caldrons ran at temperature, releasing steam that collected at the ceiling and came back down as condensation. The condensation ran down every vertical surface. In November this meant the walls were wet to the touch. The floor near the caldrons was dark and slick.

Viorica Damian came from behind the fourth wash line.

She was in her mid-forties, broad-shouldered, with her hair pinned close under a cloth cap pushed slightly back at the forehead. She came down the length of the room at a steady pace — no hesitation, no change of speed when she saw him, nothing that would be visible from the intake counter. She looked at him the way she might look at a form that required filling: correctly, and without pleasure. Whatever he was, she had encountered the category before. She carried herself in the particular way of people who had been through official questions before and had come out the other side without explanation. She was not frightened.

They sat at a folding metal table near the condensed window. The table had a ledger open on it; she closed it and moved it to one side before she sat, the gesture automatic, as if she cleared this table several times a day.

He told her he was there about a document. A handwritten document concerning health procedures that had been circulated in the community. He had reason to believe she was in possession of a copy.

She said: yes.

He said he would need it.

She said she would get it.

She went to the back of the room. She was gone perhaps two minutes. When she returned she was carrying a piece of paper folded twice into a compact square. She set it on the folding table. It was the back of a laundry work-order form — printed in pale green ink on institutional paper thin enough that the delivery columns on the other side showed through as faint ruled lines beneath the handwriting. The writing on the back was in pencil, careful and even, in a hand that was not hers. The paper had been handled enough that the fold lines had gone soft and the corners had rounded.

He put it in the evidence envelope without opening it.

He asked where she had gotten it.

From her sister, she said.

He took out his notebook and turned to the page he wanted. The name was already there, in his own handwriting, eight days old: *Moga, Rodica.* Below it the address: Dorobanților, the second bloc on the corner of Eminescu.

He turned the notebook toward her and asked if that was correct.

She looked at the page without touching it. She said: yes, that was correct.

He asked whether she had passed a copy to anyone else.

She said no.

He asked whether there was anything else she wanted to tell him.

She looked at the condensed window for a moment. She said she didn't think so.

He thanked her. He collected the evidence envelope and closed his notebook and stood. He went back down the length of the room — past the first wash line, past the second, past the caldrons and the rising steam that fogged his glasses at the temples — and let himself out through the entry door.

---

The street outside was cold after the heat of the laundry. He stood at the corner of the building and let his glasses clear. Three women were coming from the direction of the main road with laundry bundles, the beginning of the morning rush he had timed to miss. He walked in the other direction, toward the station, and the cold came through his coat collar and settled at the back of his neck.

There was fog off the river. It sat low along the street, below the level of the bloc windows, so that the buildings looked as if they were floating on it. He walked through it without looking at it particularly. The name already on the page, the address confirmed.

Moga, Rodica. G2. One step from the source.

He had not yet visited her. He had the name and address from eight days ago, from the interview with Lucia Gal, who had said she had gotten it from a woman she knew from before the mill — not from the mill itself, from before. He had written the name down and kept working in the other direction, outward and downward, because you built the tree — you mapped what you had — before you walked up it. By the time you reached the upper nodes you needed to know what you were looking for.

---

He was back at his desk by twenty past nine. He took off his coat and put it over the chair back without hanging it and sat down at once.

He opened the evidence envelope and placed the Damian copy on the desk beside the folder with the others.

Four copies. He laid them side by side and weighted the corners with what was at hand: a stapler at the left, a spare ink tin in the middle, an empty buletin folder at the right, and the flat spine of his working notebook pressed across the bottom edges to keep them from curling. The desk was not large enough for four full sheets without overlap; he arranged them in sequence, left to right, and accepted the slight overlap at the edges.

He read line nine on each in order.

The Gal copy first — he knew it, but he read it: *9. If 7 or 8, take her in in the morning.* Written in blue pencil on the grey AFE production form; the nine stood cleanly in the column, followed by the instruction.

The sister-in-law's copy, from the fourth of November: *9. If 7 or 8, take her in in the morning.* Smaller hand, black ink, ordinary household paper. The nine was the same.

The Damian copy — the one he had just collected, on the back of the green laundry form, pencil: *9. 7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why. Do not wait for the morning.*

He read this one twice. The instruction was longer than the other copies' line nine. The wording was different — not *in the morning* but *do not wait for the morning*, which was, strictly, the opposite direction. And yet the format was the same: a numbered nine, the instruction treated as a list item, sequenced after the eighth.

He wrote in his working notebook: *G3 (Damian/Str. Mihai). Line 9: numbered. Wording: "do not wait for the morning" — differs from G4 and G5. Numbered nine present at G3 level. Source of numbered nine: Moga, Rodica (address confirmed).*

He underlined the last sentence.

Then he picked up the fourth copy. He had collected it four days ago during the general canvassing — a woman who had produced it when he asked without needing to be asked twice, who had said she did not know exactly where she had gotten it: from someone she used to know, she thought, from before her current section. He had written that down and kept moving. The copy itself he had placed in the folder and had not examined closely since.

He laid it on the desk, at the far right, past the other three, and read the terminal section.

There was no line nine. There were eight numbered lines — the same format, the same block structure he had been reading for two weeks — and then below the eighth line there was a bracket. A hand-drawn bracket in ink darker than the surrounding text, as if the pen had been pressed down. Two vertical strokes and a horizontal bar connecting them at the top, enclosing two lines of text that ran slightly smaller than the list above:

*7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why.*
*Do not wait for the morning.*

He read it twice. Then he read the three numbered copies again. Then he read the bracketed copy again.

He laid all four side by side and looked at them.

Three copies had a line nine. One copy had a bracket below line eight. The content inside the bracket was close to what the Damian copy said in line nine — not identical, but in the same register, the same instruction. The difference was not in the instruction. The difference was in where on the page the instruction lived and what kind of thing it was treated as being.

A line in a list was a step. A bracket below a step was something else — an annotation, a condition, a note that interrupted the sequence to say something the sequence could not say. The three copies with a numbered nine had turned whatever the bracket was into a ninth step. The copy with the bracket had kept it as a bracket.

He wrote: *Two formats for the terminal instruction. Three copies: numbered line 9. One copy: bracket below line 8. The format is the variable. Which format is the source.*

He drew a line under the question. He did not answer it.

He thought about it for a moment — about which direction a change of this kind would run, from bracket to numbered step or from numbered step to bracket — and he was still two nodes from the answer, which was sitting in an apartment on Dorobanților, eight days written in his notebook, unvisited.

He put the four copies back in the folder in order and closed the folder.

---

The call came through the front desk at seventeen minutes past four. He picked up on the second ring.

His captain said: good afternoon. The Petrescu matter — how was it proceeding.

Munteanu said it was progressing. He had traced the document chain through three generations on the principal branch and had today confirmed the G3 node. The chain now ran clearly from the G5 copy back to a G2 source — the next visit would be to that source, in the coming days.

His captain said he was glad to hear it. He paused briefly. He said: the fifth of December was the administrative deadline for filing a determination with the procuror's office. He assumed Munteanu was aware.

Munteanu said: yes.

His captain said: he was saying it because awareness and action were not always the same relation, in his experience.

Munteanu said he understood.

His captain said: good. He would speak to him Friday.

The receiver went down.

Munteanu looked at his desk. The folder was closed. The ink tin and the stapler and the buletin folder were where he had left them after he cleared the copies away. Outside the window the afternoon had gone dark without his noticing it; the courtyard light had come on at some point in the past hour, throwing a pale rectangle on the paving.

He opened his working notebook to the inside back cover, where he had been keeping his running figures.

He wrote: *20.XI — 5.XII.*

Below that, on its own line: *15.*

He looked at the number.

Fifteen days was not a short time. It was also not a long one — not for a chain this long, working from one end, with the other end still four addresses away. Fifteen days was the number he had. He was going to work with it.

He closed the notebook.
