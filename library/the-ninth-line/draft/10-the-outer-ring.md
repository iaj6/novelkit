# 13 November 1984

The bridge over the Someș was always the worst part, and I say that as someone who spent two years crossing it in the dark at four-thirty in the morning, which gives me more authority on the subject than I would have liked.

It was the fog. Not the cold — I was used to the cold by then, or I had at least stopped expecting anything better from November in the Someș valley, which is not the same thing — but the fog came off the river in autumn and settled on the bridge deck in a way that had nothing to do with temperature and everything to do with the specific disposition of this particular valley toward the people who lived in it. You walked into it and it erased the railings and the far bank and the road beyond, and for thirty or forty seconds you were just a person in white nothing, which at four-thirty in the morning, when you were already thinking about things you should not be thinking about, was not an experience I found helpful.

On the street before the bridge: one lamp still burning in a third-floor window of the first bloc, a woman in a dark coat going the opposite direction who did not look up. I did not look up either. I had always appreciated it in the abstract, but I appreciated it more that November than I ever had before.

The mill smell reached you from two hundred meters out. Hot and wet and chemical — the celofiber production hall, the fiber drying unit, the pressing-and-sizing vats all combining into something that had no single good description and that I had long since stopped smelling as a smell. Two years on the night shift. Somewhere over seven hundred crossings of that bridge. I had done the arithmetic once and been depressed by it and had not done it again. The smell was the smell of being here, which was where I was, and had been for two years, and which was not looking like it was going to stop being the case any time soon.

I went along the road below the water treatment plant, past the parking area with the three bicycles that had been chained to the fence since before I arrived — the chain on the middle one had been rusting for longer than I knew — and around to the production entrance in the side of the weaving hall. The door was propped open during shift change. The guard in the hut was drinking from a metal cup. I showed my shift pass. He did not look at my face.

Inside: the locker bay, then the production corridor. I hung my coat and put my bag under the bench and closed the locker. The geography book was inside, on the shelf above the coat hook — too narrow for anything except exactly that. I did not look at it directly, because I had found in the last ten days that not looking at the geography book directly was a habit I was developing, like the habit of checking the lock twice, like the habit of taking the river path home instead of the main road. I checked the lock a second time and went to the floor.

---

The production floor in the morning was the loom noise. I don't know how to convey the loom noise, which is why I keep stating it as a fact and moving on.

The looms were Hungarian-made, retooled for celofiber yarn some years before I arrived. When all forty-eight were running, which they were when I came onto the floor at five past five, conversation was not possible in any practical sense — not because of any rule, but because anything produced at mouth level was absorbed immediately into the ambient volume, and the person next to you had no particular reason to suspect you had spoken. I had worked in this for two years, and in the last ten days I had come to regard it as one of the more comfortable environments available to me in the district of Dej in November of 1984. I want to be precise about this: I was genuinely grateful for the loom noise in a way I had not anticipated when I signed my contract.

I threaded the machine. Two minutes later my hands knew what they were doing and I had the rest of myself free for other purposes.

What I did with it: watched the women near me without appearing to watch them. Row across from mine: Anca, who has the kind of face that conveys nothing and who I had once seen win an argument in the break room without appearing to have an argument; past her, a woman I had worked beside for six months without ever learning her name, who today had her hair loose on both sides instead of pinned on the left; at the end of the row, a third woman attending her machine with the focused precision of someone who has decided that the machine is the only object in the room.

I recognised that last one as a decision. I had been making a version of it myself.

Three women who looked like they had not slept. I can't tell you now what it looked like exactly. I'm telling you I was getting better at seeing it. Something around the eyes. A quality of attention that was paying attention to something else entirely.

---

The break room was off the production corridor, across from the bathroom. Two long tables, eight chairs each. At the back there was a hot-water urn that had probably been there since the founding of the republic and that produced something technically classifiable as tea if you held your cup under it long enough and did not ask anything more specific from the transaction. I got my cup and sat at the far end of the first table.

Marta — she worked the adjacent loom on my section, she lived on the fourth floor of my bloc, she had a son in the fourth form and a husband who drove for the transport depot — sat down across from me but toward the middle of the table, not beside me, which I noted as a kind of tact. She looked at her cup. She said, without looking at me: they took Floarea and Geta in on Monday.

Both of them from the finishing section. Both from the other end of the hall.

I said: what did they ask them?

She said: what do you think they asked them.

She picked up her tea and moved to the far end of the table, where some other women were already involved in something about the bread queue on Victoriei, and within two minutes she was involved in it too.

I sat with my cup.

---

I knew Floarea the way you know someone from six months of sitting behind them on the morning bus — the back of her head, her posture, a blue coat she wore on cold days. Her name I had heard once in the break room.

I did not know whether she had a copy of any paper. I did not know whether she knew anyone who did. I did not know whether, in the specific context that now had a context, she knew that Doina Marcu from the night shift existed in any relevant sense.

Here is what I had been trying to think clearly about for ten days, and what kept refusing to go clear: this was not built like something that has a diagram. It was not a thing where you could stand at any point and draw lines outward and eventually reach all the other points. It was: one person knowing one person, the paper traveling between those two, neither of them necessarily knowing anyone else in the exchange. The woman on the bus had not asked my name. She had put the paper in my hand before I had finished deciding whether I was accepting it, and by the time I thought to say something she was looking out the window at the November fields outside Bonțida, and the moment had passed.

So what I knew about Floarea's connection to any of this was: nothing certain. There might be six steps between her line and mine. There might be none in common. They might share a branch with me that went all the way back to the same source, or they might be on a branch I had never had any connection to at all, in which case the fact that they were now in the file had nothing to do with my locker and I was fine.

I thought: it's probably fine.

Then I sat with the specific texture of "probably fine" for a moment.

Then I went back to the floor.

---

The second half of the shift, I worked. I want to be accurate about this because I remember being faintly surprised by it at the time: I worked. My hands moved. The loom ran. At some point the light coming through the clerestory windows above the machines changed from the flat dark of five o'clock to the particular grey of November morning — grey through a layer of hall condensation, but light — and this helped, in the way that light always helps even when you can't account for why it should.

The thing you can say about a loom is that it requires you to be there — not your feelings, but your hands, which is what I had to offer, and which held.

The woman at the end of the row kept her eyes on her machine. Anca did too. Near the end of the shift Anca looked up and caught my eye for a second. We both looked back.

---

After the shift most people went left from the production entrance, toward the main road and the bus stop and the bread shop on Victoriei that was already open by seven. I went right, along the wall of the fiber drying unit — forty meters where the heat coming off the ductwork almost reached the backs of your hands — and then around and down toward the river path.

The fog was still on the Someș at half past seven. Light was coming up behind the eastern ridge in the flat grey-gold way it comes in the valley in November: the sun above the ridge line, spreading flat along the bottom of the cloud layer without appearing in any particular place, so that everything was lit from nowhere and nothing had a shadow. The fog sat on the water in long horizontal shapes that moved with the current but went nowhere. The clay path alongside the river was wet and soft. My boots had not been fully waterproof since September, which I had known about and continued not to address, the same way you continue not to address things that are below the current top of your list.

*Keep her warm, and lying down.* Line one. It came to me the way things lodged in that particular slot come up — not because I had thought of it but because I had been not-thinking of it long enough and hard enough that it surfaced on its own. I knew line one the way I knew my hostel room number: by repetition, without intending to. I knew line three. I knew line seven. I knew the bracket at the bottom, the whole bracket. I could have recited it in order if there had been anyone to recite it to, which there wasn't and wasn't going to be, because the whole point was that you recited it to nobody. *Someone stays with her the whole night.*

The geography book was in my locker. My locker was locked. I had the key on my ring with the hostel key and my aunt's spare — the small brass key she had pressed into my hand when I moved to the hostel in 1982, which I had carried for two years and had never once needed to use. The man in the good coat had been asking questions at the mill and in the blocs for a week. He had taken Floarea and Geta in on Monday, both from the finishing section on the other side of the hall. He had not come to the locker bay.

The river path turned at the end onto the hostel road. I walked past the bench where people sat in summer. The kiosk on the corner was shut. The corner lamp was still on from the night.

I did not know if not yet was the same as not going to.

The fog moved on the Someș behind me. My feet were cold. I walked.
