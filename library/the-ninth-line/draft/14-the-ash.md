# 17 noiembrie

The district heating had come on for half an hour in the night and stopped before I was awake enough to notice. I know this because when I put my hand on the radiator at seven o'clock it was just barely warm — not warm enough to do anything useful, the residual warmth of a system that had fulfilled its obligation and moved on — and by eight it was the same temperature as the wall. By nine I had my coat on top of the blanket, which is a method that works until you have to move and only addresses one of the two problems in a cold hostel room in November.

I had the room to myself. Floarea has been on day shift since October, which means she is at the factory before six and back before four, and on my days off I have the room from seven in the morning until the afternoon, which is a quantity of solitude unusual enough that I still notice it and have been noticing it since October. My days off I normally spend sleeping until ten and then going to the market and then coming back and sleeping again, which is the correct use of a day off when you work nights. Today I had been awake since seven, lying on my back with my coat over the blanket and looking at the geography book on the table.

I had brought it home from the factory on Thursday. This is not something I do as a rule. The geography book lives in the locker in the women's changing room — second row from the left, the locker with the number plate where the seven looks like a one if you are not paying attention — in a space where nothing ever happens and no one has any reason to look. It has been there since I understood, in my third week at the mill, what it was carrying between pages thirty-four and thirty-five. Bringing it home means bringing the problem home, which is not the kind of problem that improves with proximity. But I had brought it home this time for a reason, which was that I needed the original in the room in order to find out whether I still needed the original in the room.

The test is simple in principle. You write the document out from memory, without looking at the original, and then you compare what you wrote against what is on the page. I have passed examinations this way; the method is not new. What was new was what I would have to do with the test paper afterward. But that was a manageable problem, and I had been managing it in my head since Thursday, so I got up.

---

The piece of paper was in the front pocket of my bag, where I had put it on Thursday for exactly this purpose. It was the back of a utility calculation form — the kind of form that is distributed constantly by the calculation office and turns up in pockets and bags and on windowsills without anyone being able to account for it. The front had numbers on it that had nothing to do with me. The back was blank.

I sat at the table with the geography book closed in front of me and the utility form turned over to the plain side and uncapped the pen.

*1.* I wrote, and then I waited for the line to come.

*Keep her warm, and lying down. Someone stays with her the whole night.*

The comma after *warm* I have been practicing. Without it the line runs *warm and lying down*, which is how I heard it in my head for the first several weeks — the comma does not arrive naturally, it has to be placed. I know why it is there: it makes two separate directives out of what otherwise sounds like one, and the document is precise about that kind of distinction in a way that I have come to understand as intentional. The comma is the document being careful. I placed the comma and moved on.

Lines two and three went down without trouble. Line four stopped me.

Line four is: *If she sleeps, wake her each hour and speak to her until she answers you properly.* The word that matters is *each*. I have been working on *each* since November began, since I first tried to recite line four from memory on the night shift and came out with *every* instead. Every and each mean the same thing in ordinary usage, which is exactly why *every* is what you write if you are not paying careful attention. I wrote the line and then read what I had written: *wake her every hour.* I put a line through *every* and wrote *each* above it. I continued to line five, finished the list through line eight, and went back to read from the beginning. At line four I had written *every* again. Not the crossed-out *every* — a fresh one, below the correction, as though I had decided the correction did not apply. I cannot account for this. I crossed out the second *every*, wrote the line over on the next available line of the form, said *each* aloud twice while I was writing it, and moved on.

Lines five and six. Six says *Keep and wash*, and I had the order right. Seven. Eight — *A fever that will not come down by morning is too long.* I paused at eight, the way I always pause at eight, for reasons I have been unable to isolate. Eight has the word *morning* in it. Since the beginning of November everything with *morning* in it has acquired a quality I do not have a name for.

I picked up the pen and wrote line nine.

Except there is no line nine. That is the thing. The ninth element is not a line nine in the sequence of the other lines; it is a bracket that sits below line eight and is surrounded by square brackets to signal that it is different from what is above it — a condition that overrides the sequence rather than extending it. I put the left bracket and wrote what was inside it: *7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why. Do not wait for the morning.* New line, indented, the closing bracket.

I read it back. That was right. That was exactly what it said.

---

I tore the utility form into quarters. The hostel bathroom at the end of the corridor has a window above the middle sink that opens over the yard, and on the windowsill there is a tin ashtray with a bent rim, and there is always a box of matches beside it. I have known about the ashtray since my second week in the building. Three of the four women on this floor smoke, and the bathroom is where they do it in winter when the yard is too cold, and the ashtray is a fixture as permanent as the sink.

The bathroom was empty. On a Thursday morning at half past nine, with the day-shift women at the factory and Floarea in her section, there was no reason for anyone to be in the bathroom except me. I opened the window and the November air came in off the yard, carrying the specific smell of the yard in late autumn — concrete, a little mud, the bins.

I put the torn quarters of the utility form in the ashtray. I took the first match from the box and dropped it on the tile because my hands were colder than I had understood them to be. I took the second match. It caught.

The paper was dry and went fast. The writing I had done — all nine elements, the bracket, the two crossed-out *every*s and the correction — curled at the edges and went dark and then went to white, and the white crumbled when I touched it with the spent match. Thirty seconds, at most. A page and a half of my handwriting turned into a small pile of ash that occupied roughly the space of one cigarette-end, which is less than you would expect from a page and a half of handwriting.

I carried the ashtray to the toilet. The ash flushed without trouble.

I put the ashtray back on the windowsill. I closed the window. I went back to my room.

---

The geography book was on the table where I had left it.

I want to say something here about what the woman who gave it to me looked like, because I have been asked — later, in a different context, under circumstances I am coming to — and the honest answer is that I cannot give you a picture that would help you find her. She had a face like a closed shop. She sat two rows ahead of me on the bus from Gherla and she put the paper in my hand as we were coming into Bonțida, the way you return something to a person who has dropped it, quickly, and then she looked out the window at the fields going past and did not look back. I held the paper in my lap for the rest of the trip into Dej and when I got off the bus I put it in my coat pocket and when I got to the hostel I put it in the geography book because the geography book was the first thing I could think of that nobody would open. Including me. I did not know her name. I did not ask. She did not offer it and I did not ask, which in the moment was simply not asking, and which later I came to understand as the only answer to the question of how she had kept herself safe long enough to have a copy to give.

I sat down and unfolded the document and read it through.

1. Keep her warm, and lying down. Someone stays with her the whole night.
2. Small sips of water, often. Nothing else by mouth for six hours.
3. Count the cloths. Write the number and the hour on the paper by the bed.
4. If she sleeps, wake her each hour and speak to her until she answers you properly.
5. No visitors. No telephone. If anyone asks, she has the influenza.
6. Burn nothing until she is well. Keep and wash.
7. Two cloths in one hour is too many.
8. A fever that will not come down by morning is too long.
   [ 7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why.
     Do not wait for the morning. ]

The comma was there in line one. Line four said *each hour*, which was what I had eventually written on the utility form. Line six said *Keep and wash*, in that order, which was what I had written. The bracket was below line eight, with the text inside it and the closing bracket on its own indented line, and it said *Do not wait for the morning*.

Everything was where I had put it in my head.

I read the bracket a second time. Then I folded the document back the way it had arrived: twice along the horizontal, twice along the vertical, so that the writing was inside and the outside was the plain back of the paper, which is the colour of paper that has been folded and opened many times and handled by several sets of hands. I put it back between pages thirty-four and thirty-five. The water-bird diagram on page thirty-four — some kind of delta bird, a heron perhaps, the caption too small to read without glasses I don't have — looked the same as it always did. I closed the book and put it in my bag.

It would go back to the factory tomorrow. Back in the locker, on the shelf above the coat hooks, where it would stay.

---

I went back to the bathroom and took the ashtray off the windowsill and brought it to the room.

There was nothing in it. I had flushed the ash; I knew that; I had watched it flush. What was in the ashtray was the grey film that lives in an ashtray regardless of cleaning — the residue of cigarettes I had not smoked, a small mark where the tin had rusted at the base. The ash I had made was not in there.

I sat on the cot with the ashtray in my lap and looked at the grey film.

Here is what I had, at this point in the day: the document, between pages thirty-four and thirty-five. The document also in my head, comma and all, *each* not *every*, bracket and not a line nine, with the closing bracket on its own indented line. I had tested myself against the original and the two versions matched. What to do with having established it — what the relationship was between the paper and the fact that the paper was now also in my head, whether having both meant something different than having only one, what I would do when something changed — I had not thought through yet, and I did not think through it then. I was nineteen years old and I had the information and I had no instructions for the next step.

I put the ashtray back on the windowsill where it lived and went back to my room.

I got under the blanket and put my coat back on top of it and lay on my back and looked at the crack in the ceiling, which runs from the light fitting to the wall above Floarea's cot and has been there, as far as I can tell, since the hostel was built. It does not have a shape. I have checked.

The district heating came on again at four in the afternoon, briefly.
