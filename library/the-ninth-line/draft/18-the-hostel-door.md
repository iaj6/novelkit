# 22 November 1984

I was on the top bunk with my shoes on, reading, at half past two in the afternoon, when someone knocked on the hostel door.

Nobody knocks on hostel doors at half past two in the afternoon. Bloc committee women knock in the evenings, when the shift rotation accounts for people being home. Factory management knocks, but they send someone from personnel and they do it in the morning. The woman across the hall knocks when she wants to borrow salt, which she does regularly and which I find endearing rather than irritating, probably because she always returns it in exactly the same quantity — not the salt, a different salt, but the same amount. She was the only person I could think of who had reason to knock on my door at that hour.

I got off the bunk and stood in the middle of the room.

The geography book was not here. The geography book was at the factory, in my locker, between the thermos that didn't seal properly and the spare coat I kept because the November shift started in the dark and ended in the dark and some mornings there was frost in the seams of the window. The locker was metal, key lock, three-digit code I had changed in September to something I hadn't told anyone. The factory was a fifteen-minute walk from the hostel in either direction, and the afternoon shift didn't end for another two hours, and the combination was in my head, not on any piece of paper.

I want to put this on the record because people have asked me about it since: I did not think, in that moment, that the geography book was safe. I thought it was at the factory and I was at the hostel and those were two different places. That was all I had. I stood in the middle of the room and I held those two facts next to each other for the length of time it takes to get off a bunk and stand on the floor.

I did not look through the gap in the door. I opened it.

He was younger than I'd expected. I have been asked: how did you have an expectation, you'd never seen him. Which is true. But it is not how expectation works. The investigation had been going for nineteen days. In nineteen days of working the night shift and standing in the bread queue in the morning and eating in the canteen and saying nothing to anyone about the geography book, you develop a picture. Mine had been wrong about his age. The rest of it — the coat, the manner, the particular way of saying *Tovarășă Marcu* in a hallway — that came out about how I had pictured it.

He had a face that was not going to be remembered for anything in particular. Not unkind. Not especially notable. The face of a man who goes to other people's places and asks questions and writes the answers in a notebook. He wore a dark coat, close-cut, the kind that came from a different shop than factory coats or bloc-committee coats. He said his name and rank. He said he would like to speak with me about a matter.

He said it the way men in his position say it, which is in a way that means: you will come.

I said: I need to get my coat.

My coat was on the hook inside the door. I reached back and took it off the hook and put it on, in the room, facing him, without turning around to look at anything else in the room. I put on the coat and I went with him and the door closed behind me. I did not look back at the room and I did not look back at the stairwell and I did not think about going back to the factory and getting the geography book before we went wherever we were going. I put on my coat and I went.

Not as something I'm proud of and not as something I'm ashamed of. I went, and the geography book stayed in the locker.

---

Outside it was cold and the sky was already going from grey to the pale orange of streetlights coming on. He had a vehicle — a grey Dacia, parked at the kerb in front of the hostel gate with another man in the front seat who did not look at me. I got in the back. The drive to the station took six minutes on the route we took, which went up past the main square and past the department store with one window lit and one dark and the hardware shop closed early. The man in the front seat drove carefully, at the speed of someone who did not want to be noticed. I looked out the window. There was a queue outside the bread shop on the corner of the square — I recognised the angle of bodies, the way people lean slightly inward in a queue on a cold day, using each other as windbreak without making eye contact.

The station had a plain door on Strada Republicii. He showed me in, through a hallway that smelled like the inside of every official building in Dej — linoleum and a heating pipe doing its best — and into a room off the right side of the hallway. The room had a table and two chairs and a window with frosted glass. The frosted glass let in daylight but not anything you could see through. There was a lamp on the table, lit.

On the table, in addition to the lamp, there were four pieces of paper inside clear evidence envelopes. I could see from where I sat that they were paper documents, handwritten, roughly the size of what I had in the locker.

I did not try to read them from the far side of the table. I sat down and I looked at them without moving my head toward them and I put my hands in my lap.

He sat down across from me and set his notebook to the left of the envelopes and asked if I wanted tea. I said no. He said: we have reason to believe you may be in possession of a document that has been circulating in the factory district. He did not name the document. He did not describe it. He asked if I could tell him anything about it.

I said: I don't know what you mean.

He looked at me for a moment in the way of someone who has done this many times and understands that it takes a while for people to get to the place where they can speak to each other about what is actually being spoken about. He wrote something in his notebook. He said: Tovarășă Marcu, a piece of paper. Handwritten. Medical in nature.

I said: I found it.

I want to be clear about what happened in that sentence, because people have asked. I did not decide ahead of time to say those words. They arrived already decided, the way a thing arrives when you have spent nineteen days not letting yourself think about it.

He said: tell me more about that.

I said: I found it on the bus. Between the seats. I was sitting in the third row from the back and it was on the floor, between the seats, and I picked it up. He asked which route. I said the number 4, the morning direction. He asked when. I said last spring, I don't remember the exact month, sometime in April or May. He asked if I had been alone. I said: there were other people on the bus. He said: did anyone see you pick it up. I said: I don't think so. He wrote all of this down with his even handwriting.

He asked: and you kept it.

I said: I kept it because I didn't know what it was and I couldn't throw it away without knowing what it was. I had it in the geography book. I kept it in the geography book because the geography book is a thing I own but never open — it was there from when I was in school and I never gave it back — and I thought it was safer than keeping it in a pocket. That was all. I didn't think about it much after that.

He wrote. He was not rushing.

This is what I said and it was true. Every sentence of it was true, in the way that a set of true sentences can describe a true thing and leave the whole of the true thing sitting in the middle of them, in the space the sentences make around it, untouched. I found it on the bus, on the number 4, in the morning, in spring. I kept it in the geography book. Both of those things happened. The part I did not say — the only part, the single thing I did not say — was that when I picked it up from the seat, I picked it up because the woman sitting across from me, who had a face like a closed shop and had not spoken a word to me, had placed it there. Had put it in my hand first, actually, and I had looked at it for one second, and then she had looked out the window. She got off two stops later and did not look back and I have not seen her since.

That part I did not say. I will not say it.

He reached into the evidence envelopes and slid one toward me across the table, and through the plastic I could see the handwriting. Not just handwriting: my handwriting, the geography book cover, number 7 written in my hand on the front because I had numbered my school books and never bothered to change it. The locker had been opened. I had known the locker could be opened; this was a thing I had known, and had set next to the other thing I knew, and this was the result of that particular arithmetic.

He asked: can you identify this.

I said: yes. That's mine.

He asked how I meant that. I said: my copy. The one I found. He nodded. He picked up the envelope and read from it. He read:

*7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why. Do not wait for the morning.*

He set it back down. He asked: is that how you read that section. The terminal section. Is that what it said.

I said: yes. That's what it says.

He looked at the other envelopes. He moved one a few centimetres toward me — not completely across the table, just enough that I could see the paper inside at an angle. He read from that one:

*If 7 or 8, take her in in the morning.*

He set it back. He looked at me. He said: you can see there's a difference.

I said: yes.

He said: you'd say your copy reads the first version, not the second.

I said: yes. That's what mine says.

I had memorized the whole document, every line of it, including the bracket, in the hostel bathroom five days ago. I knew what the difference was. I knew what it meant. I said yes, and I looked at him, and I did not say anything else about it, because I was nineteen years old in a room with a lamp and frosted glass and I had learned early that in rooms like this you answer what you're asked and you do not volunteer the rest.

He made a note. He said: you understand this document is evidence in a criminal investigation. I said: I understand. He said: we're going to hold you here for further questioning while we clarify a few things. I said: what things. He said: where you got the document.

I said: I told you where I found it.

He said: I know what you told me.

Not unkindly. Without any particular weight on it. He reached for his notebook, opened it to a new page, set his pen in the binding.

I sat back in the chair. The lamp put most of its light down on the evidence envelopes, and the rest of the room was November grey. Through the frosted glass, the street outside was dark now; the night shift had started without me. The geography book was in the locker at the factory, fifteen minutes from this chair, and it would stay there until someone with a key had a reason to go there and open it.

The man across the table from me reached for his pen.
