# 4 December 1984

The administrative wing is dark when she arrives. Seven in the morning, the overhead strip not yet on, the corridor windows still the gray before proper daylight. She can hear the overnight caretaker somewhere at the far end, the sound of the cart's metal wheel against the linoleum. She does not call out to him. She goes directly to Mița's desk and lines the payroll envelopes along the front edge — one per name, alphabetical, sealed and initialed the night before. She counts them as she sets them down. Fourteen. The same as every month.

She does not sign the distribution sheet.

She goes to her own desk. She does not hang her coat. She sets her bag on the floor beside the chair and does not open it. She sits for a moment with her hands flat on the desk and then she opens the bottom shelf of the bookcase and lifts out the 1983 ledger — black cover, her own label starting to lift at the lower right corner, the binding soft from two years on the shelf.

She has produced this ledger on demand three times: the spring audit, the year-end reconciliation, the mill director's signature on the October figures. Each time she has set it on the desk and opened it to the requested page and said nothing while whoever was reading it finished. Each time she has put it back on the shelf afterward and the paper in the spine was still there.

She holds the ledger spine-down over her desk and waits. The paper slides free — a moment of resistance, a small sound, and then it is in her palm. A single sheet, folded in thirds, the creases gone soft from fourteen months in one position.

She sets the ledger back on the shelf, spine straight with the others.

She does not unfold the paper. She folds it once more, the same fold, the same direction, along the same crease, and puts it into her left breast pocket.

She stands. She buttons her coat.

Right coat pocket: N4, where it has been since the second of December.
Left breast pocket: N1, where it has been since October 1983.

She puts on her gloves. She picks up the pen from her desk and sets it parallel to the edge — the way it sits when she has not been in. She turns off the lamp.

She goes out through the side door onto the service road.

---

The cold outside is complete and still — not the cold with wind, but the cold that does not need it. A thin layer of snow from the night before, already dirty along the road edges, pressed hard underfoot and squeaking at every step. The sky is pale yellow to the east, the sun at the rooflines but not above them yet.

She goes around the outer fence to the main avenue and turns toward the market. Strada 22 Decembrie is fifteen minutes at her pace. She does not change her pace.

At the corner of the market, the bread queue is six deep. Ilinca walks by the end of it and does not look at anyone in it.

She passes the post office — the notice board outside with the water-stain across the lower right corner that has been there since September. She passes the pharmacy, grille still down, the window dark. She passes the bloc where Georgeta lives. Third floor, second window from the right — the curtains closed, the light behind them not on. She passes the bloc where Livia Moldovan lives. She does not look up at either of them.

She turns onto Strada 22 Decembrie.

The station is visible from the corner. Stone facade, two stories, the national emblem above the public entrance, an official vehicle parked in the yard to the side. She pushes the door open.

---

Inside: warm air and the smell of floor wax and something underneath it, the particular smell of rooms where forms accumulate over years. A long counter with a frosted glass partition, two stations, only one occupied. The duty officer is young — round-faced, the collar of his uniform one size large for his neck. He looks up from what he is writing.

She says: I need to speak with Lieutenant Munteanu. Regarding the Petrescu matter.

He says: Your name.

She says: Roșca, Ilinca. Mill administration.

He writes this down. He says: Wait there.

There is a bench to her left. Two men are sitting on it, not speaking to each other, both in their fifties, both with the look of people who have been waiting longer than they expected. She stays at the counter. She sets her gloved hands on the counter edge and then takes them off and holds them at her sides.

From behind the partition she can hear a typewriter — three quick strokes, a pause, three more — and then a door somewhere opening. And then a door closing. And then nothing, except the two men on the bench and her own breathing and the sound of a vehicle in the yard outside starting its engine and then going quiet again.

The duty officer comes back through the door at the partition's end. He says: Come through.

---

The corridor beyond is narrower than she expected. Linoleum floor in a color between green and gray, scuffed to nothing along the center. Doors on both sides, most closed. At the far end a window looks out on the courtyard — she can see the official vehicle and the frost still white on the upper course of brickwork on the wall opposite. They go up one short flight of stairs, uncarpeted, and along another corridor. The duty officer stops at the second door on the right. It is open.

---

Munteanu is at his desk. The Petrescu dosar is open in front of him, its cover folded back, and she can see the working pages — forms, notebook sheets, printed headings filled in by hand. Along the left edge of the desk: seven evidence envelopes in a row. She counts them. Seven. She knows the dates they arrived. She knows what each one holds. To the right of the envelopes: his notebook, open to a page of handwriting dense enough that the white of the page is almost gone. At the far corner of the desk, a coffee cup with the coffee dried to a ring at the bottom.

He looks up from the dosar. He looks at her. There is a moment before he says anything. He does not look surprised. He looks at her the way a man looks at a figure that finally matches the column it belongs in.

He says: Tovarășă Roșca?

She says: Yes.

He says: Please, sit down.

The chair across from the desk is wooden and straight-backed, a worn patch on the seat. She sits.

She takes N1 from her left breast pocket, unfolding it as she lifts it free. She takes N4 from her right coat pocket. She sets both documents on the desk in front of him — flat, side by side, N1 to the left, N4 to the right — and removes her hands from both pages.

He looks at them. He does not pick either one up immediately. He reads a few words of each from where he is sitting. Then he picks up N1.

---

1. Keep her warm and lying down. Someone stays with her the whole night.
2. Small sips of water, often. Nothing else by mouth for six hours.
3. Count the cloths. Write the number and the hour on the paper by the bed.
4. If she sleeps, wake her every hour and speak to her until she answers you properly.
5. No visitors. No telephone. If anyone asks, she has the influenza.
6. Burn nothing until she is well. Wash and keep.
7. Two cloths in one hour is too many.
8. A fever that will not come down by morning is too long.
   [ 7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why.
     Do not wait for the morning. ]

He reads it line by line. She watches his eyes track down the page. When he reaches line eight he slows. His eyes go to the bracket below it — the indented lines, the closing bracket — and he reads to the end and holds the page.

He sets N1 down. He picks up N4.

---

1. Keep her warm and lying down. Someone stays with her the whole night.
2. Small sips of water, often. Nothing else by mouth for six hours.
3. Count the cloths. Write the number and the hour on the paper by the bed.
4. If she sleeps, wake her every hour and speak to her until she answers you properly.
5. No visitors. No telephone. If anyone asks, she has the influenza.
6. Burn nothing until she is well. Wash and keep.
7. Two cloths in one hour is too many.
8. A fever that will not come down by morning is too long.
9. 7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why. Do not wait for the morning.

He reads it and then reads it again from line seven. He sets N4 beside N1 and looks at both pages at the same time. His eyes move from one to the other. He looks at the lower half of the left-hand page — the bracket, the indented lines. He looks at the lower half of the right-hand page — the numbered ninth line, flush with the margin.

She does not speak.

He says: Did you make these?

She says: Yes.

He says: Which one first?

She says: The one with the bracket.

He looks at the two documents once more. Then he reaches for the telephone.

He dials the internal line. It rings twice.

He says: This is Munteanu. I need you to release Marcu Doina from the holding section. She can sign for her things and go.

A pause. She can hear the voice at the other end, small and metallic.

He says: Immediately.

He puts the telephone down.

He picks it up again. He dials a different number. A longer wait this time.

He says: Captain. This is Munteanu. The Petrescu matter — I've resolved it. I have the originating party here in my office. He says her name. Her employer. A pause, the captain's voice rising once. He says: A voluntary approach. This morning. Yes. I can have the full documentation to you by end of business. He listens. He says: Understood.

He puts the telephone down.

---

Somewhere along the corridor a door opens and then closes.

He pulls the right-hand drawer open and takes out a blank form — white, the Miliția heading printed across the top — and sets it on the desk beside the evidence envelopes. He picks up his pen.

He writes her name first, last name before first. Then the date. Then her address.

He writes on.

The two documents are still between them on the desk. N1 to the left, N4 to the right. She can read both from where she is sitting. She has read both so many times that the lines run in sequence whether she means them to or not — eight lines and a bracket on the left; nine lines on the right; the same words in both, and the ninth line in a different place, and everything that came from that. She has not moved. Her coat is still buttoned. Her hands are in her lap.

He writes on.

The writing on both documents is hers.

She sits.
