# 7 November 1984

He read the folder in his car with the engine off. The station was behind him; the Someș was somewhere ahead in the fog, down past the junction at Strada Gării, invisible from here. The folder held three items: the hospital's notification form, the death certificate stamped by the district procuror's office, and a handwritten sheet giving the name of the next of kin who had been notified. He read them in that order.

Ana Petrescu, nineteen years old. Admitted to the Spitalul Orășenesc at nineteen hundred hours on the fourth of November. Death recorded at twenty-three hundred and twelve on the same date. Cause of death: complications arising from hemorrhage. The hospital's notification form, submitted to the procuror's office on the morning of the fifth, classified the case as requiring investigation under the applicable decree. The hospital had done what it was required to do. His captain had made that clear, yesterday afternoon, when he had put the folder on Munteanu's desk and remained standing in the doorway: it was not the hospital under examination. What remained to establish was whether a criminal act had occurred, and on whose part.

The assignment had come to him on the afternoon of the sixth as an administrative case — the kind his captain classified as requiring a determination, not an investigation. Look at it, write down what happened, decide whether there is a basis for charges, close the file or pass it up. Munteanu had read the file the previous evening and concluded that these instructions would not cover what the file contained. He had not said this to his captain, because the determination had not been written yet and it was possible he was wrong.

He put the three sheets back into the folder in the order he had found them. He started the car and drove to Strada Gării.

---

The valley fog had not lifted by half past eight. It lay in the basin of the Someș below the road, dense in the low places, thinning only at the height of the bloc roofs. He drove with the window cracked. The market square was empty, the bread queue already dispersed — whatever had been available this morning had been available early. Past the square the road narrowed; the houses on Strada Gării ran close together, each with a small winter garden behind wire or concrete fencing: maize stalks cut low, a few cabbages still in the ground, a cold frame with nothing in it. Number forty-one had a metal gate that was not locked.

He parked and looked at the house for a moment before getting out. A single-storey building, extended at the back, with a kitchen light on behind a curtain not quite closed. He noted the gate, the cracked concrete of the garden path, the fact that the curtain had moved slightly while he was looking at it.

---

She came to the door before he had finished latching the gate. She had been watching from the kitchen window. A woman who knew the Miliția would eventually arrive, watching for when it arrived, was a woman who had been thinking about what to say. That was not deception. That was the preparation a person made when they understood that a conversation had consequences.

Her name was Rodica Petrescu. She was the wife of Ana's eldest brother and had been living in the house since the spring. She was not yet thirty and she had not slept.

He introduced himself: Locotenent Munteanu, Secția de Investigații. He said he was sorry for her loss. He was, in the way that he was always sorry at the beginning of this kind of interview.

She asked him in. The kitchen was small. A pot on the stove, the burner turned low, the smell of onions cooked an hour earlier and still in the air. She sat at the table with her hands in her lap; he sat across from her and took the notebook from the inside pocket of his coat.

He asked her to tell him what had happened from the evening of the third of November onward.

She had been telling it to herself for three days. He could hear it in the quality of the account — not rehearsed, exactly, but worn into a groove by repetition, the way a path is worn by the same feet going back and forth across it. Ana came home ill on the evening of the third; this was not the first time she had been unwell, so Rodica did not immediately understand what kind of ill this was. She stayed with her through the night. She counted the cloths, because the paper told her to, and she sat beside her and brought water in small amounts, because that was also on the paper. By noon on the fourth she understood that what she was counting had crossed a threshold. By the evening she understood that staying home was no longer the decision she was making.

She stopped speaking. He waited.

She said: she did not take her to the hospital until the evening of the fourth because going to the hospital was — she stopped again. She said: because she did not know what to say when she arrived.

He wrote: *did not know what to say at the hospital.*

He asked: was there a piece of paper in the house.

She was very still. Then she said yes, and rose from the table and went into the next room. He waited. The pot hissed against the burner and went quiet. Outside, somewhere on the street, a cart moved across frozen gravel.

She came back with a folded piece of paper and put it on the table between them. It had been kept somewhere specific — she had returned without hesitation, without searching. The paper was folded twice, both folds going in the same direction. He could see handwriting through the sheet where the paper had gone thin at the creases.

He looked at it without touching it. Then he took an evidence envelope from his coat, held it open, and she put the paper into it. He sealed the envelope and held it in his lap.

He asked where the paper had come from.

From her neighbor, she said. A woman in the next bloc on the street. She gave the name. He wrote it.

He asked nothing else about the paper. He wrote the time in the margin of his notebook, wrote his station number on a card, and left the card on the table. She did not speak again. He put his coat on and went out the way he had come.

---

The neighbor's bloc was a hundred meters up the street. He walked slowly enough to look at the other houses: a light on behind frosted glass, a light on in an upstairs room, a dog behind a wire fence that watched him and did not bark. The fog was thinning; he could see to the end of the block now, the bare lindens black against the white-grey sky.

He found the address and rang the bell.

The woman who came to the door wore her kitchen apron and her coat at the same time. She read his identification card for a long moment — she read it properly, not just the top line of it — and stepped back.

The kitchen was slightly warmer than the previous one. A clothesline ran along the back wall with two towels folded over it. She stood near the stove with a dish towel in her hands, which were not still: she moved the towel from one hand to the other, set it on the counter, picked it up again.

He asked for her copy of the paper.

She said: what paper.

He said: the one you gave to Rodica Petrescu, on the street.

She was quiet. She looked at the dish towel and then at him. She put the dish towel on the counter and went to a drawer — second from the left, beneath the counter — and took out a folded piece of paper and brought it to him. Her hands were steady when she placed it in the second evidence envelope. It was the pause before the drawer that had not been steady.

He sealed the envelope. He asked her a few further questions: when she had last spoken with Rodica Petrescu, whether she had discussed Ana Petrescu's illness with anyone in the past three days, whether she had anyone else living in the household. She answered each question separately. He wrote the answers. He did not ask her who had given her the paper, and she did not say.

He thanked her. He left his card on the counter and went back out to the street.

---

He drove to the station and sat at his desk with both envelopes in front of him.

The investigations room held four other desks, only one of them occupied — Sandu, on the far side of the room, writing something in a ledger with his back to the window. The overhead fixture cast yellow light that reached only as far as the middle of the room. Munteanu turned on his lamp. He put his coat on the back of the chair. He set the two evidence envelopes side by side and looked at them for a moment.

He opened the first envelope and laid the paper flat on the right side of the desk. He opened the second envelope and laid the paper flat on the left. He moved the stapler to weight the right-hand sheet and a bottle of correction fluid to weight the left. Then he read them.

Line nine on both copies was the same. He read it on the right-hand sheet, then read it on the left: *If 7 or 8, take her in in the morning.*

He read from the top of each copy. Line one: the same on both. Line two: the same. Line three: he stopped.

On the right-hand sheet — the one Rodica Petrescu had given him, the one that had been in the house during the night of the third — line three read: *Count the cloths. Write the number and the hour on the paper by the bed.*

On the left-hand sheet it read: *Count the cloths. Write the number and the hour on the paper beside the bed.*

He read both sentences again.

The handwriting was different. Not only different hands, but different enough in their letterforms that one person could not account for both. The right-hand script was small and even, the letters placed with some care; the left-hand script was slightly larger, the o's in *cloths* and in *hour* formed differently, opened where the right-hand script closed them. These had not been produced by the same person.

He read lines four through eight on both copies. In line six, the right-hand sheet had a full stop after *well* and the left-hand sheet had a comma — but the word was the same on both. Lines seven and eight were identical between the copies. Line nine was identical: *If 7 or 8, take her in in the morning.*

He held both sheets up toward the lamp. Not to see through them — the lamp was not strong enough for that — but to hold them in proximity, as two things that were related to each other. Then he set them back on the desk.

He had seen forged documents before. He had seen copied documents: a will transcribed in a different hand, a typed letter produced on two machines. In those cases, the second document had tried to be the same as the first, and what was interesting was where it had not succeeded. This was different. Both of these documents had tried to be the same as some third thing — something they had each been copied from — and both had succeeded, and each had arrived at something slightly different from the other. *By.* *Beside.* Neither of them wrong. Neither of them the other.

The variation between *by* and *beside* was what happened when a person read a word and wrote the word that was in their head, and the word in their head was slightly different from the word on the page. That was not an error of copying. That was copying. That was what copying produced, line by line and hand by hand, generation by generation. A word at a time.

And if both of these copies had drifted one step from the same source, then the source existed. And the source had come from somewhere.

He did not say any of this aloud. He did not write it down.

Line nine was the same on both copies: *take her in in the morning.* Nine numbered lines on both sheets, the ninth sitting square in the column with the rest.

He picked up his pen. He pulled the folder toward him and turned to a fresh page at the back, behind the death certificate and the notification form and his notes from the morning. He wrote the neighbor's name at the top of the page. He drew a line under it.

The neighbor was where the chain between his desk and the Petrescu household stopped, at the moment. Behind her name was whoever had given the paper to her. Behind that name was another, and behind that name another still. He did not know yet how many. He did not know yet where the nine-line version of this document had come from, or when, or in whose hand.

He wrote the date in the margin and began.
