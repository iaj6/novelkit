# 29 November 1984

N1 is on the kitchen table.

She put it there an hour ago. She carried it from the mill yesterday in her coat pocket, folded along its crease, and when she came in at half past four she set it on the table and has not touched it since. The district heating has been off since morning. The kitchen is the warmest room because she has been in it; her body holds three or four degrees against stopping, and the forty-watt lamp above the table gives a little more. She has not eaten. She is in the chair nearest the window, which puts the lamp behind her and to her left, throwing her shoulder's shadow across the near edge of the table. The fold of N1 runs along the shadow line.

She gets up. She moves the chair closer to the lamp and sits again and picks up N1 and unfolds it.

The bracket. She drew it in October 1983, at this table, with this lamp, in the time it took her to draw it carefully: the horizontal at the top, the vertical running down the left margin to the full depth of the text below line eight, the horizontal closing at the bottom. The text of the override instruction inside the enclosed space. She put it there because the ninth element is not what comes after line eight. It is what you do instead of continuing — if seven or eight is true, at whatever hour seven or eight becomes true. She put the bracket in the left margin and not in the numbered column because the ninth element has no number. She knew this when she made N1. She made it correctly.

She reads it:

*7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why. Do not wait for the morning.*

She has read it many times. She wrote it. She sets N1 on the table with the lamp directly over it and looks at the bracket in the full light.

---

On the third of November of last year she received a message at her desk in the late afternoon. It arrived through the established channel: a form delivered in person by someone with no reason to come to her office unless she had something to say. Nothing written on the form that was not the form's own text. The message was what was not written: the woman needed the copy that evening, not next week.

Ilinca finished the column she was on. She locked the desk. She went to the second shelf and took down the 1983 ledger and opened the spine and unfolded N1 from where she kept it and put it in her coat pocket. She had a blank sheet. She uncapped the pen to confirm it was full, capped it again, put it in her inside pocket. She told herself: when you are done, check the words.

She went out through the administrative wing and turned left on the street and walked to Strada Crișan.

The coal storage was in the basement of the bloc at the corner. She went in through the street door, down the first staircase, and through a low door at the base with a hook latch. She lifted the latch carefully; the latch made a sound when dropped. She set it back against the frame and pushed the door open and went inside.

Four meters by three, low-ceilinged. Concrete floor. Unpainted brick walls. It smelled of coal in the way a space smells of coal after many years: the mineral density of it living in the brick and the mortar and the concrete, not requiring the coal's presence to persist, the smell the permanent occupant of the room whatever else comes and goes. The cold was sharp, sharper than the street — below-ground cold, undiluted by any building heat, the air having sat here for months at the temperature of the earth around it. There was a wooden crate in the center of the room, set there at some point for some purpose and remaining. No window anywhere. The only source of light was the door.

The door opened below street level. When cracked, it admitted a band of light from the street lamp above, entering at a descending angle — not straight across the room but down and to the right, narrowing as it went. At the door stop the band was six centimeters, maybe seven.

The woman held the door. She stood with her back against it and her body maintaining the angle, her coat against the cold metal, both hands free at her sides. Ilinca could hear her breathing behind her: shallow, steady, the breathing of a person holding a position and waiting. She did not look at her. She set N1 on the crate. She put the blank paper flat beside it. She uncapped the pen.

The light came over her right shoulder and fell across the page from the upper right. The left margin of N1 — where the numerals of the column sat, and where, after line eight, the bracket sat — was in relative shadow. She tilted N1 slightly to bring the margin into the light. She does not remember doing this, but she must have done it, because the numerals were in the left margin and she needed to track them. She was in the procedure of the copy: read a line, hold it, set it down. Read the next.

Lines one through eight. She copied them in order, each in its position, the numeral flush left and the text following. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight. The words of each line as they stood on N1. She did not pause between lines; there was no reason to. She kept pace.

She came to the ninth element.

On N1, the ninth element is in the left margin, on the shadow side of the tilted page. The bracket's left stroke is a vertical line in the margin. It terminates in a horizontal at the top and a horizontal at the bottom. She was reading the left margin for position: where the line begins, what numeral or mark stands there. The light was entering from the upper right at a descending angle. The shadow deepened toward the left edge of the page. The bracket's left stroke, in that light, at that angle, from the angle she was at — she cannot know now, reconstructing, whether she saw the bracket's left stroke for what it was, or whether the top horizontal of the bracket was in the shadow above the light's edge so that what remained visible was the vertical and the bottom horizontal, which together look like a mark with a curve at its base. She cannot know which she saw, or whether her eyes reached the margin at all at that moment, or whether her hand simply moved to the next position in the column without her eyes having given it a signal.

Her pen was at the margin. Eight positions in the column: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight. The column procedure. The ninth position is the next position. The hand moved.

She wrote a 9.

She did not draw the bracket. She wrote a nine in the column, in the position of a numeral, because the column procedure says: the next position gets the next numeral. It was faster than drawing a bracket. It was what the column called for, except that the ninth element is not in the column. She was in the procedure of the column and the ninth element is outside the procedure and she did not stop for the element that was outside it.

She wrote the text after the 9. Every word from N1, in order, nothing dropped. She finished the page. She capped the pen. She checked the words. She counted them along the line and then along the line again. Every word was there. She handed the copy to the woman. The woman put it in her coat and went.

---

She looks at the bracket under the lamp.

She holds N1 up and turns it so the left margin comes into the full light. The bracket is clear: the two horizontals and the one vertical, the interior, the text enclosed inside. She angles the paper slightly to the left, toward the shadow, the way she would have tilted it in the cellar, and looks at the bracket in the reduced light. She can see it. She can also see: at a certain angle, with the top horizontal in the shadow, the left stroke looks like a vertical mark at the column's edge, shorter than a bracket, in the position of a numeral. She can see both. She cannot, from seeing both, recover which one she saw in November of 1983 in the coal cellar. She is not trying to. She already has the confirmed figure.

She can see herself writing the 9. She can see it because the column procedure is a procedure she knows, and because the nine is on the paper, and because the nine on the paper is in her handwriting, and she has read her handwriting on that paper with adequate light in a stairwell on Strada Crișan two days ago and the nine was there. The evidence is the nine.

She wrote the nine because she was in the coal cellar in November, in twenty minutes, in six centimeters of light, with a woman waiting at the door. She checked the words when she was done. She did not check the column. A dropped word is what happens in a hurry; the column procedure does not drop words, it produces numbers, and she did not think to check whether the number it produced was wanted.

---

She folds N1 back to its crease. She lays both hands flat on the paper.

What the nine produces, two hands further along the branch: a woman recopying the document. A careful woman — the handwriting Ilinca heard described, clean and evenly spaced. This woman reads nine numbered lines in sequence. She reads line eight: *A fever that will not come down by morning is too long.* She reads the nine, and after it: the text of the ninth element, which says *7 or 8 — take her in* and then says *Do not wait for the morning.* Nine numbered steps. The ninth step follows the eighth. The eighth step says morning. The ninth step says take her in. A careful woman making nine clean steps from nine numbered steps writes the ninth step in sequence with the eighth, and the morning from line eight is in the air of line nine, and she writes:

*If 7 or 8, take her in in the morning.*

Every word she was given. Nothing wrong in the words; nothing invented; the morning from line eight entering line nine because the ninth step follows the eighth, because that is what it means to be in a numbered sequence.

On N1, there is no morning in the ninth element. The ninth element says *Do not wait for the morning.* It says this because it is not the next step in the sequence; it is the override, which applies at any hour, which is why the bracket is there and why the bracket says what it says. She put the bracket there because she knew the difference. She produced a nine in the coal cellar and the difference disappeared.

She holds what she confirmed on Tuesday:

*9. 7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why. Do not wait for the morning.*

She holds what N1 says beside it, in the margin, in the bracket:

*7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why. Do not wait for the morning.*

The same words. The column is wrong.

---

She counts the entries in the order she can act on them.

N4, the copy she made on Strada Crișan in November of last year: the nine in the column, confirmed two days ago. The woman who carried it from the cellar. That woman's copy, passed further; her sister at the laundry on Strada Mihai; the copy from that copy. The woman who made nine numbered steps and wrote the morning into the ninth. The copy from that copy; one more copy; Ana Petrescu's household.

The fourth of November. She arrived at the hospital in the morning.

Ilinca does not move her hands from the paper.

---

Doina Marcu has been in the holding section at the Miliția station for nine days.

Ilinca has N1. She brought it from the mill yesterday because she had worked through the accounting as far as it could be worked through without being committed to what the accounting required, and when the accounting is complete it requires an action, and she needed to have the document in her hands to make the action real. N4 is with the woman on Strada Crișan; she gave it back on Tuesday because she was not yet ready to take it. She will go back for it. She knows when she will go back.

There is no column for this. A bookkeeper's discipline says: what you cannot enter in a ledger does not mean what you cannot do. It means the record is in a different place. The record is in N1 and N4, which together say what they say without requiring a ledger entry, and in Doina Marcu, nineteen, nine days in the holding section with a copy that Munteanu cannot trace to its source because she will not name the source. And in Ana Petrescu, nineteen, the fourth of November, the district hospital in Dej, nine hours after she should have arrived.

Ilinca has been holding this figure since she read it on Strada Crișan on Tuesday. A confirmed figure does not change when you keep looking at it.

She reaches over and moves the lamp to the window side of the table, so the light falls across N1 from the left, the way the coal cellar light did not fall on it, and the bracket is lit cleanly from the side. The left stroke, the top horizontal, the bottom horizontal. Unmistakable. She looks at it in the good light from a forty-watt bulb at close range, the light she had at this table in October 1983 when she drew it.

She turns off the lamp.

She sits in the dark kitchen with N1 in front of her, the fold line under her fingers, the bracket she can no longer see but does not need to see. Outside, the courtyard. The neighbor's window across the way, its yellow rectangle of light going from the kitchen to the hall and then the hall going dark. No heat in the pipes. The cold coming back into the room from the walls that have been holding it since morning.

She will go to the cellar woman tomorrow. She will ask for N4 back. She will tell her she needs it; she will not tell her why; the woman will understand and give it to her. She will take N1 and N4 and she will carry them to the Miliția station on Strada 22 Decembrie. She will ask for Locotenent Munteanu. She will put both documents on his desk.

It has the quality of a confirmed figure: it will not change.

She sits with her hands on the table and the dark and the cold, and she does not move, and she does not enter it anywhere, and she knows it completely.
