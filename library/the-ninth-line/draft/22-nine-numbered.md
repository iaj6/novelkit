# 27 November 1984

She carries the county finance office form in her coat, folded once at the center. It is the reason for crossing. The finance office is across the river and the form is real and she will go there first.

The Someș is below her before she is halfway across the bridge. She can see it on both sides — wide, dark, running at the pace of water that has been moving without interruption for weeks: slow in the center, a little faster where the current angles against the bridge piers. The fog sits on the surface. The willows on the far bank have lost every leaf and stand bare along the water's edge, their branches the color of wet iron. The cold is sharper over the river than it is in the streets; the fog on the water is a few degrees colder than the air above it, and when she puts a hand on the railing the railing gives her that cold immediately. She does not hold it long. Two men in padded jackets come toward her from the far side and she goes past them without meeting their eyes.

She goes across.

The county finance building is three streets from the bridge landing: the embankment road, then left across the square where the fountain has been drained for November, then right into the street with the prewar buildings, yellow and gray-fronted in the fog. The finance office is on the ground floor of the largest one. She goes in through the heavy double doors.

The corridor inside: long, the floor tiles worn to a gray the same shade in the center of each tile, the color that comes from forty years of feet taking the same line through the same space. Two overhead bulbs on a cord, lit, casting the pale yellowish light that bulbs make when they are forty watts and the ceiling is high. She waits at the counter behind a man in an overcoat who has a question that takes longer than it needs to. When he gathers his papers and goes she presents her form. She says she needs the Q4 materials budget supplement for the AFE, standard two-part version. The woman at the counter opens a metal cabinet — the drawer squeaks once on its runner — and locates the form without difficulty and stamps it once with the date stamp and passes it across. Ilinca thanks her and puts the new form in her coat on top of the old one and goes back out into the fog.

She stands on the pavement. The street is quiet except for a woman with a string bag moving toward the corner and a boy on a bicycle going the other way. She puts her hands in her pockets. She goes left, which is away from the bridge and toward Strada Crișan.

---

The bloc on Strada Crișan is four stories, the lower half surfaced in sand-colored render gone dark at the base from the autumn wet, the upper two floors in unpainted concrete still showing the board-marks from the original pour, a decade of weather having made the texture permanent. The letterboxes by the entrance have two standing open. She enters the code — two-six-four, the three digits she memorized in November of 1983 and has not needed to use since — and the door clicks and she goes in and lets it close behind her.

The stairwell is unlit. She has been here exactly once. She goes up the first flight with her left hand flat on the wall, the plaster cold and slightly rough under her fingers, and the second flight with her hand on the metal rail, which is colder than the plaster. The stairwell holds the smell of the building: boiled vegetable and the faint chemical cut of washing powder from somewhere on the second floor, and the damp that comes up through the concrete floor in winter, and the particular smell of a building's coal heat when the coal heat has not yet reached the stairs. Third floor. She stops on the landing and waits for her eyes to find whatever light there is.

The door on the left. The name-card holder with no card in it, as before. The narrow strip of tape along the base of the door frame where someone has tried to seal a draft. Across the landing, the door opposite has a black scuff mark low on its panel, a boot heel's mark, which she does not remember from before. From the fourth floor, through two floors of concrete and a ceiling, the sound of a radio: a man's voice, the unvarying tempo of the morning news. The heating pipe in the wall gives its low intermittent knock — once, twice, silence — and then is quiet.

She knocks.

---

The woman who opens the door is the same woman. Thirteen months: she may be thinner in the face, or the north-facing window at the end of the landing is giving less light than Ilinca remembers — it is late November and the fog and the morning are making the same gray together. The woman looks at Ilinca. She does not close the door and she does not open it further. She waits.

Ilinca says: I'm sorry to come like this. I need to see the copy I made for you. Just to check something.

The woman's face does not give her a reading. She steps back inside without speaking. Ilinca stays in the corridor. The radio on the fourth floor continues its news. She waits with her hands in her pockets and the cold of the stairwell around her. A door opens somewhere on the floor below — footsteps, the door pulling shut, footsteps going down. The stairwell returns to its quiet.

The woman comes back with a folded piece of paper in her hand. She holds it out.

Ilinca takes it.

---

She unfolds it against the corridor wall, turning so the gray light from the window at the end of the landing falls on the page.

The paper has been in someone's possession for thirteen months and it shows: the fold lines have gone soft, the edges at the creases gone furry where the paper has been bent and straightened and bent again. There is a stain at the lower left corner, pale, oval, that she does not remember making. She reads the handwriting before she reads the text. The descent of the *g* in the first line. The way she makes a compressed *ș*. The way her threes close at the top and her sevens cross themselves through the vertical. Her hand. This is her hand.

She goes through the lines.

Line one: correct. Line two: correct. Line three: correct. Line four: correct. Line five: correct. Line six: correct. Line seven: correct. Line eight: correct.

She goes down the left margin as she reads, tracking the numerals in the column she kept flush from the top: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, each in its position, each the same distance from the left edge. She reaches the ninth position.

`9. 7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why. Do not wait for the morning.`

She reads it once.

She reads it again.

The nine is in the left margin. It is in the column with the other numbers, at the same distance from the left edge, the same height. It is not a bracket. It is not two horizontal strokes and one vertical enclosing text from the margin. On N1, in that space, in the margin to the left of line eight, there is a bracket — *[ 7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why. Do not wait for the morning. ]* — and the text inside it is not line nine. On the paper she is holding, in that space, in that margin, there is a nine.

She folds the paper back along its original crease — the two horizontal folds, then the vertical — and holds it out.

She says: thank you.

The woman takes it. She closes the door.

Ilinca goes back down the stairwell to the street.

---

She is not crying. She does not do that where anyone can see.

She walks to the bridge. The fog on the Someș is the same as when she crossed an hour ago — the same layer sitting on the brown water, the same willows disappearing into it at the shoulders. She goes across without stopping, without taking her hands from her pockets, without looking at the river below. On the far side she turns toward Strada Avântului and keeps walking at the same pace.

She made N4 in the cellar on Strada Crișan on the third of November of 1983. Twenty minutes. The light from the door gap: six, seven centimeters, enough to read by, enough to write by. She was copying eight numbered lines and a bracketed override. She checked the copy once when she was finished. She checked for words, because a dropped word is what happens in a hurry and the words were the thing she was most likely to get wrong. The words were correct. All of them. She handed the copy to the woman and the woman put it in her coat and went. The nine was on the paper when she put it in her coat.

She made the nine. She was the one who made it.

---

A nine is a step in a sequence. A bracket is not a step in a sequence. She chose the bracket deliberately. She put the override instruction in the left margin and drew the bracket around it because the instruction is not what follows from step eight — it is what you do when step seven or step eight is true, before any further steps are needed. She drew it in October of 1983 at her kitchen table, with the lamp on and the paper flat in front of her and the time to get it right. She made it correctly.

In the coal cellar, in November, in twenty minutes, in the light from a door gap, she made a nine.

A person who reads nine numbered steps reads nine steps in a sequence. Step eight says: *a fever that will not come down by morning is too long.* The ninth step follows from the eighth. The ninth step says: take her in. A careful person recopying nine steps in sequence comes to the ninth, which says take her in, and connects it to the eighth, which says morning, and writes *in the morning* because that is what the text before it has said, because she is reading nine numbered steps and making nine clean numbered steps from them, because she is doing the work carefully. She is not careless. She is reading what she has been given and she is making it correct.

On N1, there is no ninth step. On N1, the bracket stands outside the sequence, in the margin, separate from the column. The bracket says: when seven or eight is true, do this instead. Do not wait for the morning.

Nothing on the copy she handed over in the cellar was wrong in the way she checked for. Every word was correct. She counted the words and counted them right and the count came out right and she handed it over. The figure was right. The column was wrong. She did not check the column.

She counts: the third of November, 1983. Four hands from Strada Crișan before the paper reached the woman who made the ninth step tidy. Two more hands before it reached Ana Petrescu's sister-in-law. The fourth of November, 1984. Thirteen months and one day. The nine was there the whole time, in the coal cellar, in the light from the door.

---

She is at her building on Strada Avântului by half past ten.

She stops on the step before going in. The fog has not lifted; it is the same fog it was at eight this morning, the same flat gray on the street in both directions. The lamp at the building entrance is not lit because it is day, or the hour that passes for day in late November here at the bottom of the Someș valley, where the fog comes off the river and fills the space between the buildings and does not leave until the afternoon, if it leaves. Her breath goes out in front of her and the cold takes it. She can hear the woman above her on the fourth floor moving across her kitchen, the particular rhythm of someone doing something routine in a familiar room.

She has not left a confirmed figure wrong since she was twenty-three years old. She found the error on the March payroll in April, on the review, and she corrected it and entered the correction in the margin of the source ledger in red. An error you have confirmed against source is not a provisional figure. A provisional figure is one you have not yet checked. She has now checked.

She has seen it. It is confirmed: there is a nine. There has been a nine since the third of November of 1983, in the coal cellar on Strada Crișan, in the light from a gap in the door.

She puts her hand on the door handle.

The fog is on the street. She opens the door and goes up.
