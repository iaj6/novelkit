# 6 November 1984

She pulls the November stat de plată from the in-tray and sets it flat on the desk to the left of the adding machine. Forty-seven lines. There are forty-seven workers on the administrative and technical rolls, each of them a column: name, labour category, days worked, deductions, net. She starts at line one.

The adding machine is a Felix 5010, original equipment from 1972, which she has run every working day for twelve years. It adds and subtracts; it keeps a running total on a paper strip and rings a note — more of a click than a ring, though she calls it a ring — when a column clears. She runs column one. It is clean. She tears the strip at the perforation, clips it to the form, and moves to column two.

The window behind her left shoulder gives onto the administrative courtyard. The heat pipe will not come on until after six in the evening, which is the November arrangement on the shared pipe, and the office in the morning is the temperature of the stairwell, which is approximately the temperature of outside. She has dressed for this since October: wool underneath, the heavy coat she keeps on until ten. She runs column three.

She turns to look at the courtyard window twice in the hour: once when a truck from the transportation depot drives in, and once when it drives out. Both times there is nothing that needs seeing. The sky over the courtyard is the grey that will hold all day.

By nine she has worked through line twenty-eight. The materials consumption report for October is in the out-tray, waiting for the floor section chief's signature before she can send it to the district financial office. She does this every first week of the month; having a reason to be on the production corridor is something she is in the habit of accounting for. She finishes through line thirty-one, takes the materials form from the out-tray, and puts her coat on.

---

The passage between the administrative building and the production hall is covered but open at the base, and in November the cold collects along the corridor floor. She reported the gap at the bottom of the connecting door to maintenance in October 1979 and nothing was done. She walks through it and into the production corridor.

The loom floor is to her left, behind the baffle wall. The sound it makes is not quite a sound — it is a vibration below the range of what she identifies as noise, which she stopped distinguishing from silence after the first year. The smell is the synthetic-fiber smell: wet chemistry and steam heat, the exhaust of the wet-spinning process mixed with cold air through every unsealed gap. It has been in the walls of this corridor since 1974 and she stopped registering it years ago. What she registers now is the cold through the soles of her shoes.

The floor section chief's office is the third door on the right. She knocks once and goes in. He is on the telephone — receiver in one hand, a pen in the other, his eyes on the wall. She puts the materials form on the desk in front of him. He signs it without looking at it; she has had it typed cleanly. He does not stop talking. She goes back into the corridor.

Three women from the day shift are near the locker-bay door at the far end. She knows two of them by their coats. She does not turn toward them.

Georgeta Marin, the production supervisor for Hall B, is moving toward the women's bathroom at the far end of the corridor. She walks at the particular pace she uses when she has somewhere to be before the shift bell — not hurried, not slow. She has a ring of keys on a lanyard around her left wrist, tucked into her palm so they do not sound against each other. Three minutes to the shift change, perhaps less.

Ilinca walks to the bathroom.

---

One working bulb in the overhead fixture. Four sinks along the left wall; four stalls, two with latches — one of which has been difficult to close since August. The window above the sinks is painted shut, looking onto the outer wall of the fiber drying unit close enough to touch. The drain smell is more present in November.

Georgeta is at the far sink. Ilinca takes the one next to her and turns the tap.

"I've been thinking about the influenza," Ilinca says. "Whether it's going around. One of the women on the floor was ill last week."

Georgeta does not look up. Her hands are under the tap; she is washing them left over right, carefully.

"I had mine last year," she says. "I gave it to my neighbor on the street. Livia. No one else."

Ilinca runs cold water over her left wrist, then her right.

"And Livia?" she says.

"She's kept it to herself. She's a private woman — she doesn't pass things on. That's just her character."

The water is cold. Ilinca turns the tap off. In the mirror: the painted-shut window behind them, the single bulb above, Georgeta's keys in her palm.

"The influenza passes," Ilinca says.

"It does," Georgeta says, and goes.

---

Ilinca dries her hands and goes back up the corridor, through the connecting door, to her office. She hangs her coat. She sits.

Two pieces of information. She runs them in the order she can act on.

The first: Georgeta holds what Ilinca gave her, and it has not moved. Ilinca has known Georgeta Marin for eleven years — from before the supervisor promotion, from a period when the section rosters put them in proximity and she learned what Georgeta's silences mean and what her assurances mean.

The second: Georgeta gave one copy to her neighbor Livia, on her street, who has not passed it further. Ilinca has seen her perhaps three times in eleven years — grey coming into her hair before it should, a manner of walking with her chin slightly forward. Livia's family name Ilinca does not know by heart. She could find it in the bloc registry at the street committee. She will not need to. She will not look.

Two nodes on this branch: Georgeta, Livia. She runs every name she can place in the Petrescu household, and every name she can place on Georgeta's street, and the two sets do not intersect. The branch does not reach Ana Petrescu.

She thinks of the fourth line, the way she wrote it for Georgeta: *If she sleeps, wake her every hour and speak to her until she answers you properly.* The copy she made for Georgeta — the third week of November last year, at this desk, by this lamp, in the blue-black ink she uses for fair copies — she can see the page as clearly as if it were in front of her. Line four. Numbered. In its place. The upstroke of the y in *properly*, where the nib caught slightly at the end of the word.

The branch is clean.

She enters both nodes into the account she carries in her head, which has no ledger and no supporting document and which she has maintained for thirteen months by discipline alone. The count is two.

She returns to line thirty-two.

---

Elena Florescu holds the other copy. Third shift now; when Ilinca first knew her they were both on the morning schedule, eleven years ago, when the roster rotation put them in the same section for two seasons. Since 1979 their paths cross in two ways: at the bakery on Strada Lungă, which Elena passes on her way to the morning handover and which Ilinca passes on her way home on certain days; and twice a year at the bloc committee office when the identity documents come up for renewal. Ilinca has counted fourteen exchanges at the bakery in three years. This is not friendship; it is adjacency, which is a more reliable thing.

Elena Florescu is not someone Ilinca knows — what she knows of her is procedural: the schedule she keeps, the coat she wears in November, the way she holds her bag on the same shoulder always.

She cannot go to Elena today. Elena is on the floor, on the machine, and a visit from the contabil-șef without an administrative basis is a visit that anyone nearby can note. The bakery is the correct approach. Elena passes it on Friday mornings at half past five on her way to the handover. Ilinca passes it at that hour only when she has reason to, which she now has. She will be there on Friday.

She does not write any of this down.

---

The stat de plată is finished at quarter past three. She tears the final strip, clips it to the summary line, puts the folder in the out-tray with a rubber band. The next item in the in-tray is a letter from the district financial office about the Q4 consumption norms. She reads it, makes a note in the margin, and puts it in the pending tray.

At four-fifteen the door opens.

A boy she does not know — not older than twenty; she has not seen him in the building before, which means he started recently. He is carrying a folder from the cadres office: she reads the stamp before he speaks. He sets a form on the edge of her desk.

"From the cadres office," he says. "Supplementary materials-usage calculation, Q3. Needs to be back by the fifteenth."

She takes it. "Sign the receipt book."

He finds it in his folder. She signs the line. He closes the folder and goes. She listens: footsteps in the corridor, the connecting door, then nothing.

She holds the form.

It is a Ministry of Finance series form. Q3 is July through September, which closed in October. She submitted the standard materials-usage calculation on the twenty-second of October; the district financial office stamped it received on the twenty-fifth. She ran the numbers four times before submitting because the supplementary ministerial instruction from August had adjusted the Q3 norms mid-quarter, and she wanted the adjustment applied consistently through every line item. It was. If there is a discrepancy in the Q3 accounts, it is not here.

She turns the form over. The back is blank. The watermark is in the lower left corner, the serial number in the upper right, where it belongs. She received a supplementary form for a closed quarter once before, in 1981, during the audit year — but that year all enterprises in the district received supplementary requests for everything, and those circumstances were general and known. This is not that.

She is holding the form and she has not decided what to do with it yet.

In fifteen years she has learned that most things which appear to be something are nothing. She has also learned that a thing which is something looks, from the outside, exactly like the things which are nothing. A form that arrives because a number was transposed two months ago in the district office looks like a form that arrives because someone wants to observe what a person does when a form arrives. She knows what a test looks like. She has not been given one she could verify before, which means she has no way to compare now.

The shift bell sounds in the production hall — muffled through the baffle wall, but she knows the pitch of it, the duration; she has known it for fifteen years and this is it. Footsteps in the corridor. Voices, the brief noise of the shift change, and then the corridor going quiet again under the machines.

She has not moved. The form is in her hand.
