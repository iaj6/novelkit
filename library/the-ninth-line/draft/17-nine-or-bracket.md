# 21 November 1984

She has not touched the November overtime summary. It sits on the right side of her desk, a pencil line at the third row where she stopped before eight. The adding machine has paper still rolled in it from yesterday's last column.

The administrative wing does not have heat until after six in the evening. She works in her coat. The sound from the weaving hall comes through the partition wall at a regular pitch — not a noise now, after fifteen years, just the condition of mornings.

She went to the bread queue at six-fifteen. She goes every Thursday unless the shift has changed and the queue has moved. Niculina Hora goes every Thursday too; Ilinca has known this for two years without making use of it until this morning.

Niculina Hora lives in the bloc on the corner of Avântului and Livezilor. Her husband was at the fiber plant before the accident. She has a knee that predicts weather and a daughter who lives near the Strada Mihai laundry. Ilinca has spoken to her for perhaps fifteen minutes in total over two years: mostly queue business, once the knee, once the heating fault in Ilinca's bloc and how long the building superintendent took to file the report.

She had said: there's a woman near the Strada Mihai laundry with a little boy, in the second or third bloc on the left side. Does she go to church?

Niculina had said: same church as my daughter. Why?

Ilinca had said: a friend is looking for a paper she lent out in the spring. A recipe, something small. She thinks it might have gone that direction.

Niculina had looked at her for two seconds. A measuring look, not a hostile one. Then she said: I can ask.

The overtime summary has not moved since. She has been at the desk since eight, with the ledger forms to her left and the adding machine paper curling out of the platen, and the pencil line at the third row of the overtime summary, and the column of figures she has not started. The weaving hall runs its regular pitch through the wall. She does the payroll forms for the second-shift bonus allocation instead. She does them correctly. She puts them in the outgoing tray.

---

At eleven minutes past one, Niculina appeared at the door of the administrative wing. She does not work at the mill. The lunch queue runs outside and people step in from the cold for reasons; nobody asked. She said: my daughter says yes. To a woman from their church — she comes from near the market, they don't know her well — she gave it. One thing. In October.

Ilinca said: thank you. That's exactly what I needed.

Niculina said: I hope your friend finds her paper.

She left. Ilinca ate lunch at her desk. The thermos of tea had gone cold at six-fifteen and she had not thought to reheat it.

---

She runs the count in the columns she keeps only in her head.

N2: two nodes. Georgeta and Livia on the Eminescu side. Both clean. Branch confirmed and closed.

N3: Elena on Crângului, and the fourth-generation woman in Elena's bloc she identified three days ago, and the G3 intermediate she has not traced directly but knows exists somewhere between them. Three nodes. All of it clean; the bracket where the bracket should be.

N4: the copy she made in the cellar. And below it, three she has traced working downward from the cellar woman: the sister at the Strada Mihai laundry. The bloc neighbor with the child. Now the church woman, near the market, no name.

Three nodes below the copy she has not looked at since the night she made it. None of them verified for the format of line nine.

There is also the arm she did not trace — the branch through Viorica Damian, which Munteanu reached before her. She knows from the mill what those copies say in line nine. Numbered ninth line. Morning. Both of them.

Every branch she has traced that is not N4 is clean. Every copy she has heard of with the mutant wording is on N4's branch.

She has now used every indirect route she has. She has worked outward through N2 and N3, downward through the branches she reached first. She has mapped nine nodes. She has not turned back toward Dorobanților.

She adds the church woman and makes the count nine. She opens the overtime summary and draws a line through the third row and starts the column again.

---

The stairwell in her bloc is dark when she gets home. The light goes off at five-thirty; it is quarter past six. She counts the stairs by memory: seventeen to the first landing, eleven to the second, thirteen to the third floor. Her right hand finds the banister before she reaches for it.

Inside, she lights the lamp. Forty watts. She hangs her coat and fills the kettle and sets it on the ring. She eats bread and the last of the dripping from the tin in the window alcove — she has been meaning to render more dripping for a week and has not done it — and she washes the plate and puts it away.

Then she takes the ledger down from the second shelf.

---

Four hundred pages. Ministry of Finance format. The spine retaped with brown tape in February of this year, after the binding cracked. Second shelf, between the quarterly labour-plan compliance records and the annual balance submission. She sets it on the table and does not open it; she puts her fingers inside the back cover and finds the paper in the spine by feel. Folded twice. The fold has gone furry at the crease — it has been wet once and dried badly, the paper giving at the corners. She unfolds it flat on the table under the lamp.

She reads from line seven.

```
7. Two cloths in one hour is too many.
8. A fever that will not come down by morning is too long.
   [ 7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why.
     Do not wait for the morning. ]
```

The bracket. Two short horizontal strokes and one vertical, drawn in ink at the left margin below line eight. She drew it in October 1983, at this table, by this lamp. Lines one through eight were a sequence: you did each one after the previous, for as long as you needed to. The ninth element was not a step in that sequence. It was a condition that answered a different question entirely: when to stop doing the previous eight and do something else. She drew a bracket because a bracket sits outside the list. It is not the next thing. It is the thing that says: if you are here, stop counting steps and do this instead.

The ink is slightly heavy at the base of the vertical stroke. She used the wide-nib metal pen she kept for ruling that month, not the narrow-nib; the narrow-nib was empty. The wide nib put down more ink at the base of each downstroke.

She holds the paper closer to the lamp and looks at the bracket.

Two strokes she can see clearly: the top horizontal and the vertical descending from it. The lower horizontal — the stroke that makes a bracket a bracket and not a descending line — at the base. In this light, in the circle of forty watts, all three strokes are distinct.

She tilts the paper away from the lamp until the lower horizontal goes into shadow.

She tries to bring up the cellar copy. She can see the workbench. She can see lines one and two in the strip of light from the door. She can see line eight and the way the light fell on the right side of the page, on the open field of the lines. She can see herself reaching the bottom of the numbered section.

She cannot see what she wrote below line eight. She cannot see whether the shape at the left margin, in that light, was a bracket or a nine.

---

Third of November 1983. She left the mill at half five and went directly to Dorobanților. She had not arranged it in advance; the woman came to her at the end of the shift with a particular brevity, and Ilinca had said: two hours. She went back for the master at six, when the administrative wing was empty, and carried it to Dorobanților inside her coat.

The cellar was on the ground floor off the interior courtyard. Coal and old timber; the smell was wet dust and something alkaline she had never identified. She pushed the door open two centimeters to check the courtyard — empty — then held it at six or seven centimeters to give herself a strip of light on the workbench. The workbench ran along the left wall. The light from the door fell across it from the right, across the open field of the page.

She placed the master flat and angled it clockwise until the numbers on the left margin came into the better part of the light. She wrote fast; she could hear the woman's shoes at the top of the cellar stairs, the weight-shifting of someone trying not to be impatient. Twenty minutes. She checked each line in turn when she finished: the words against what she had written. She was checking for presence. She was not asking herself whether the format had been preserved — a bracket or a numbered step, a margin notation or a sequence item. She read the words. The words were correct. She folded it and went up the stairs.

What was in the dimmer half of that strip of light: the left margin. The indented part. Where the bracket was.

She looks at the bracket now.

In that light, at that angle: the top horizontal stroke would catch the edge of the light coming through the door. The vertical would drop away from it into the darker part of the strip. The lower horizontal stroke, at the base — the third stroke, the one she cannot make out when she tilts the paper away from the lamp — would be in the worst of the light. The corner of the page that the door crack could not reach.

The top horizontal and the descending vertical, without the lower bar.

She has just shown herself what that looks like.

She made one check.

---

She folds the master along the furred crease. She puts it back in the ledger spine, back cover, where her fingers found it. She carries the ledger to the second shelf and sets it between the labour-plan records and the annual balance submission, where it has to be findable.

The lamp is on. She sits at the kitchen table.

She has spent seventeen days tracing N2 and N3 and the downward branches of N4, working outward and down from every node she could reach without going back to where she started. She has not been to Dorobanților since the night of the third of November 1983.

He has the address on Dorobanților in his notebook — she knows this from the bread queue, third-hand through people who know Rodica Moga's corridor neighbors, in the way that anything on Dorobanților eventually comes back around. He has not gone there yet. One node above the cellar copy. In the coming days. That was how it had stood for a week.

In the coming days.

The lamp is on. The heat is not. Outside the window, the fog from the river has moved onto the street; she can see it at the edge of the glass. The bracket on N1 is a bracket: she drew it in this light, at this table, in October 1983.

What she wrote in the cellar in twenty minutes by a strip of light from a cracked door: she does not know. She has shown herself this evening, with a forty-watt lamp and a tilted page, what the answer probably is.

She is not going to go to Dorobanților tonight. She is going to hold what she probably knows for a little longer. She does not get up.
