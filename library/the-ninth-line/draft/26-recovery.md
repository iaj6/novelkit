# 2 December 1984

She takes the coat from the hook and pulls it on.

At the hallway window, before she buttons it, she stops. The glass is clear — no condensation, which means the temperature last night fell below what the shared pipe requires before the boiler bothers. Through the glass and past the northern roofline she can see the river: brown, flat, moving, the surface catching nothing of the pale early light. She watches it for a moment and then buttons the coat from the bottom up. Five buttons. She has known the bottom three by feel for eleven years; they are slightly smaller than the upper two and require less hand. She takes her gloves from the shelf beside the lamp. She takes the key.

The stairwell is dark. The third-floor light does not come on in the mornings; this is the building superintendent's arrangement and it has been his arrangement for as long as she has lived here. She goes down by the wall, her right hand on the railing, the key in her left.

Outside: frost on the first-floor sill, a white line along the bottom of the glass, already softening at its edges. The air is below zero and dry. The sky is pale and without clouds — the kind of December clarity that follows a hard night and will not last past midmorning, when the valley's moisture finds its way back in. She has not seen a clear sky in Dej since before the fifteenth of November. She walks toward the bridge.

She is going to say, when the door opens: *I need to take the copy back. It is not safe for you to have it anymore.*

She has had this sentence since Thursday morning, worked out on the walk to the mill while the street lamps were still on. She has not revised it because there is nothing in it to revise. Ilinca is going to Strada Crișan to retrieve the copy.

The bridge is fifteen minutes from her building. On the approach, the Someș is below and to the left — running full and brown in the clear morning, carrying what the plateau has sent down to it. At the center span, the east railing has been unsettled since at least the spring of 1977. She knows precisely where the intact section resumes. She walks in the center of the lane until she reaches it.

She has not been on this bridge twice in eight days for eleven years.

The last time was November 1973: three crossings in four days with consolidated labor tallies for the district planning office, each one logged on the mill's courier-receipt form. She counts what she can count. She is making her second crossing in eight days.

On the far side she turns west. The river is behind her. She passes the coal depot — iron doors padlocked, a permanent line of fine coal dust at the base of the wall where the wind has carried it. Then the corner bakery, its window fogged, a short queue visible inside: two women with net bags who glance at her as she passes and then look away. She does not know them. She does not know whether they know her, which is a different question and one she cannot answer.

She passes the pharmacy, its window dark at this hour. Past the pharmacy the street widens slightly — the bloc on the left set back from the pavement by a strip of bare ground where a hedge was, no longer — and then narrows again at the entry to the row. She walks on the left, near the wall, which is her habit on streets she does not know well. She knows this one better now than she did eight days ago. She knows bloc eighteen's entry: the latch, the slight resistance of the door, the smell of the stairwell — damp plaster and the cold that an enclosure receives last and holds longest. She goes up. On the second landing there is a folded baby carriage against the wall, the same one or one like it from the visit on the twenty-second. She does not stop. She goes up.

---

The third-floor landing. The water stain above the door frame — old and brown, shaped like nothing — and the patch of darker linoleum, and the draft from the stairwell window that she felt on the last visit and feels again now. She knocks.

The woman who answers is wearing the gray cardigan she wore in October, or one like it. She holds the door at the width she held it before: enough for a face. She looks at Ilinca for a moment without speaking.

Ilinca says: I need to take the copy back. It is not safe for you to have it anymore.

The woman says: a moment. She lets the door fall almost closed.

Inside: footsteps, unhurried. A drawer opening. A pause — the pause of a person who knows where the thing is and is retrieving it, not the pause of a person who is deciding what to do with it. The sound of paper against paper. The drawer closing.

Ilinca stands on the landing and looks at the water stain. It is old and the edges are dry and the center is slightly darker, which means the seep behind the plaster has not resolved itself. She has managed accounts with a discrepancy like this: the surface figure stable for months, the slow wet thing behind it unreported and accumulating. She has managed them by going into the column behind the surface and finding the entry that did not make it out. She is not thinking about accounts. She is waiting.

The door opens. The woman extends one hand through the gap.

N4 is folded twice: once along the long axis, once across. A single sheet. Ilinca takes it and puts it in the right pocket of her coat, flat against the lining. She feels the paper settle against her side — the weight of one sheet, which is less weight than almost anything.

She says: Thank you.

She says: Do not mention this visit to anyone.

The woman says: I know.

The door closes.

When someone comes to this landing — and someone will — the woman will have nothing to produce. She goes down by the wall and out.

---

The wind comes off the river on the return. She walks into it for the first hundred meters, keeping her hands in her coat pockets — left in the left, right in the right, fingers resting on N4 through the lining. One sheet of paper. She does not need to unfold it.

At the center of the bridge she stops.

Below: the Someș, brown and flat and wide, running without urgency in the cold morning. Twenty meters out, where the current presses against something below the surface and then rejoins itself, there is a seam — a long still line in the moving water. She has crossed this bridge more times than she has counted and she has looked at that seam before without stopping. Today she stops. Her breath is visible in the cold air. The railing under her right hand is rough with frost and she can feel the texture of it through her glove.

N4 is in her right pocket.

N1 is in the spine of the 1983 ledger. Second shelf of the mill's administrative wing, between the quarterly labour plan compliance records and the annual balance submission. She has opened that ledger three hundred times in the past year, or something near it. She knows the binding by touch — the brown tape she put on in February when the original cracked. Tomorrow morning at seven she will open it again. She will take N1 from the spine. She will fold it: once along the long axis, once across. She will put it in the left pocket.

She has both documents. She has had them both since she walked off this bridge twenty minutes ago.

Doina Marcu has been in the holding section for ten days.

December the fifth is three days from today.

She holds these in the column she keeps for things that cannot go on paper. Confirmed figures. Counting them again does not change them. She has counted them.

*9. 7 or 8 — take her in.*

She knows the rest. She has known it since the twenty-second of November, when she stood in the cellar woman's corridor and read the nine where the bracket should have been. She knows what the nine produced, two hands further down the branch: a careful woman reading nine numbered steps in a column put the ninth in sequence with the eighth, and the eighth spoke of morning, and the ninth inherited it. She knows what will happen when she lays both documents on the desk at Strada 22 Decembrie. The bracket and the nine. Two things she made, provable as hers by the paper and the hand and everything a document is. The distance between them is twenty minutes in a coal cellar on a November night in 1983. The distance between that night and the fourth of November 1984 is twelve months and four women's hands. She has run this arithmetic. She knows what the figure is.

She does not think about what happens after the desk.

She takes her right hand from the railing — frost on her glove where it touched the iron — and walks.

---

Her apartment in the evening is the same temperature it was this morning. Cold enough that she does not take the coat off immediately on arrival; she stands in the hallway for a moment, looking at nothing in particular, and then hangs it on the hook. She does not take N4 from the pocket.

She makes supper: bread, the end of the hard cheese from earlier in the week, water heated for tea. She eats at the kitchen table with the lamp on and the window showing the street lamp at the corner of Strada Avântului and the dark sky beyond it. The sky is still clear. Darker now, fully dark, and when she looks past the northern roofline she can see the river has gone black in the night. There will be frost again. She does not write this down.

Tomorrow: the mill at seven. She has decided. She holds the decision the way she holds everything that cannot go in a column: in the part of the account that is not written down, exact, permanent, requiring nothing further.

She rinses the cup and the plate and sets them beside the sink.

She turns off the kitchen lamp.

In the dark hallway, the coat is on the hook. The right pocket holds a single folded sheet. In the other room the lamp is still on, making a thin line of yellow light under the door.

She goes toward it and then turns off that lamp too.

The coat hangs in the dark. The right pocket holds a single folded sheet. Outside the window the clear sky turns colder and the frost comes down and the river goes on moving in the dark.
