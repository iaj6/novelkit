# 5 December 1984

The officer who gave me back my coat was not one I'd seen in twelve days of faces, which was the first good thing about the morning. He set the items on the counter in a row: the coat, my buletin, and the bread roll that had been in my coat pocket when they brought me in, which was hard now and slightly flattened from eleven days in an evidence drawer. He put it on the counter with the same procedural neutrality as the buletin and pushed a form across for me to sign. I signed it. It said I was released following the determination of the case, which I knew was what the form would say because it was the only thing that would produce a morning where an officer I didn't know returned my coat instead of an officer I did know collecting me for a fourth session in the second-floor room.

A bread roll in an evidence drawer.

I asked once where my geography book was. Not hopefully — I had worked out, on approximately the fourth day, that the geography book was not coming back, and I had made a kind of peace with that, or at least the functional version of peace that you make with a thing you cannot change while you still have other things to think about. The officer checked a printed list. He said it was retained in evidence. I nodded and picked up the coat.

I put the buletin in my coat pocket and the bread in the other and I walked out through the public entrance. The duty officer at the front desk did not look up. The door was heavier than I remembered, or possibly I was lighter; twelve days of holding-section food takes something from you that you notice most when you have to push a door.

Outside it was cold and barely light. Dej at six in the morning in early December is the idea of a town rather than the actual one — the shapes of blocs in the gray, the outlines of streets, everything the same color as the sky with only texture to tell them apart. I stood on the step and worked out what day it was. I had gone in on a Thursday — I knew it was a Thursday because it was a shift day and I was supposed to be at the mill at six in the evening, and Munteanu had knocked on my hostel door at half past four in the afternoon, which had given me an hour and a half to not go anywhere. Twelve nights from Thursday was Tuesday. I was willing to accept some uncertainty in that count, because some of the nights in the holding section had been long enough to have contained two separate nights inside them — not literally, but in the way that a night without sleep and with nothing to do but recite something to yourself in the dark stretches until it does not resemble a single unit of time anymore.

Tuesday, I decided. Approximately.

I ate the bread standing on the step. It was better than nothing — that is the most accurate thing I can say about the bread, and I have spent some time since trying to arrive at a more nuanced assessment and I cannot manage one. It was cold and hard and stale in a way that suggested it had never been particularly warm, and it was breakfast. When it was finished I was still on the step because I had not decided where to go.

The Someș was two streets over. I could hear the particular quality of quiet that a wide river makes when there is December fog sitting on it — flatter than ordinary quiet, slightly muffled, as if the river has drawn something heavy across itself and is no longer available. I had been inside for twelve days. The fog in the Someș valley had been happening for twelve days without my participation and it had managed fine.

The geography book was in an evidence room somewhere in the building behind me, entered in a register, sealed in an envelope, labelled. The piece of paper that had been inside it for eleven months was in the same room, in a different envelope or possibly the same one — I did not know the procedure for that level of specificity. Either way, both of them were now state property, which meant they were in a place where they would be kept safe and used for nothing. I thought about the state holding that piece of paper and I thought: good luck. You have it. You will file it. You will never know what it is.

Something had determined the case. The form told me that much. I had a theory about what that something was — who it had been, what they had done, what it had cost them — and the theory was detailed enough by the end of twelve days in the second-floor holding section that I am going to leave it here as a theory. Some things I was still not writing down and this was one of them. What I knew was that I was out, and that someone had made that possible, and that I would know more eventually or I would not.

I tested myself.

Line one. I said it quietly, to the empty street in front of me, to the fog that was beginning to move in from the direction of the river.

*Keep her warm, and lying down. Someone stays with her the whole night.*

Line two.

*Small sips of water, often. Nothing else by mouth for six hours.*

Line three.

*Count the cloths. Write the number and the hour on the paper by the bed.*

Line four. I started to say *every*. This had been the fault in my memorization from the first time I had tried to recall it — a groove cut into the wrong word, a word-shaped rut that my memory ran into before my attention could prevent it. I had corrected it every time I had gone through the document in the hostel room in November, and on the hostel bathroom floor with the test copy burning in the sink, and on the nights in the holding section when there was nothing else to do with the dark. I corrected it now.

*If she sleeps, wake her each hour and speak to her until she answers you properly.*

Line five.

*No visitors. No telephone. If anyone asks, she has the influenza.*

Line six.

*Burn nothing until she is well. Keep and wash.*

Line seven.

*Two cloths in one hour is too many.*

Line eight.

*A fever that will not come down by morning is too long.*

I stopped there. A pigeon on the cornice of the building across the street watched me with the comprehensive indifference of pigeons. There is no way to draw a bracket in memory. What you have instead is the space where the bracket was: the pause before the thing that overrides the sequence, the one that says the eight steps end here and here is what supersedes them. I had read it right. I had said it right in the hostel room in November when I had first gone through the whole document without the paper, and on the bathroom floor when I burned the test copy and checked it against the original and found the bracket still there, and on the nights in the second-floor room when the document was the thing I held against the dark and the dark didn't win.

I said it quietly, on the step, to the street.

*Seven or eight — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why. Do not wait for the morning.*

The pigeon left. I went down the steps.

---

The fog was on the Someș when I got to the bank — white and thick, sitting on the surface of the brown water so that the river looked like it went on in both directions indefinitely without going anywhere. The water was running fast from the autumn rains and I could hear it more clearly than I could see it. There was no proper path along the bank, just ground that had been walked flat over enough years to stay flat. I walked along it with my hands in my coat pockets and the fog around me on every side.

I was not going to try to find the woman on the bus.

I want to be clear about this because it has come up since, in various forms, from various people who have heard different versions of the story. I did not know her name. I did not know her stop or where she was going or what she normally did on the number four bus in the morning direction. She had put the paper in my hand the way you pass someone the salt — before I could think of a reason to say no, which I suspect was not accidental, which I suspect was the technique of someone who had done this kind of transaction before and knew that the window for consent is narrower than the window for refusal. I had not said her name in eleven months of having the paper. I had not said it in twelve days of Munteanu asking me questions across the table in the second-floor room, in three sessions across twelve days, the third of which lasted long enough that I lost track of whether it was still the same day. I was not going to say it now, on the Someș bank in the fog, because I was cold and out and the bread was finished and the geography book was in an evidence room. The not-knowing was the correct version of not-knowing. She was safe or she wasn't, and I would go on not-knowing, and that was not going to change because the case was determined and I was standing on the flat ground by the river.

I thought about Ana Petrescu. Not for very long. I find I can hold that thought for a certain stretch before it tips into something I can't do anything useful with, and I try to keep it to the useful part. I didn't know her when she was alive. What I knew about her was what I'd known since November — her name, her house on Strada Someșului, her sister-in-law's account, the time she arrived at the district hospital, the time she did not come out. She was nineteen years old. I kept coming back to that, the way you come back to a number that doesn't add up — nineteen, the same as me, which is not a meaningful coincidence and I know that, it is just the number I could not set aside. She was nineteen and the paper said *in the morning* and she died in the morning, and the paper said *in the morning* because somewhere along the chain of hands through which it had traveled, someone had read a bracket as a ninth step in the sequence and written it down that way. Nobody in that chain had been careless. A girl had died who should not have.

The fog was around me and the water was somewhere to my right, present as sound and smell rather than sight — the brown-water smell of the Someș in December, the cold that comes off a river moving fast in early winter. The far bank was somewhere I had to take on faith. The bloc-tops behind me on my side of the river went in and out of the white as I walked.

I turned around when the concrete apron ended and the bank became proper mud. I walked back the way I had come, into the fog from the other direction.

Eventually I would go back to the hostel. Eventually the hostel would let me in and I would sleep, and then there would be next things, because there are always next things at nineteen — the next shift to figure out, the next month, whatever the determination of the case meant for the rest of it, which I did not know yet and was not going to know on an approximately Tuesday morning on the Someș bank before the town had fully opened for the day. I had my coat. I had my buletin. I had the nine elements in my head, all nine, in order, the bracket included.

The fog did not come up. I got cold first.
