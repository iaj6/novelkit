# 9 November 1984

My old roommate Luiza, who transferred to the Cluj plant in September, used to say the bloc heating was like a sentence: it said something and then it stopped before you understood it. I don't know that she was wrong. The heating came on at seven and stopped at a quarter to eight. Forty minutes. Enough to warm your hands if you held them over the radiator and not enough to do much else, which was more or less Luiza's point.

The room had two cots, a shared wardrobe, and a window that looked onto the factory yard. From the window, at seven in the morning in November, you could see the loading dock lights and the upper edge of the mill roofline and approximately nothing else beyond that — the fog sat on the yard like something deliberately placed. Luiza had kept a photograph on her side of the wardrobe door: a man in a white shirt at some summer occasion, squinting into the sun. She took it with her to Cluj in September. Her side of the wardrobe door was bare now, which was the kind of thing you noticed even when you weren't looking.

I was in my coat. This is not a detail I mention for atmosphere. I mention it because being in my coat meant I didn't have to decide what to do with my coat, and that morning I had enough to decide about without adding coat.

The paper was not in the room. I want to be clear about this before anything else. The geography book was at the mill, in my locker — on the shelf above the coat hooks in the production floor locker bay, exactly where I had left it when the shift ended on Wednesday. The locker has a padlock. I lock it when I leave and unlock it when I arrive; I have done this every day since the week I understood what the geography book was carrying between pages thirty-four and thirty-five. When I left on Wednesday I had closed the padlock and heard it click and walked out of the locker bay and caught the tram home. I had no reason to doubt any of this. But the paper was not here in the room with me, which meant there was one kind of thinking available to me that morning and another kind that wasn't. I could think about the paper. I could not look at it.

So I thought about it.

Line one: *Keep her warm, and lying down. Someone stays with her the whole night.* I had that.

Line three: *Count the cloths. Write the number and the hour on the paper by the bed.* I had that without trying. It was the kind of line you kept without deciding to, the way you keep a number that has stopped making sense by repeating it until it either makes sense or stops mattering.

Line seven: *Two cloths in one hour is too many.* That one I knew the way I knew the tram schedule — not by remembering it but by never having a reason to forget it.

The bracket at the end. *7 or 8 — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why. Do not wait for the morning.* I had read it enough times that it was simply there: part of the furniture of the room, without me having unpacked it. The bracket was not a step. I understood it as a bracket. I had understood that from the first reading, in the eleven months since the woman on the bus gave it to me on the route from Gherla, between the stop at Bonțida and the stop before Dej, while I held it in my hand and she looked at the window.

I tested line four, because line four was the one I was least sure of. *If she sleeps, wake her every hour and speak to her until she answers you properly.* I held that in my head for a moment, and then the doubt: every. Was it every? I went back through the text in my mind. No — the paper said *each hour*, not every. Each. I corrected it and sat with the correction. Every and each mean the same thing in ordinary speech, which is why the error was easy to make and not easy to catch. I had caught it. I didn't know what to do with that information except note it.

---

The question of burning it had come to me before. Several times, on several nights, with the seriousness you give to a question you're not going to answer but feel you should have considered properly at least once. I had considered it properly.

Burning it required fire. In the hostel room: no fire. The hostel room had a radiator that performed its duty for forty minutes a day and otherwise sat there cool and impersonal, taking up space under the window. No woodstove, no fireplace. The hallway had an ashtray on the communal table, which was for cigarettes — it was not, in practice, for papers. Burning a piece of paper in a hallway ashtray is the kind of event that gets a name: *remember the girl in room fourteen who burned something in the hallway* — and then a series of questions that don't get easier the more they're asked.

The factory yard in November was more watched than in summer, which sounds counterintuitive until you account for the fact that in summer there are more reasons to be standing outside at odd hours doing things with your hands. In November, the only people in the factory yard at the end of a shift were people with a specific destination in mind, and standing near a rubbish bin burning something was not a destination.

The hostel toilet was the final option, and I had looked at it long enough to conclude that it required the latch in stall two to hold for the duration of a burning paper, which was a duration I couldn't calculate precisely, and the latch in stall two had been holding for me roughly sixty percent of the time since August.

I didn't burn it. I kept it.

Keeping it was a decision I had made eleven months earlier without knowing I was making it. The geography book had been in the locker since my third week at the mill. Nobody had ever asked to borrow it, read it, or look at the spine. My last roommate Luiza had looked at it once, in the first week, the way you look at a book to see if it has anything on the cover that interests you, and then she had not looked at it again. I had never opened it past page thirty-three. Everything I knew about the Northern Carpathians watershed drainage system I knew from the edge of the diagram visible when I lifted the paper out: a watershed, hills, something running northward. That was sufficient.

The forty minutes of heat had warmed the room more than I expected. I went to the window and unlatched it and pushed it open a few centimetres. Outside: the factory yard, quiet in the mid-morning, with all the noise moved inside. Two women in production aprons went across the yard at a diagonal toward the canteen, not talking to each other. A cat crossed the loading dock. Someone had left a bicycle propped against the far wall below the sign that said bicycles were not to be left against the far wall. The sky above the mill roofline was white-grey and flat, the kind of sky that has finished deciding what to do and has decided to do nothing.

I had nowhere to be until the bread allocation. If I left it until tomorrow the loaf for the week would be smaller, which was a separate problem from the one I was sitting with but still a problem. The bread queue closed at four. I sat on the cot and looked at the water stain in the upper left corner of the ceiling, which had been there since I moved in and had no recognizable shape, which was something I had checked a number of times over two years, mostly in the middle of the night when I was awake and there was nothing else to look at.

It was ten to ten.

---

I went out in the early afternoon. The bread queue on my street was shorter than usual — nine people ahead of me when I got there, which was shorter than average for a Friday. Someone in line said the delivery had come late and the loaves were smaller than the allocation said they should be. The woman who said this was not saying it to me specifically; she was saying it to the air, in the way people have of putting a complaint where it can't be held against them. The cold was dry, the kind that doesn't feel acute until your nose stops working.

At the front of the queue: a child in a yellow coat, small, shifting weight from one foot to the other. His mother put her hand on his shoulder and he went still. The motorized cart somewhere around the corner made a sound and stopped making it. I was third from the front when I looked across the street.

Two women from the production floor. Second shift — I knew their faces from the locker bay and the break room, the way you know people you see at the same hour every day without ever having occasion to use their names. They should not have been on this street at this hour on a Friday morning. They were standing outside the locksmith's shop, close to the wall, in the attitude of people who have been placed somewhere rather than chosen to be there. A man was talking to them. He had a notepad open in his left hand, and while I watched he wrote something down.

Then I looked at the coat.

Factory coats are green-grey. They smell of fibre dust even when they're not in the factory; the smell gets into the weave after the first week and it doesn't come out after that. From across a street you know a factory coat by the colour and the cut and by the way the collar has gone lighter than the body from too many cold-water washes. The man's coat was not a factory coat. Bloc-committee coats are brownish, and the people who wear them have a way of carrying their shoulders that says the communal infrastructure is their professional responsibility, which they take seriously, which they would prefer you also took seriously — you can read it in the lapels from twenty metres. The man's coat was not a bloc-committee coat. It was darker and cut closer and longer, the coat of a job where you went to other people's places rather than to a fixed address. Where you arrived somewhere and wrote down what people told you.

The counter woman put my bread on the counter. I paid. I took my change and put it in my pocket without counting it.

I turned left instead of right. The long way back to the hostel was six minutes longer than the short way, along the block where the pharmacy had been closed since August. I took it. I walked at the speed of someone who had somewhere to be, which is faster than the speed of someone who is leaving a situation and slower than the speed of someone who is obviously leaving a situation. I did not look back. I did not cross the street to say hello to the two women from the production floor, who would have known my face from the locker bay, and who were standing outside a locksmith's shop talking to a man in a coat like that, and who were not where they should have been at this hour.

I went home.

---

In the hostel I went directly to my room. I shut the door. I sat down on the cot with the bread in my lap, and I did not take my coat off.

I thought about the locker. I found the ring in my coat pocket. Three keys.

The man with the notepad had been two hundred metres from the mill gate. The two women from the production floor had been talking to him on a Friday morning when they should have been in their section or at home. The coat said he had been somewhere before this street and would be somewhere after. The notepad said he was writing down what people told him, which was the kind of writing that, once written, moved somewhere you had no access to.

The paper was in the geography book, between pages thirty-four and thirty-five. The geography book was in the locker. The locker had a padlock, and I was holding the key.

I was fairly certain the locker was locked. I had locked it. I had the key in my hand.

I sat on the cot in my coat with the bread in my lap and the ring of keys in my other hand and thought about the word *fairly*, and how it was not the same word as *certainly*, and how it was the only word I had, and how it was something I had never needed to think about before this week.
