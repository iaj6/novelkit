# 4 November 1984

She is at the copying room door when the name comes through from personnel.

*Petrescu.* Then the second woman's voice, lower: *at the hospital in Dej.* A pause, something she cannot catch. *The younger one.*

Ilinca is holding October payroll tallies for Shift A. She stands in the corridor long enough to hear nothing more. Then she goes back to her desk.

The adding machine is mechanical, a grey metal instrument with a paper roll she has not changed since September. She feeds it the Shift A wage register — three hundred and twelve names, each with their production coefficient and their October total, which she has already computed twice. The keys require force. The administrative building shares a heating pipe with the weaving hall, but the weaving hall takes precedence and in November there is no heat in the administrative wing before six o'clock. She has kept her coat on since three. She works the keys. The machine returns its additions. The paper roll advances.

One by one the office empties. At five, the accounts assistant from payroll goes without a word — she has a child to collect and a bread queue to stand in before dark. At ten past, the junior planner from the second floor, who has been on the telephone all afternoon. The line is shared with three other offices and every call is audible to the corridor, but Ilinca gave up not-hearing years ago and has learned to hear without retaining. At twenty past, the woman from payroll accounts. At twenty-five past, the two women from the typing pool at the far end of the hall, who are not yet finished but go anyway.

At half past five, Domnu Boteanu from the second floor switches off his light and goes down the stairs. He does not look in. He never looks in.

At five-forty the corridor is quiet.

She puts the monthly register in the right-hand drawer, but she does not close it, because closing it would be finishing and she is not finished — the November overtime sheets are still on the left corner of the desk, untouched. She will do them in the morning.

She looks at the second shelf.

---

The 1983 ledger is where it always is: between the quarterly labour plan compliance records and the annual balance submission, the two thickest items on the shelf, which is why she placed it there. A standard four-hundred-page Ministry of Finance form-book. The cover is stamped with the year; the spine is reinforced with brown tape she applied herself in February when the original binding cracked. It is one of six year-ledgers on that shelf. A state auditor arriving without notice — and auditors arrive without notice, always; this is the first thing a bookkeeper learns — could ask her to produce any of them. She would produce them. This one she would produce without hesitation, because her entries in it are correct and complete and every transaction has a supporting document in the adjacent drawer.

She takes it down.

She carries it to her desk and opens it to the spine. The paper is there, folded twice. She works it free with two fingers, careful of the edge of the binding tape, and lays it flat on the desk under the lamp.

She reads it. Not from memory — she knows the document well enough that memory is not the issue — but in order, word by word, the way she was taught to verify a column. You do not trust yourself to have already seen the error. You begin again at the beginning.

Line one. Line two. Line three: *Count the cloths. Write the number and the hour on the paper by the bed.* When she wrote "by the bed" she had been thinking about what would already be in the room — a chair, a footstool, a folded coat on the floor, a surface for a glass and a pencil. There would always be something. She has reread this line many times since October of last year. She still thinks it is right. Line four. Line five. Line six.

Line seven: *Two cloths in one hour is too many.*

Line eight: *A fever that will not come down by morning is too long.*

Below the eighth line is the bracket. She drew it herself, in the bottom margin, with the ruler from the left-hand drawer. The bracket was necessary because she added the last instruction after the column above it was already full. She needed something to set it apart from the eight numbered lines — not a ninth step in sequence with the others, but a condition, an override, the kind of instruction that applies outside the sequence and over it. She drew the bracket with the ruler. She printed the text inside it.

She reads it. The words are all there.

She folds the sheet back along the original creases and works it into the spine. She closes the ledger and returns it to the second shelf. She straightens the adjacent ledgers so nothing is out of line.

The copying room at the end of the hall is dark. She did not hear anyone switch that light off.

---

She has made three copies of this document, in the thirteen months since October of last year.

The first: the third week of November. A woman she has known since they were both on the third shift eleven years ago, a woman who came to her directly and asked in plain terms, with no pretense of asking about something else. Ilinca trusted her then and trusts her now without qualification. She went to the woman's apartment on a Tuesday evening and copied it at her kitchen table. She left the bracket space before writing anything in the column above it, because a bracket that looks added-on will read as added-on and a bracket that was clearly intended from the start will read as intentional. She checked it twice, let the ink dry, and was home before the building superintendent locked the front entrance.

The second: December, the week before the holidays. A woman from the bloc adjacent to hers, whom she had spoken to twice — once in the bread queue in January darkness, once at the water tap when the third-floor pipes froze. She had asked a question that used none of the words for the question she was asking, and Ilinca understood the question and took a week before she said yes. She made the copy at her own kitchen table, a Sunday night, the district heating off and the kitchen cold enough that her hand was stiff by the end. She checked it twice. She left the bracket space.

The third was different.

November 3 of last year. A coal cellar off the back of a building on Strada Crișan. A woman had asked and could not come to her and could not wait. Ilinca had twenty minutes. The light came through a gap in the door — six centimetres, perhaps eight. She had brought two sheets of paper and a pencil from the administrative building that morning, carried inside the lining of her bag along with the original. She held the original at an angle to the gap and worked down the page. She did not pause between lines. She moved as quickly as she could while keeping each word close to the word it was copied from, because close is not the same as exact but it is the nearest you can get in twenty minutes by the light of a door.

She checked it once: down the lines in order. Yes to each of them. She handed it over and the woman was gone.

She has not seen that third copy since.

She does not write any of this down. She never writes it down. The names live in the column she keeps for everything that cannot be placed in a form — the machine on the second floor of the weaving hall that has run a false coefficient since February and whose correction would open questions she cannot answer, the rounding differences a generous auditor might excuse. She keeps a clean set of books. She also keeps this other one, always current, never on paper.

---

She takes the side passage out of the mill. The main gate is for the shift change — she can hear it through the wall as she passes, the sound of three hundred women going in while three hundred more come out — and she does not want to be counted in that movement this evening.

It is fully dark. The streetlamps along the river road make yellow spheres in the fog without illuminating the pavement beneath them. She knows the route well enough not to need them. The road here is cobbled and the kerb has been broken at the corner since July. She knows where the broken section is. She steps around it.

The Someș is at the bottom of Strada Someșului and she can feel it before she reaches it: the air grows wetter as she descends and the cold changes character, becomes the cold that comes up off moving water rather than the dry cold of the street. The mill's outflow enters the river three hundred metres upstream. In fog, in November, the smell of the process is in the air — hot-wet cellulose, the faint chemical sharpness she stopped noticing years ago and notices again now, because she is not thinking about the smell. The three copies.

The women she gave them to: she knows each one. She is not uncertain about any of them. She chose each for a reason. The reasons hold.

What she has not counted before is the next hand. The women she gave copies to may have passed their copies on to others, who may have passed theirs on. That was the point. That was always the point of the thing — a paper that stays in one hand reaches one person and a paper that moves reaches more. She understood this when she wrote it and she still understands it and the understanding has never before required her to trace the movement.

She needs to trace it now. Without a record. Without asking.

She crosses the bridge. Her hand rests on the railing for two seconds and the cold that comes up through the metal is the cold of the river. Below the railing there is nothing visible but white.

---

The stairwell of the bloc on Strada Avântului is dark on the third floor. The building superintendent switches the light off at five-thirty and does not switch it back on until six-thirty, because the energy reduction programme applies to common areas too, and he applies it without variation and without apology. She goes up by feel, hand on the painted rail, counting the steps from the second-floor landing. Third floor. Her key is on the left ring.

She does not turn on the overhead. She turns on the lamp by the kitchen table. The circle it makes on the oilcloth is the same circle it always makes.

She does not take her coat off.

She sits at the table.

The question still open is not about the three women she gave copies to. She knows the three women. The copies in their first hands she has accounted for.

The question is what happened to the copies after the first hands. Whether any of those hands passed a copy on, and whether any of the copies that followed from that passing eventually reached a house on Strada Gării. Whether there is a connection between the paper in the ledger spine and a nineteen-year-old who arrived at the district hospital on the morning of the fourth of November and did not leave.

She does not say the name. She does not write it.

The lamp makes its forty-watt circle on the oilcloth and nowhere else. Outside the window, across the courtyard, the bloc opposite has one window lit — the yellow of someone else's lamp behind their curtain, the same forty watts, the same circle on someone else's table. Above it and below it, the building is dark. The fog has come up level with the third floor and softened the one lit window until it is just a shape in the white.

She sits in her coat. She counts.
