# 1 December 1984

He unlocked the questioning room with the second key on his ring and went in before telling the duty guard to bring Marcu. He put the folder on the table. He arranged the chair on his side at the slight angle to the door that he had settled on years ago and had never changed because it had never given him a reason to. He took the file from the folder and opened it to the first page — the arrest entry, twenty-second of November, followed by the first and second questioning records in his own hand — and he was reading through the second record when the guard knocked and she came in.

She came in without being told to knock. She sat down across from him without being asked.

The questioning room had a frosted-glass window at shoulder height, set into the wall that faced the interior courtyard. On cold mornings the inner surface ran with condensation along its lower edge, and today it did not run. The light that came through it was uniformly gray — flat and even, without shadow — the kind of light that made a face difficult to read and a document easy to.

She had been in the holding section for nine days. The jacket she had arrived in was losing its shape along the collar and the right shoulder seam. She had stopped trying to smooth it down. She looked tired. She did not look hollowed. She ate what was given to her — he had checked the holding-section notes every other day since her arrival — and according to the night guard's log she slept, or at minimum lay quiet on the bunk when the door was checked. She had not performed distress. The people who did not perform distress were either people who had concluded that the performance would not help them, or people who had something they were protecting that they had decided was worth the cost of the room. He had not settled on which kind she was because he had not needed to. What she would or would not tell him was the same either way.

He said: Is there anything you would like to add to your previous statements?

She said: No.

He said: You found this document on bus route four. In the spring of 1984.

She said: Yes.

He said: Between the seats.

She said: Yes.

He said: Do you know a woman who was distributing this document on bus route four during the spring of 1984?

She said: No.

He had not expected her to say yes. In three questionings across nine days she had answered this sequence in the same way, at the same pace, and the consistency was not the consistency of a rehearsed lie — or not only that. It had the quality of something decided before it was said, which was a different consistency from the kind he encountered with people who were caught off-balance, and a different consistency from the kind he encountered with people who were performing. He asked because procedure required it, and because on occasion a person who had held their account for nine days revised it — not from fear, but because they had spent nine days calculating and had arrived at a different number.

He reached into the folder and took out N14 and set it on the table between them.

She looked at it. She did not pick it up.

He had observed this in each of the three sessions: she would look at the paper on the table without picking it up. He had set other forms in front of her — procedural forms she had signed at the opening of each session, a second document in the first questioning — and she had handled those without hesitation. The paper was the one thing she did not touch. He watched her not touch it for a moment.

He reached into the folder and took out another sheet — one of the short-form copies from the numbered branch, the one he had collected in the early weeks — and unfolded it beside the first.

He said: In the last line. Your copy says something different from this one.

He pointed to the area below the eighth line on N14. The hand-drawn bracket in the left margin, the text inside it.

He said: Yours says this.

He read aloud: *Seven or eight — take her in. Say she began bleeding on her own and you do not know why. Do not wait for the morning.*

He let it settle.

He said: This copy says something different in the same position.

He pointed to line 9 on the numbered sheet and read: *If seven or eight, take her in in the morning.*

He set both sheets flat on the table.

He said: Copies that come from the same source are similar to each other in the same ways. Copies that come from different sources are similar to each other in different ways. The last line of your copy is different from the last line of most of the copies I have collected. The difference is inherited — it means your copy comes from a branch that did not go through the version that produced this wording. A branch that is older, or separate. That is what the arithmetic says.

She was looking at N14. Not at him, and not at the numbered sheet.

He said: If you could tell me where you received yours, I could close this more quickly.

She said: I found it on the bus.

He looked at her for a moment. Whatever was behind that account — a name, a transaction, a face on a morning bus in the spring — had not shifted in nine days and was not going to shift in this room today.

He wrote in the file: *Marcu, D. — maintains original statement. No new information. Third questioning completed.* He dated it and added his initials and the file number.

He said: You can go back.

She stood. She did not ask him anything. She went to the door and through it, and he heard the guard in the corridor say a word to her — no content, just the sound of a word, the register of a man addressing someone to be moved — and then her footsteps going down the hall until he could not hear them.

He sat with the open file for a moment. Through the frosted window the courtyard was white — frost on the stone paving. He replaced N14 in its sleeve and the numbered copy in its envelope. He closed and locked the bottom drawer of the table, where the longer-hold dosare lived, and took his keys and went out.

---

The Population Section was at the far end of the same corridor, past the duty officer's alcove, where the floor went from green linoleum to gray. He had walked past it every working day for seven years.

The register room was smaller than the investigations room. Two metal shelving units occupied the wall opposite the door, holding the residential ledgers organized by street name. A writing table sat under a lamp with a green glass shade; a window to the right overlooked an interior yard wall. The registrar was a man of about fifty, ink on both cuffs and the middle two fingers of his right hand, who looked up when Munteanu came in but remained seated.

Munteanu said he needed the residential register for Strada Crișan, bloc eighteen.

The registrar took a ledger from the second shelving unit — the B-series, he said, blocks four through thirty-two — and opened it on the writing table. It was a large hardbound book, printed registration forms filled in by hand over the years, at least three different hands by the variation in slant. It smelled of carbon paper. Underneath the carbon there was an older smell, the smell of stored air and damp binding, a room kept closed.

Munteanu turned the pages himself.

Third floor, left-side flat. One registered occupant since 1979. Female. He wrote the name in his notebook in the small characters he used for names: surname first, then given name, then the date of primary registration. Three items.

He moved to the amendment column.

November 1983: a second occupant added under temporary registration. Female. Same surname. Listed as *sora.* Stamped and dated the eighth of November. He wrote this.

February 1984: a departure notation. Forwarding address in a narrower, more regular hand than the amendment entry — the handwriting of someone who had done this particular form many times.

He read the forwarding address. Strada Mihai, number fourteen, stairwell B, flat eight.

He had been to that address on the twelfth day of the investigation. He had collected the copy he had designated N10 from Viorica Damian there. During that interview she had told him, when he asked about any prior residential address, that she had stayed with her sister for a few months and then moved back to her own flat when it became available again. He had written this down. He had not done anything with it at the time because it was adjacent information — a family connection, not a lead.

The population register made it a different kind of information. The sister who had been temporarily registered at Strada Crișan, bloc eighteen, third floor, in November of 1983, and who had departed in February of 1984 with a forwarding address at Strada Mihai 14/B/8, was the same woman he had already spoken to. The connection between the two flats was documented, dated, stamped. He had known it from Viorica Damian's account; now he had the administrative record behind it.

He asked the registrar to log the consultation and stamp it. The registrar did this without asking why. Munteanu thanked him and went back down the corridor.

---

At his desk he opened the notebook to the current page and wrote the cellar woman's full name below the seven entries already there. Below the name: flat number, bloc number, street. He drew a single line under it.

Eight nodes: seven copies in evidence envelopes in the desk drawer, one confirmed address in the notebook.

He counted the days since the fourth of November: twenty-eight. The fifth of December was four days from today.

He had planned to go to Strada Crișan on the twenty-ninth. He had gone to Dorobanților instead, which had taken the whole morning and produced an account that required the population register before he could act on it. On the thirtieth he had written the weekly summary for the Inspectoratul Județean. Today he had done the questioning and the register. Tomorrow he would go to Strada Crișan.

He opened the folder and found the blank space on the inside front cover. In pencil, at the lower margin: *5 dec.* He had carried the date in his head for nineteen days, since the captain had said it in the corridor on Day 16. He wrote it now because writing it on the inside of the folder was a commitment of a different kind — on paper, in his own hand, in the file — and he had decided he was going to resolve this before the date arrived.

He looked at the seven evidence envelopes on the desk. N17, N11, N10, N8, N14, N18, N12 — in the order he had collected them. Beyond the cellar woman at Strada Crișan was one more step: whoever had given her the copy she had made in the fall of 1983. The woman who had received the cellar copy and produced, through two further hands, the version that Ana Petrescu's household had followed on the fourth of November. He was one house call from the person who had made the cellar copy.

The arithmetic was complete.

He put on his coat.

He wrote the Strada Crișan address on a fresh notebook page, tore it out along the perforated edge, folded it once, and put it in the right outer pocket. He straightened the folder on the desk with one hand. He turned off the desk lamp and went out.
