# 8 November 1984

She is at the window when the heating pipes knock. Seven o'clock. She has been up since half past five — there is no point lying still after five thirty in November, because the cold that comes through the bloc wall at that hour is not a cold she can sleep through, and the heating will not come on for another ninety minutes.

The nechezol is in her hand. She drinks it standing. The glass has condensation in the lower corners; there is a clear strip across the centre where she can see the street — two bare lindens, the pavement, and then nothing. The fog sits in the valley, pressed down against the cold, not moving. On still mornings it fills from the river to the rooftops and higher. The Someș is somewhere behind it, wide and flat, invisible.

She thinks: what she holds.

N2 branch: Georgeta, Livia. Both confirmed. Both clean.

N3: Elena Florescu, third shift, production floor. Not yet confirmed. Today.

The cellar copy. November third, last year. One entry. She lets it stand.

Three copies. One branch still open. She will reach Elena today, and then the count will be settled.

The pipes hiss and then go quiet. The heat is still in the radiator — she can feel it from where she stands, two metres away. By eight forty-five the radiator will be cold. She finishes the nechezol and rinses the cup. The adding machine is at the mill. Here there is only the pen and the pale-green notebook she keeps for grocery calculations, both of them on the corner of the kitchen table. She does not touch them. She puts her coat on and goes out.

---

The bread queue is on Strada Comercială. She is in it by seven twenty, and there are already eleven people ahead of her. The shutters of the alimentară are down and dark; there is no movement inside and no sign there will be, for another twenty minutes at least. The cold is dry, the kind that does not feel acute until you have been standing in it long enough. Her breath comes white. The breath of the woman ahead of her comes white. At the front of the queue a child shifts his weight from one foot to the other, his collar up, his hands in his pockets. Nobody looks at the child.

She knows three of the faces by sight: the woman from the second floor of her bloc, whose name she does not know but whose light was on at eleven the previous night; a man with a grey moustache who is in this queue every Thursday at this hour, regular as a ledger entry; a woman of sixty with a knitted hat she has worn every day since September, regardless of weather. Ilinca does not greet any of them. You stand in the queue. You do not calculate. You wait for the shutters to go up.

At five past eight the shutters go up. At twenty to nine she has her two loaves, wrapped in paper, and is walking back up Strada Comercială.

---

She leaves the bread on the kitchen table. She puts on her heavier coat and her boots, the ones with the rubber soles she bought at the universal shop in September, which have so far kept out most of the wet. She takes the pot from the corner of the kitchen where it has been standing since the first week of October: Marta's pot, cast iron, the large one she borrowed for the plum preserves. She has owed Marta the pot for six weeks.

Her cousin Marta lives on Bulevardul Victoriei in a second-floor flat. The walk is twelve minutes by the back streets and eleven by the main road. She takes the back streets. The pot is heavy; she carries it with both hands. The fog has not lifted yet from the street level and she walks through it, past the bicycle workshop that is never open in the mornings, past the bakery on Strada Lungă with its shutters still down, past a cluster of boys in school coats going the other way.

Marta opens the door before Ilinca has reached the top of the stairs. She is in her apron and indoor shoes, and she has flour on one hand, which means she has been at the kitchen table. She looks at the pot and says: you didn't need to make a special trip.

Ilinca says: I was passing.

She was not passing. Marta works the day shift in hall A, production floor, and returned from her shift two hours ago. She has known Ilinca since childhood and she knows when Ilinca is not passing. She looks at her with the particular expression of a woman who has decided not to ask.

The kitchen smells of dough and of something earlier in the day — onions, a broth that has cooked down. It is warmer than Ilinca's kitchen, larger, with a window over the courtyard and a rug that has always been there as long as Ilinca can remember. Marta sets the pot on the counter and pours a second nechezol without asking. They sit at the table. Marta talks: the new tram schedule, which is two months old now but which she has only recently begun to need; her youngest daughter's classroom, where a window has not closed properly since August and the teacher has taped cardboard over the gap, which helps with the draught but not the light. Ilinca listens. She notes the clock above the stove.

After nine minutes she says: I heard Elena Florescu has been unwell.

Marta says: has she.

Third shift, Ilinca says. Lives in your next bloc over. If you happen to see her — tell her I said to keep the influenza quiet. Not to come in until she's better. No reason to make it worse.

Marta looks at her cup and then at Ilinca and then back at her cup.

Ilinca says: it's nothing urgent. Only if it's convenient.

Marta says: I can go now. She'll be in at this hour.

She takes her apron off and hangs it on the back of the door. Her daughter, who is eleven, has been doing schoolwork at the table in the other room; Ilinca can hear the scratch of a pencil, steady, not hurried. Marta puts her coat on. She says: ten minutes.

Ilinca says she will wait in the stairwell.

She goes down and stands on the second-floor landing, below the working light bulb, and waits. The stairwell smells of cabbage and of the particular cold that accumulates in enclosed spaces that are not quite as cold as the outside. Somewhere above her a door opens and closes. On the street, the sound of a cart, and then nothing.

Seventeen minutes. Then the front door and Marta's youngest on the stairs, coat on, satchel on her back, going to the afternoon school shift. She stops when she sees Ilinca.

Marta's voice from above: tell her what Elena said.

The girl says: Elena says she's fine. She's been careful. She hasn't seen anyone she doesn't know.

She goes out the door. Ilinca stands for a moment on the landing.

---

She takes the long way home. It adds twelve minutes and goes along the embankment. The fog has lifted from street level; she can see to the end of the block, and beyond it the bare tops of the willows over the river. The Someș is brown and fast and cold-looking. Yellow leaves turn in the slow current near the bank. The sky is white-grey and not moving.

She walks, and she accounts.

N2 branch: confirmed. N3 branch: confirmed, this afternoon. Both clean, both quiet. No contact, no names said, no questions asked from any direction she has not herself sent. The count is three. All three branches she seeded herself. All three accounted for.

Which leaves one that she does not think of as a question.

November third, last year. The door to the street was open just enough for the light from the lamp outside to fall onto the paper — a rectangle of yellow, not steady, because the hinge moved slightly in the draft from the coal chute stairs. She had the master in her left hand and the pen in her right. Ten minutes. Less, possibly; she did not look at the time. The woman could not wait. The woman had come a long way and could not be late and could not be seen standing.

She counted the lines as she wrote them. One through eight, in order, each numbered in sequence, identical to the master. She can see the lines. She can see the numbers.

And then the bracket. In October she had drawn it herself: two strokes — one down, one across the bottom — and a corner, making the three sides of a rectangle around the words. An override, not a step. In the cellar she drew it again: two strokes and a corner. She remembers the pressure of the pen tip on the paper. She remembers drawing the corner last. And inside it she wrote: *7 or 8 — take her in.* And then: *Do not wait for the morning.*

Those words are in her handwriting, in the bracket, on that copy.

She has checked this before. She found it correct before.

She was in a hurry, and she was careful, and she has been both at the same time her entire working life. She counted the lines. She put the bracket.

A woman passes her on the embankment path with a bicycle. A door somewhere in the bloc row to her left opens and shuts. The willows at the edge of the path are bare and black, roots in the river mud, branches reaching across the path and not quite reaching the other side.

She is almost certain.

---

She is home by half past four. She hangs up her coat and her scarf on the door hook and stands for a moment in the kitchen. The heating will come on again at six, if it comes on.

She sits at the table. The pale-green notebook is where it always is. The pen is beside it. She thinks about the adding machine at the mill: the weight of the crank, the sound it makes, the column of figures it confirms without ambiguity. Here, only the pen. She picks it up and holds it. She puts it down.

She thinks about writing something.

She does not open the notebook.

Outside the window the grey is deepening into the grey before dark, and she watches it change without moving to turn on the second lamp. The forty-watt bulb on the table makes a circle and leaves the rest of the kitchen in shadow. This is enough light. She has worked in worse.

She gets up and goes into the bedroom and lies down on the bed with her shoes on and looks at the ceiling. The ceiling is white in the last light from the window and then less white as the light drops. On the street below: a cart, the sound of a metal door on a hinge, something with an engine that does not accelerate. Nothing she needs to account for.

N2. N3. The cellar copy.

Three. Confirmed, confirmed, and — she was careful, she counted, she drew the bracket.

The second lamp stays off. Outside, it is full dark now, and the block heating will come on at six or it will not come on. She does not get up. She is almost certain, and certainty of this kind is a column that balances; she has closed it before and found it correct.

She lies still.
