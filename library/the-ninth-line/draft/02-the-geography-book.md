# 5 November 1984

The woman from the third floor told us the way you tell a thing you don't want to be the one to tell. She was already in her coat. She said it toward the wall at the end of the hallway — *she died at the hospital in Dej, the younger Petrescu* — and then she went downstairs and left us standing there. Nobody moved first. We all stood in the hallway waiting for someone else to be the person who stopped standing there.

I didn't know Ana Petrescu. I knew the name the way you know names in a mill town — she worked the day shift, I worked nights, we had never been in the same building at the same time as far as I could reckon. What I knew about her was that she was nineteen, which is what I also am, and now she wasn't. The woman from the third floor was already gone. I went back to my room and got my jacket and my gloves and my bag. The shift started at six and if I didn't leave now the section head would have a reason to remember my face, and I didn't want that, and so I put my gloves on and went downstairs, and that was November fifth, 1984, which is where this begins.

---

It was dark at five-thirty. In November in the Someș valley darkness is not an event, it is the default condition that the daytime interrupts briefly and incompletely. The streetlamps along the road to the mill make yellow haloes in the fog rather than light on the pavement, and this is the light I have walked to work by for two years: more fog than lamp, more direction than illumination. I know the road by the soles of my shoes. I know the stretch where the cobbles are loose near the transformer station and the bend where the puddle collects against the cracked kerb and the place where the smell of the mill arrives before you can see the mill's lights — the hot-wet chemical sweetness of the fiber process mixed with the river cold, the smell that lives in my coat whether I notice it or not.

The women ahead of me I knew by their coats. Mariana's was the navy one with the collar lining coming loose on the left side — she had pinned it twice and the pin kept giving way. Costina's was the brown one with the wooden toggles, short on her because she is taller than it was made for. The two women from Shift B whose names I had not learned in fourteen months, for reasons that say something about me or about fourteen months and I am not sure which. I fell in behind their backs and followed them through the dark and the fog and the mill smell and didn't say anything, because it was five-thirty in the morning and the woman from the third floor had said what she said and I didn't have anything to add to it.

By the time the smell of the mill was strong I could see the lights over the production floor. They are always on. It is the most reliable thing about my life in Dej, and I am not going to pretend I don't find it comforting. I showed my cartelă at the gate. I went to put my things in the locker.

The geography book was on the shelf above the coat hooks, where I had put it on my first day two years ago and never moved it since. It was published in 1978, purchased for me by my mother when I was twelve, and neither of us has ever read it. I brought it to Dej because I thought I ought to look like a person who read things, and then when I got to the hostel and understood the situation I decided the factory locker was more private than the hostel room, and I left the book on the shelf and walked away from it. For eleven months it had been just a thing I owned, the way the coat is a thing I own. I did not look at it. I went to work.

---

On the production floor the looms are twelve metres high and if you want to say something to someone standing two metres away, you put your mouth near their ear and shout. If what you want to say is not the kind of thing you shout, you wait. Everyone waits. We have all been waiting for years at whatever volume is required by the job, and we are all very good at it.

What this meant on the morning of the fifth was that I had six hours in which nothing was required of me except to watch the thread and count the revolutions and do what I was there to do. The woman from the third floor had said what she said and Ana Petrescu was in the past tense and the name was in my head and I was on the production floor where the looms make other things impossible. I watched Costina's hands on her machine — she has been here eight years and knows things about it that the maintenance record doesn't, and I have learned more from watching her hands than from anything the section head has ever said directly to me — and I counted what I was supposed to count, and I was very grateful for the noise, which is not something I say lightly because the looms are genuinely very loud.

Six hours. The break is twenty minutes. I had been not-thinking for six hours and I was very tired from it.

---

I went to my locker and took the geography book off the shelf.

Nobody has ever opened my geography book, including me. I want that on the record because of everything that came after: I received the paper that was inside it on the bus from Gherla, eleven months before the fifth of November. The woman who gave it to me had a face like a closed shop and she put it in my hand the way you pass someone the salt — without asking if they wanted salt, without looking at them while they took it. I had eleven minutes between the stop at Bonțida and the stop before Dej and I read what was on the paper in those eleven minutes and I understood that it was the kind of thing you kept somewhere safe and I needed to decide, in eleven minutes, where safe was.

The hostel was not safe. The hostel room has a door with a latch that can be lifted from the outside with a fingernail. I know this because the woman across the hall demonstrated it in the second week, not as a threat but as a fact worth knowing, and I have been grateful for it since. The factory locker locks. I am the only one with the key. I have not lost the key in two years and I do not intend to.

So I put the paper inside the geography book, between pages 34 and 35, which is a double-page diagram of the watershed drainage of the Northern Carpathians, illustrated in blue and grey. I put the geography book on the shelf in the locker. For eleven months it sat there. I want to be clear about this: I never used it. I never showed it to anyone. I could not have told you what was written on any line of it if you had held me upside down. It was just a thing I owned, the same category as the coat — the green wool one with the loose button on the left cuff that I keep meaning to resew.

I took the geography book to the bathroom. There are four stalls and two of them have latches that work. I took one of the ones with a latch.

---

I sat on the edge of the lid with the book on my knees and opened it to pages 34 and 35 and took out the paper. It was folded three times — twice across and once along — and the fold lines had softened over eleven months into the kind of creases that will hold their shape for years without being asked to.

I read it straight through.

Lines one through eight are instructions, numbered, imperative. I am not going to set all of them out here. What I want to say is that I read each one and it was legible and I continued to the next. At the bottom of the page, below the eighth numbered line, there is a bracket. It is drawn in pencil — a hand-drawn rectangle around the last part of the document, with lines that are mostly straight. Inside the bracket it says:

*7 or 8 — take her in.*

And more. The bracket holds more than that — it says what to say when you get there, and one other thing, one line that I didn't retain on the bus and didn't retain in the first week at the hostel and didn't retain now. What I retained, this time, was the bracket itself. The drawn line around it. It was not a ninth step in the same sequence. It was something else — a different kind of instruction, the kind you draw a box around to show it overrides the sequence rather than continuing it.

I had never been able to tell you what was inside that bracket before the fifth. Not if you had asked me on the fourth or the third or at any point in the previous eleven months. I read the paper three times in my life before the fifth of November and each time I read it the way you read something you have been handed and don't know what to do with yet — looking to find out what it is, not to understand it. I retained almost nothing, which I am telling you now because of what came after. I am not asking you to believe it changed anything. I am asking you to believe it is true.

On the fifth of November what I saw was the bracket. It was drawn there. I could see that the thing at the bottom was bracketed, which made it different from the lines above, and today that difference mattered in a way it hadn't mattered before. I didn't know why. I know why now.

---

I folded the paper back along the three creases and put it between pages 34 and 35. I closed the geography book.

The women's bathroom at the AFE production floor has a window above the sinks that is painted shut. In the fluorescent light the walls are a colour that started as pale yellow and has become something else over a number of years I will not guess at. I stood at the sink and ran cold water on my wrists, which does something to my heart rate that is worth doing in certain situations, and then I went back to the locker and put the geography book on the shelf above the coat hooks.

What I told myself: this is still a thing I own, like the coat. Not evidence of anything I did. I had received it on a bus, from a woman I had never seen before and have not seen since, and I had not used it and I had not shown it to anyone and I could not recite a single line of it from memory, including the line inside the bracket. Eleven months of owning it and I had done nothing with it except leave it on a shelf. Same as the coat. Same category entirely.

What I also told myself: the locker locks. The factory has a gate and a guard. I am a woman who tends the looms and has not been in any trouble that mattered, and there is no reason for anyone to open my locker, because there is no reason for anyone to look at me.

I went back to the production floor and worked until two.

---

After the shift I walked back through fog that had not lifted. In November in this valley it often does not. The hostel is ten minutes from the mill gate on a clear day and fifteen when you have to navigate by memory, and on the fifth it was closer to fifteen. I signed in at the desk and went upstairs to my room.

The ceiling of my room has a water stain in the upper left corner. It has been there since before I moved in and it has not grown in two years, which I find reassuring. It does not look like anything. Some water stains resolve into shapes — a face, an animal, a map of somewhere — but this one is just a stain. I find this restful.

I lay on the bed with my coat on.

The geography book was in the locker. The locker was in a building with a gate. I had not used the paper. I had not shown it to anyone. I had not recited it to anyone, could not recite it to anyone. What had gone wrong for Ana Petrescu had gone wrong before the fifth, before I read the bracket or the numbered lines above it, before I ran cold water on my wrists and put the book back on the shelf. I had not been any part of it. I was as innocent as I had been when I got on the bus from Gherla, and the geography book in the locker was the geography book in the locker, and that was the afternoon of the fifth.

Whether that was the right place for it, I did not know. I did not know what the right place was, or whether there was one, or whether the bracket being there rather than not being there made any difference to anything that had already happened or was going to happen next. I lay there with my coat on and I did not know any of it, and so I did nothing, which is itself a kind of decision, as I found out later.

The ceiling stain was in its corner. The light through the curtain was the grey of mid-afternoon fog. I stayed on the bed until it was dark enough that the grey went away.
