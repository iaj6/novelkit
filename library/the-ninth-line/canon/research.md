## 1. Decree 770: The Law and Its Enforcement Machinery

### The decree and its core prohibition

Decree 770 was signed personally by Nicolae Ceaușescu in October 1966. It prohibited abortion
in almost all circumstances (exceptions: women over 40 or 45 depending on the period; women who
had already raised four or more children; cases of rape, incest, or fetal deformity; and a narrow
category of life-threatening conditions). [cite:01] Contraceptives were simultaneously removed from
sale. [cite:01]

### Monthly gynecological examinations

**All women of childbearing age (generally defined as 15–45) were required to undergo mandatory
monthly gynecological examinations.** [cite:01] Where those examinations were conducted varied, but
the workplace was the primary site in industrial settings.

One documented literary account, Adrian Sângeorzan's "The Bicycle Factory" (published in English
translation in Words Without Borders, 2012), describes the procedure from the examining physician's
perspective: the factory physician was assigned to perform gynecological check-ups on forty to sixty
female workers each week in less than two hours; foremen lined women up and brought them to be
checked; the factory floor examination was the enforcement mechanism in industrial settings. [cite:20]
One worker's sardonic response — that the doctor "searches us for eggs, like hens" — was remembered
by the narrating physician as capturing the daily register of the examinations. [cite:20]

From 1984, the requirement at state institutions was formalized further: women working for or
attending any state institution were required to undergo at least annual gynecological examinations
at their place of employment, with investigations into all miscarriages. [cite:03] The monthly
requirement remained the practice at most factories regardless. [cite:02]

All detected pregnancies were followed until birth; a woman found to be pregnant was effectively
tracked by the state from detection through delivery. [cite:01]

### Physician obligations and penalties

Doctors were legally required to notify prosecutors of any abortion intervention, either beforehand
or within twenty-four hours. [cite:04] Doctors who performed abortion for health reasons and failed
to report to higher authorities faced one to three months imprisonment. [cite:05] Between 1967 and
1973 alone, 83 doctors were indicted alongside 187 health workers. [cite:04] Each administrative
region had a Disciplinary Board for Health Personnel that conducted investigations and occasional
show trials. [cite:03] Medical practitioners were held partly responsible for the national birthrate.
[cite:03]

Despite these pressures, many doctors helped women by falsely diagnosing qualifying conditions
(diabetes, hepatitis) or prescribing drugs known to cause miscarriage. [cite:03] The enforcement
system therefore coexisted with widespread evasion by sympathetic physicians — which is part of what
made the Securitate's hospital surveillance so intensive.

### The 1984 secret action plan

A third secret action plan, approved by Internal Affairs Minister George Homoștean in the spring of
1984, mandated surveillance of "all medical units, including those without obstetrics and
gynaecology, as well as units where most of the staff is female." [cite:04] The plan placed
informants throughout healthcare facilities. In Cluj County alone, 54 doctors were recruited as
Securitate informants under this plan. [cite:04]

Securitate agents were stationed in or near operating theatres. If doctors performed a dilation and
curettage, agents would assess whether the uterus showed signs of infection — if it did, they
concluded the woman had had a failed illegal abortion. [cite:04]

### Maternal mortality statistics

This is the most precisely documented element of the decree's consequences. The figures are:

- **1980**: 132.1 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births [cite:02]
- **1985**: 137.4 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births [cite:02]
- **1989**: 169.4 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births (the highest ever recorded in Europe) [cite:02]

By the late 1980s, Romanian women were dying at rates more than ten times higher than those in
Western Europe, despite Western European rates declining during the same period. [cite:03] Over 9,000
women died between 1965 and 1989 from complications of illegal procedures. [cite:03] Unsafe abortion
deaths accounted for more than 80% — one source states 87% — of all maternal deaths by the late
1980s. [cite:02]

**The November 1984 setting of the book falls between the 1980 and 1985 data points.** The actual
rate in November 1984 is not in the sources; a value near the 1985 figure of 137.4 is the closest
anchored estimate.

**Source disagreement:** One source (Wikipedia, Decree 770) does not provide year-by-year figures
and refers only to the rate becoming "the highest in Europe." Another (Decree Chronicles, "Guardians
of the Decree") provides the specific year-by-year figures. The Decree Chronicles figures are the
most specific and are used above. A third source ([cite:06], the 1984 CSMonitor article) does not
address maternal mortality directly but confirms the general political and enforcement climate in
the period.

## 2. Going to the Hospital: The Danger and the Calculus

### Why hospital was dangerous

This section is directly load-bearing for the book's premise and must be handled precisely.
**The research scope explicitly excludes the medical content of the circulated document.**
What follows describes only the hospital environment and the enforcement regime.

Women who arrived at a hospital with complications from an illegal procedure were not simply
treated. The documented practice was: **Securitate agents or prosecutors would arrive at the
bedside and require the woman to name who had performed the procedure before treatment was
authorized.** [cite:04] One documented case describes a woman with severe hemorrhage being withheld
treatment for five days while being threatened; she died. [cite:04] Another formulation: "state
security operatives would hover at hospitals, forcing women to give up the names of underground
clinics even as they bled to death." [cite:02]

This enforcement created a lethal calculus: going to the hospital meant interrogation and the
near-certainty of naming someone; not going meant managing the complication at home. The timing
of the decision — how long to wait before the risk of staying home exceeded the risk of going —
is precisely the subject of the circulated document.

### What the hospital would know

Hospitals were required to report all cases to the Miliția and to prosecutors. **Every doctor who
treated a woman for complications of an illegal procedure was in theory liable.** [cite:05] The
enforcement agents were looking not just at the woman's condition but at whether the attending
physician had been complicit. [cite:04] This created a situation where even sympathetic doctors
were constrained by the presence of agents.

**Inference (not in sources):** A woman arriving at the district hospital in Dej in November 1984
who was already gravely ill would have been assessed first by the duty physician, who would have
been required to classify the case and notify the relevant authorities (prosecutor and, for a death
or near-death, Miliția). The specific notification form or chain is not documented in the sources
consulted, but the obligation to report is consistent across all sources.

### The "say she began bleeding on her own" instruction

The canonical document in the brief (N1) includes the line: *Say she began bleeding on her own
and you do not know why.* This specific framing maps directly onto the documented hospital
enforcement regime. If a woman could plausibly present as a spontaneous miscarriage, the 
attending physician had cover to treat without triggering a prosecution investigation. The
instruction is accurate to the actual enforcement environment. [cite:03], [cite:04]

## 3. The Someș Valley: Geography and the Town of Dej

### Location and rivers

Dej is a municipality in Cluj County, Transylvania, 60 kilometres north of Cluj-Napoca. [cite:11]
The town sits at the confluence of two rivers: **the Someșul Mic (Little Someș) and the Someșul
Mare (Large Someș) join approximately 4 km upstream of Dej at a village called Mica, and the
combined Someș then flows through and past the town.** [cite:12] The town itself lies at the left
bank of the river at an elevation of approximately 285 meters above sea level, within the valley
corridor. [cite:11]

The Someș is 415 km long. It flows west and northwest from Dej toward Satu Mare and eventually
into the Tisza in Hungary. The valley at Dej is a wide alluvial basin where the Transylvanian
Plateau slopes down to the river. [cite:12]

### Population

Census data: 32,345 residents in 1977; 41,216 in 1992. [cite:11] **Inference: The 1984 population
was likely in the range of 35,000–38,000.** The 1977–1992 growth reflects heavy communist-era
industrialization and rural-to-urban migration characteristic of all Romanian towns with significant
industrial enterprises. No census figure for the 1980s was found in sources consulted.

### Industry during communism: the fiber enterprise

The research confirmed one major industrial facility in Dej during the communist period:
**the Artificial Fiber Enterprise (Intreprinderea de Fibre Artificiale, or AFE)**, which produced
celofiber (cellulose-based synthetic fiber) and employed approximately 1,500 workers. [cite:13]
This facility was located approximately 2 km from the Someș river bed. [cite:13]

Celofiber is a regenerated cellulose fiber (similar to rayon) used in textile manufacturing.
**Inference:** A mill town with a fiber enterprise would have downstream textile production —
weaving and finishing facilities that used the fiber — as well as the AFE itself. The brief's
"textile mill" (fabrica de textile) is consistent with the known industrial profile of Dej. The
exact name and ownership structure of the textile operation (as distinct from the AFE itself) is
not confirmed by sources consulted; the AFE is the documented industrial anchor.

Historical salt mining also operated at Ocna Dej (Ocna Dejului), one of the villages administered
by Dej. [cite:11] The salt works predate communism but continued through the period.

### November in the Someș valley

**Average temperatures in November, Transylvania:** daytime maximum 8–11°C; nighttime minimum
approaching or below 0°C. [cite:21] Precipitation averages 40–58mm for the month — moderate rain
with occasional early frost and, by late November, the possibility of snow. [cite:21]

River valleys in Transylvania are characteristically foggy in autumn and early winter. The Someș
valley between Dej and the confluence point would collect ground fog on still mornings. **Inference:**
The industrial fiber enterprise, which involves hot-wet processes and produces effluent into the
river, would add steam and chemical haze to the natural valley fog in cold weather. No source
confirmed this specific industrial detail for the AFE at Dej; it is an inference from general
knowledge of rayon/celofiber production.

### The district hospital at Dej

**Inference (not in sources):** A town of 35,000–38,000 with an established industrial base would
have had a district/municipal hospital (Spitalul Orășenesc Dej or similar designation) as part of
the communist-era healthcare infrastructure. The brief specifies that Ana dies at the "district
hospital in Dej." No primary source describing the specific hospital's layout, staffing, or
conditions was found. Given the documented power-cut conditions across Romanian hospitals in
1983–84 [cite:07] — including the death of dozens of neonatal patients from power cuts to
incubators in winter 1983 — the brief's implication that the hospital was understaffed and under
power constraints is historically grounded.

## 4. Daily Life in 1984: Scarcity, Rationing, and the Texture of November

### The rationing system (cartelă)

Romania introduced formal food rationing by county decree in December 1980, intensifying through
1981–1989. [cite:09] In December 1983, a new dietary program for 1984 established even lower
allowances. [cite:07]

**The documented monthly/daily rations for 1984:**

| Item | Allowance |
|------|-----------|
| Bread | 300 g per day (approximately half a loaf) [cite:10] |
| Meat (pork or beef) | 500 g per month [cite:08] |
| Chicken | 1 kg per month [cite:08] |
| Eggs | 8–12 per month [cite:08], [cite:09] |
| Butter | 100 g per month [cite:08] |
| Oil | 750 ml – 1 liter per month [cite:09] |
| Sugar | 1 kg per month [cite:08] |
| Cheese | 500 g per month [cite:08] |

The government framed these quantities as a "Rational Nutrition Programme," claiming Romanians
required only 2,700–2,800 calories daily. [cite:09] Official rations were theoretical minimums;
actual availability was frequently lower. One account: "If you were able to get 500g of meat during
whole month then it was huge success." [cite:09]

### Food substitutes and queue culture

Coffee had entirely disappeared; the substitute was **nechezol**, a horse-feed-based mixture of
chicory, chickpeas, and barley. [cite:08] Meat was often replaced by **soy salami** (salam de soia)
and smoked pig feet colloquially called **adidași** (a joke: they had "stripes" like Adidas
shoes). [cite:08] Vietnamese shrimp chips (imported under a trade arrangement) appeared on shelves
when nothing else did. [cite:08]

Food queues were a daily institution. Citizens stood for hours — sometimes overnight — only to be
turned away when supplies ran out. [cite:08] Bread walks (buying bread from the assigned state
store) were organized by family, with different members taking different days. [cite:10] One
contemporary journalist (CSMonitor, January 1984) witnessed "people lined up to buy broken eggs
being handed out in paper cups" and "two lines of people spread from the back of a van selling
freezer-burned chickens." Food prices had increased 35% in a single year. [cite:06]

Hoarding food beyond the ration was criminalized starting in 1981. [cite:08] Rural people with
private plots had an advantage; urban industrial workers had almost none. [cite:08]

### Electricity and heating

**One 40-watt bulb per household** was the effective norm in 1984; this was not a legal limit but
the result of a government-mandated 50% energy consumption reduction combined with ongoing
shortages. [cite:06] Indoor electric heaters were banned; if one person in a building used one and
was caught, power was cut to the entire building. [cite:07]

District heating (centralized hot water through apartment-block systems) was cut frequently and
without notice to save energy. Hot water availability was limited to approximately one day per week
in most apartments. [cite:07] The government's own 1984 target was 50% reduction in all energy
consumption. [cite:06]

The consequences for hospitals were documented: in the winter of 1983 (one year before the book's
setting), dozens of babies in neonatal intensive care units died from power cuts to incubators.
[cite:07]

### The texture of a November workday

**From the 1984 CSMonitor article, directly observed:** Police and armed soldiers on street corners.
No visible protest. Even hotel rooms stripped of extra light bulbs. People waiting at vans for
meat, at state stores for eggs, starting at dawn. [cite:06]

**Inference:** November in an industrial town like Dej in 1984 would mean: darkness at 4:30 pm;
unheated hallways in apartment blocks; the night shift arriving at the factory while it was still
dark and leaving after dark again; block committees responsible for turning off stairwell lights
after a set hour; coal furnaces in older buildings for those who could get coal; heavy coats worn
indoors in offices where the district heating had not come on.

## 5. The Miliția: Structure, Procedure, and the Criminal File

### Organization

The Miliția was the civilian police force of the Socialist Republic of Romania, established by
decree in January 1949, under the Interior Ministry and directly controlled by the Romanian
Workers' Party (later Romanian Communist Party). [cite:14] It was modeled on the Soviet Militsiya
with Soviet advisors providing ideological guidance. [cite:14]

Three branches: Territorial Militia, Railroad Militia, Prison Militia. Each territorial district
had a militia with a Population Section, Investigations Section, and Information Office. [cite:14]

### Rank structure

Personnel held military ranks. The documented officer ranks include (ascending): Sub-Locotenent,
**Locotenent**, Locotenent Major, Căpitan, Major, Locotenent-Colonel, Colonel, then general
officer grades (General Major, General Locotenent, General Colonel). [cite:14]

The book's character Vasile Munteanu is a lieutenant (Locotenent), which is a mid-level officer
rank — plausibly the rank of someone handling a county criminal investigation in a district town,
reporting to a superior (căpitan or higher) at the county inspectorate level.

### Relationship with the Securitate

The Miliția handled visible public order and ordinary criminal matters. The Securitate (Departamentul
Securității Statului, DSS) handled political intelligence and repression. The two operated in
parallel, both reporting through the Interior Ministry. [cite:14] In practice, the Securitate
frequently co-opted Miliția manpower for arrests and surveillance. [cite:14]

A suspicious-death investigation touching on Decree 770 (which was enforced partly by the
Securitate) would almost certainly draw Securitate attention, but the initial file (dosar penal)
would be opened by the Miliția's Investigations Section and referred to the procuror (prosecutor)
for classification. **Inference:** Whether a specific death was handled as a criminal matter by
Miliția alone or was escalated to Securitate involvement would depend on whether it had political
implications. In the book's setup, it begins as a Miliția investigation that has not yet attracted
Securitate attention because it has been classified as an administrative matter — which is
consistent with Munteanu's handling of it.

### Criminal procedure and the dosar penal

Romania's Penal Code was reformed in 1969 under Ceaușescu. The basic unit of criminal
investigation was the **dosar penal** (criminal file). [cite:17] Under Romanian criminal procedure,
the procuror (prosecutor) was involved from the outset in classifying deaths — natural, suspicious,
or criminal — and the Miliția's Investigations Section conducted the physical evidence gathering
under prosecutorial supervision. [cite:17]

**Inference (not confirmed in sources for this specific period):** A suspicious death at a district
hospital — a young woman who arrived mortally ill and died within hours — would generate a hospital
report, a death certificate, and a notification to the local procuror and Miliția. The attending
physician was required to report. The Miliția's Investigations Section would be assigned to
determine whether a crime had occurred (i.e., whether the death resulted from an illegal procedure).
The file would move from the district office to the county inspectorate (Inspectoratul Județean de
Miliție, Cluj County) if it could not be resolved locally or if escalation was required.

**The book's fixed deadline** — after which the file moves from a lieutenant who wants the truth
to an office that wants a name — reflects the real administrative pressure in this system, though
no specific statutory deadline was found in sources consulted. **Inference:** In the communist
administrative culture, a case that remained unresolved beyond a set period (likely 30 days for
an initial investigation) would generate pressure from above to produce a result, and county-level
or central offices would demand a "name on the file."

### Investigative methods available to Munteanu

What a Miliția lieutenant could actually do in November 1984, based on documented organizational
capacity [cite:14]:
- Knock on doors (explicitly: the Miliția's Population Section maintained population registers)
- Demand identity documents (buletin de identitate, see §8)
- Summon individuals to the county Miliția building for questioning
- Collect and compare physical objects (the paper copies in the book)
- Contact the hospital for the patient's admission record and any statement given before death

What a Miliția lieutenant could **not** easily do without Securitate involvement:
- Tap telephones (required Securitate authorization and equipment)
- Run surveillance across multiple neighborhoods simultaneously
- Access Securitate files on individuals without a formal request

## 6. The Securitate Informer Network at Neighborhood and Workplace Level

### Scale

By the mid-1980s: **approximately 11,000–13,000 full Securitate agents and approximately 450,000
informants** across a population of 22 million. [cite:15], [cite:16] One summary formulation: one
person in every thirty Romanians had been recruited as an informant by the 1980s. [cite:16]

The network grew significantly over time: approximately 12,000–13,000 informants in 1958; 30,000 by
1960; 80,000 by 1963; 110,000 by 1967. By the 1980s, the figure had reached several hundred
thousand. [cite:15]

### The shift from repression to social control

In the 1970s and 1980s, the Securitate **shifted from open violent repression to pervasive
monitoring.** [cite:16] Its primary function in the 1980s was to track and report to party
leadership on "economic, social, ethnic, and political grievances" — the state of mind of the
population. [cite:15] The fear this created was pervasive but not usually spectacular. As one
source puts it: the defining atmosphere was "fear rather than terror." [cite:16]

### Recruitment and coercion

Informers came from all social categories: neighbors, coworkers, students, family members. [cite:16]
Blackmail was the primary recruitment tool — discovering a vulnerability (an illegal act, a foreign
contact, a moral "irregularity") and threatening disclosure. [cite:16] The Securitate also used
appeals to civic duty, framing informing as voluntary participation in the protection of the state.
[cite:15]

Close friends were among the most productive informers; many Romanians discovered from their
post-1989 files that intimate friends had been reporting on them for years. [cite:16]

### What informers reported

Informers were required to produce written reports covering: who a target met with, what places
they visited, what was said about the regime, the country's economy, and its leadership. [cite:16]
In the 1980s context, the Securitate was particularly interested in: signs of collective organizing,
contact with foreigners, illegal economic activity (black market, hoarding), and — for the
Decree 770 enforcement — reproductive violations.

### How ordinary people managed surveillance risk

**No primary-source account of specific individual risk-management strategies was found** in the
sources consulted. What the sources confirm:

- People refrained from criticizing Ceaușescu in public, aware that anyone could be an informer.
  [cite:16]
- "Private space in apartment buildings could easily turn into public space," meaning neighbors
  could report on what happened inside flats. [cite:15]
- The telephone was a risk. Phones were tapped; the Securitate intercepted mail; typewriters were
  registered. [cite:16]
- The assumption that any neighbor might be reporting was sufficiently pervasive that it structured
  ordinary social behavior. [cite:16]

**Inference:** In the context of the book — women sharing information about aftercare — the most
rational behavior would be: no telephone, no written list, face-to-face only, strictly limited
disclosure (only people already known and trusted), and the assumption that any transaction that
became visible to a neighbor or building superintendent (locatar de bloc) was potentially reported.
Doina's silence about the woman on the bus — refusing to name her — is precisely the behavior
the surveillance system trained ordinary Romanians to adopt as rational self-protection.

## 7. The Factory Bookkeeper: Communist Accounting and the Ledger

### The accounting system under communism

In Romania's centrally planned economy, enterprise accounting served the state, not the enterprise.
[cite:17] As documented in academic analyses of Romanian communist-era accounting: "Accounting
information was exclusively prepared for the state, and public disclosure was non-existent." [cite:17]
The enterprise's accounts were submitted to the Ministry of Finance and the State Planning Committee;
they were instruments of plan verification, not business management.

The principle that governed the work: **"Accountants cannot journalize anything in accounting unless
they have a primary document."** [cite:17] This emphasis on documentary evidence was fundamental to
the communist system and persisted into the post-communist period as an institutional legacy. The
significance for the book: every transaction required a supporting document; a bookkeeper who kept
clean records was legally protected; a bookkeeper whose records had gaps was exposed.

### Role of the enterprise bookkeeper (contabil)

Under communism, accounting at Romanian state enterprises was "largely a matter of clerical
bookkeeping." [cite:17] The chief accountant (contabil-șef) was responsible for the accuracy of
the enterprise's books and for producing records on demand for state auditors and inspectors.
[cite:17] The enterprise had no ability to withhold records from the state; the books were, in
principle, government property.

**What an enterprise bookkeeper was legally required to produce on demand:**

- Production plan fulfillment records
- Payroll registers (stat de plată)
- Material consumption records
- Inventory registers
- Cost-per-unit calculations

**Inference:** The specific forms and ledger names in use at a Romanian textile enterprise in 1984
are not documented in the English-language sources consulted. Romanian communist accounting used
standardized forms issued by the Ministry of Finance, updated periodically through the Five-Year
Plans. A bookkeeper like Ilinca, responsible for the accounts of a textile mill (fabrica de
textile/industrie textilă) in Dej, would have maintained the following categories: production
records, wage/salary registers, material input/output, and annual balance submitted to the county
financial office. A state audit could arrive without notice and demand any record going back up to
five years. The ledger Ilinca uses to hide the master document is a ledger she is "legally required
to produce on demand" — this is accurate to the actual exposure: the ledger is not a private object.

### The bookkeeper's physical workspace

**Inference:** A mill bookkeeper in 1984 would have worked at a desk in the enterprise's
administrative building (separate from the production floor), with access to a telephone (a risk),
a typewriter (registered with the Securitate by serial number), and the physical ledgers and forms.
Calculations were done by hand or, in larger enterprises, on a mechanical adding machine. The
accountant's office smelled of paper, carbon copy paper, and the mill production (synthetic fiber
mills produce a distinctive chemical smell). No source confirmed the specific physical layout or
equipment of the AFE or its associated textile facilities.

## 8. Names, Address Forms, and Period Vocabulary

### Surname structure

Romanian surnames standardized in the nineteenth century from patronymic forms. Common suffixes:
**-escu** ("child of," as in Petrescu = "child of Petru"); **-eanu/-anu** (place-based, as in
Munteanu = "man from the mountains/munte"); **-aru/-ată** (occupational); **-oiu** (Slavic
influence). [cite:18], [cite:19]

Male and female versions of the same surname are often identical in modern usage, though some
surnames have gender-inflected forms. [cite:18]

**Validation of the book's surnames:**
- **Roșca**: Romanian adjective *roș* (red); common Romanian surname. [cite:18]
- **Petrescu**: -escu suffix from Petru; one of the most common Romanian surnames. [cite:19]
- **Munteanu**: from *munte* (mountain); very common throughout Romania, especially in Transylvania. [cite:18]
- **Marcu**: Romanian form of Marcus/Mark; used as both given name and, occasionally, surname. [cite:18]

### Given names

**Female given names** almost always end in -a. [cite:18] Traditional names come from the Orthodox
saint calendar. [cite:18]

**Validation of the book's given names:**
- **Ilinca**: Romanian diminutive of Elena/Ileana; documented in use. [cite:23]
- **Doina**: Romanian given name meaning "folk song" (a distinctively Romanian lyric form); used as
  a given name, especially in Transylvania. [cite:23]
- **Ana**: directly from the Orthodox calendar (Saint Anne); extremely common in Romania. [cite:18]

**Male given names** end in a consonant. [cite:18]

**Validation:**
- **Vasile**: Romanian form of Basil (Vasiliu in some older forms); major Orthodox saint's name;
  very common in Romania. [cite:18]

### Forms of address

Standard formal address: **Domn/Domnul** (Mr.) + surname for a man; **Doamnă** (Mrs./Madam) +
surname for a woman. [cite:22] These forms were retained through communism for civilian interactions.

**Inference:** In the communist context, the forms "tovarăș" (comrade, m.) and "tovarășă" (comrade,
f.) were also in official use, especially in party/workplace settings. A Miliția officer might
address a woman he was questioning as "Tovarășă [Surname]" in a formal setting, or "Doamnă
[Surname]" in a more civil interaction. No source confirmed the specific preference between these
forms for a 1984 Miliția interrogation setting; the distinction is an inference.

### Other period vocabulary

**Buletin**: the identity card (buletin de identitate), issued by the Miliția, 8-page booklet format
in the communist era, containing photograph, occupation, and registration stamps. Required to be
carried at all times. [cite:22]

**Bloc**: the standard communist-era apartment block; residents were organized in bloc committees,
with a building superintendent (administrator de bloc). The bloc as social unit was also a
surveillance unit. [cite:15]

**Cartelă**: the ration card. Issued by county, valid for one month or three months depending on
region. Non-transferable; losing it meant forfeiting the ration. [cite:09]

**Contabil-șef**: chief accountant at a state enterprise, the senior bookkeeper responsible for
all records. [cite:17]

**Dosar**: file. **Dosar penal**: criminal file, the unit of a Miliția investigation. [cite:17]

**Procuror**: state prosecutor, involved from the outset of any criminal matter in classifying
the nature of the death or incident. [cite:17]

**Inspectoratul Județean de Miliție**: the county-level Miliția office, to which district cases
were escalated. [cite:14]

**Locotenent**: lieutenant; the rank of the book's investigator Munteanu, in the lower-middle of
the officer hierarchy. [cite:14]

**Tovarăș/tovarășă**: comrade; the official form of address in institutional and party contexts.
**Inference**: not from a specific source but consistent with documented communist-era Romanian usage.

## Disputed or Uncertain Points

1. **The timeline for escalating a criminal file from district to county level.** No source
   confirmed the specific number of days before a dosar penal had to be moved up or resolved.
   The book uses a 31-day deadline. This is plausible but not confirmed.

2. **Whether a Miliția lieutenant at district level in Dej would handle a Decree 770 death alone
   or would immediately involve Securitate.** The sources establish the two organizations worked
   in parallel but do not specify the trigger for Securitate involvement in an individual suspicious
   death. The book's premise (that Munteanu is working the case himself, with Securitate as a
   future escalation risk) is plausible given the documented division of labor.

3. **The specific forms and ledgers a textile-enterprise bookkeeper would have maintained.** No
   English-language primary source was found for the exact documentary requirements. The general
   principle (on-demand production required; primary documents for every transaction) is documented.

4. **The exact fuel and heating conditions inside the AFE and associated textile enterprise in Dej
   in November 1984.** The general austerity conditions are well-documented; the specific
