# World — The Ninth Line

## Period and location

November 1984. Dej, Cluj County, Transylvania, Romania. A municipal town of approximately 36,000 people, 60 kilometres north of Cluj-Napoca, at the confluence of the Someșul Mic and Someșul Mare rivers. The two Someșuri join four kilometres upstream at the village of Mica and arrive at Dej as one river, wide and flat, carrying the cold of the Transylvanian plateau downhill toward Satu Mare.

## The town

Dej is a mill town in the late stages of communist industrialisation. The dominant employer is the Artificial Fiber Enterprise (Intreprinderea de Fibre Artificiale — AFE), which employs roughly 1,500 workers producing celofiber, a cellulose-based synthetic fiber used in downstream textile manufacturing. The mill keeps two shifts. The administrative building sits apart from the production floor; the factory physician's office is two doors down from the personnel office, and both shifts of female workers above fifteen are examined monthly.

The town is served by a district hospital (Spitalul Orășenesc Dej), a county Miliția station, a procuror's office in the same building, and the usual apparatus of bloc-committee surveillance: building superintendents (administrator de bloc) who know whose light is on at 2 a.m. and who came home at what hour.

## Physical texture — November 1984

**Light and heat.** District heating comes on when it comes on. On the nights it does not, the unheated stairwells of apartment blocks are colder than the street. One 40-watt bulb per household is the effective norm — not a legal limit, the result of a 50% energy-consumption reduction decree and chronic shortage. Indoor electric heaters are banned; a neighbour who reports one to the bloc committee can cut power to the whole building. Darkness falls by 4:30 p.m. Night-shift workers walk to the factory in the dark and walk home in the dark.

**Food.** The ration card (cartelă) is issued monthly. Documented 1984 allowances: 300 g bread per day; 500 g pork or beef per month; one kilogram chicken per month; eight to twelve eggs; 100 g butter; one litre oil; one kilogram sugar; 500 g cheese. Actual availability is frequently lower. Coffee has been replaced with nechezol — chicory, chickpeas, and barley — and nobody pretends to like it. Queues form before dawn for bread, for eggs, for the occasional van selling freezer-burned chickens. Hoarding beyond the ration has been a criminal offence since 1981.

**Weather.** Daytime high in early November: 8–11°C. Nights approaching or below zero. The Someș valley collects fog on still mornings. The AFE's wet-heat production processes add steam and chemical haze where the valley fog has already settled. By late November, frost and the possibility of early snow. Rain is frequent, 40–58 mm for the month. The outdoor cold is a constant background condition: the kind of cold that makes unheated rooms feel like the outside continued indoors.

**Paper and hands.** Carbon paper, mechanical adding machines, ledgers in standardized Ministry of Finance format. Typewriters are registered with the Securitate by serial number. A telephone is a risk. Writing a list is a risk. The act of not writing things down is its own kind of record-keeping.

## Enforcement atmosphere

Decree 770 (1966) banned abortion and contraception; Decree 770 and its successor instruments are enforced by a layered apparatus. All women of childbearing age (15–45) are subject to mandatory monthly gynecological examinations at their place of employment. Detected pregnancies are tracked from detection through delivery. Every physician is legally required to notify the procuror of any abortion-related intervention within 24 hours; physicians who fail face imprisonment. Securitate informants are stationed in or near hospital operating theatres. In Cluj County alone, 54 doctors have been recruited as informants.

The result: a woman who arrives at the district hospital with complications from an illegal procedure faces bedside interrogation before treatment is authorized. The documented practice includes withholding treatment until the woman names who helped her. The timing of the decision to go to the hospital — how long to wait at home before the risk of staying exceeded the risk of going — is the decision that kills women. That is what the paper is for.

At the neighbourhood level: approximately one in thirty Romanians has been recruited as an informant by the 1980s. The defining atmosphere is fear rather than terror — pervasive, structural, not usually spectacular. Private space in apartment blocks is effectively public. Close friends have been reporting on friends for years. Nobody mentions Ceaușescu critically in a space where a neighbour can hear.

## The Miliția

Civilian police force under the Interior Ministry, modelled on the Soviet Militsiya. Three branches: Territorial, Railroad, Prison. Each district office has a Population Section (population registers, identity documents), an Investigations Section (criminal files, dosar penal), and an Information Office. The Securitate operates in parallel; a Miliția case that acquires political dimensions can be co-opted. Lieutenant-rank officers handle district-level criminal investigations, reporting to a captain or higher at the Inspectoratul Județean de Miliție (county Miliția inspectorate) in Cluj-Napoca. Identity documents: the buletin de identitate, an 8-page booklet issued and stamped by the Miliția, required to be carried at all times.

## Period vocabulary in use

- **Cartelă** — ration card
- **Buletin** — identity booklet
- **Dosar (penal)** — (criminal) file
- **Procuror** — state prosecutor
- **Contabil-șef** — chief accountant at a state enterprise
- **Bloc** — apartment block, also a surveillance unit
- **Tovarăș/Tovarășă** — Comrade (m./f.); official address in institutional contexts
- **Nechezol** — the coffee substitute
- **Locotenent** — Lieutenant (Miliția rank)
