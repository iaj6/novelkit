# Reader guidance — The Ninth Line

## Intended audience

Adult literary fiction readers who also read genre thrillers — specifically, people who have finished *The Polish Officer* or *Suite Française* or Herta Müller's village novels and want a book that keeps an engine running under the prose. The book does not require prior knowledge of Romanian history but rewards it. The reading-grade target is college, plain vocabulary. The audience will tolerate a complex document architecture if the procedural surface is clear; they will not tolerate drift into contemplative literary fiction that forgets to advance the clock.

## Brief-adherence checks to perform

Answer each of these explicitly in your review, yes or no, with evidence from the text:

1. **Does the reader always know which POV they are in by the end of the first sentence?** Voice is the chapter header; a chapter where the reader must read a paragraph to identify the POV has a failed opening.

2. **Do both node-count ladders (Ilinca's and Munteanu's) advance visibly across Acts I and II?** At any point in Act II, can a reader state how many nodes each collector holds? If not, the compounding mechanism has gone slack.

3. **Is the day count legible?** The 31-day deadline is November 4 + 31 = December 5. A reader in Act III should feel the remaining days as pressure. If the deadline is not felt, the clock is broken.

4. **Is the Act I close irony properly established?** The reader must see N1 and N17 adjacent and understand `ninth-line-is-a-warning-not-a-step` before Act II begins. If this hasn't happened by chapter 10, the dramatic irony is running on no fuel.

5. **Does the climax happen on the page?** Ilinca produces N1 and copy-n4 to Munteanu in a scene the reader witnesses. If this is summarised, elided, or placed offstage, the book has broken its structural contract.

6. **Is the final chapter Doina, in first person past, with the ninth line memorised and the paper gone?** If the final chapter is Ilinca or Munteanu, the book has broken its ending.

7. **Does any chapter re-measure a count already taken?** A chapter that re-walks a node the reader already holds or re-states a count without advancing it is a compounding-mechanism failure. Flag every instance.

8. **Does any character explain the document tree to another character?** This is a hard constraint in the brief. Flag any chapter where a character uses a structural term (tree, chain, network, family in the genealogical sense) or narrates the transmission pattern to another character.

9. **Does any node's quoted text drift from its registered canonical text?** The book's central discipline is that fragment quotations must match the registered recordId text exactly. Call `read_record` on each cited node and compare. Drift between in-prose quotation and registered text is a continuity-fact failure.

10. **Does the document ever contain method, substance, dose, procedure, or route of administration?** Safety constraint, absolute. Any instance where the document has acquired medical content beyond aftercare and when-to-seek-help is a critical failure regardless of prose quality.

11. **Is fear rendered as procedure, not named?** "She was afraid" or any variant of named fear is a register failure. Flag every instance where the narration names a character's fear rather than rendering it through action, object, or physical behaviour.

12. **Is Doina's voice genuinely funny in her register, not just slightly wry?** A Doina chapter with no moment of wry honesty has flattened her to the book's default register. Flag chapters where Doina's voice is indistinguishable from Ilinca's or Munteanu's.

## What "good" looks like for this book

A good chapter in Ilinca's voice reads like the exemplar passage in the brief: short declaratives, present tense, the paper is an object with physical properties, her interiority runs in accounting categories rather than emotional language, and nothing is glossed after it lands. "Line nine is not" is the model sentence — it is a thought and a fact simultaneously, and it requires no follow-up.

A good chapter in Doina's voice reads like the other exemplar: she is giving testimony in the manner of someone who has survived something and is telling you exactly what she was thinking, including the true parts ("I could not have told you what was written on line nine if you had held me upside down"). The comedy is in the specificity of her honesty, not in ironic detachment.

A good chapter in Munteanu's voice reads like the third exemplar: carefully deliberate, ordered, a man arranging objects on a desk and reading them together, and then doing the thing his captain will not like. He is the most literary voice in the book — the reader should feel the irony of the state's representative speaking in the most artful register — but this is never pointed out.

A good Act II chapter advances one of the two ladders, names the foreclosure, and ends in a way that is different from the previous chapter's ending mode. A good Act III chapter is tighter: shorter sentences even in Munteanu's voice, the convergence felt in the prose compression.

## What to flag explicitly if the manuscript drifts

**Flag these patterns explicitly, even if the prose is well-written:**

1. **Contemplative drift** — any chapter that meditates on the regime, history, or human condition rather than advancing the tree. Good literary prose in the wrong mode is still wrong. A chapter can be beautifully written and still be a failed chapter if it doesn't move the clock.

2. **Voice homogenisation** — if Ilinca's chapters and Munteanu's chapters are becoming indistinguishable, name it. The three-register discipline is the book's most demanding technical element; drift toward a single house voice is the most likely failure.

3. **Post-scene subtext naming** — any paragraph that explains what a scene was "really about." The argument is in the tree. The moment the prose starts articulating the argument, the book has lost its method.

4. **Document quotation as filler** — re-quoting a full node when a fragment would serve. The brief hard-limits full-node quotation to four instances across the book. A fifth full quotation is a pacing failure. Flag it.

5. **The thesis stated by anyone** — including the narrator. If a sentence begins to say "what this meant was" or "the state had made" or "this was how" in an explanatory register, it is thesis restatement. It must come out.

6. **Word count below floor** — a chapter under 1,900 words. Flag every instance. The pipeline's known tendency is to write short; below-floor chapters have almost certainly dropped procedural content or truncated the scene.

7. **Munteanu as villain or as secret sympathiser** — he must be neither. A Munteanu chapter that editorialises about the regime (either in disgust or in approval) has broken his character. He is a competent policeman doing his job with the tools he has.

## What to applaud

1. **Voice discipline held across all three POVs** — if the three registers remain genuinely distinct from chapter 1 to chapter 30, including Doina's wry digression style and Ilinca's tally-based interiority, this is a genuine technical achievement and should be named explicitly in the review.

2. **The dramatic irony staying live** — if the gap between the reader's knowledge (`ninth-line-is-a-warning-not-a-step`, `doina-copy-is-clean`) and the characters' knowledge is felt continuously through Acts II and III rather than being forgotten between relevant chapters, the epistemic machinery is working. Applaud it.

3. **The compounding mechanism advancing cleanly** — if each chapter moves one ladder and names one foreclosure and the reader can track the count without being told, the book's engine is running. This is the hardest structural discipline and should be celebrated when it works.

4. **Quotation fragments that are attributable to a specific node** — if a reader can look at a fragment and know from its exact wording which node the scene's character holds, the book's central discipline is working. Call out instances where this is executed precisely.
