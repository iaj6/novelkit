# Pitch

## The Cold Signal

Antarctica, June 1991. Dr. Elena Hartmann — sixty-four years old, astrobiologist, thirty years of career built on calling nothing real until it was — receives a forty-seven-second signal in the receiver hut at Winslow Station, a small, isolated USAP outpost near the Weddell Sea coast. The signal is not terrestrial. It is not natural. It is not the kind of pattern noise throws even once in a hundred million years.

It also came once and will not repeat.

What remains is a magnetic tape, a printout, and Elena's ability to re-run her verification protocol — three things now dying on a clock she did not set. The generator is failing; power for instrument runs is finite. The tape and printout are the only copy, fragile in a hut sliding toward minus-forty. And in roughly six days there is a single mid-winter communications window — the SSB net to the sister station, the one weather-window uplink to the relay — that can carry the data out of her hands and into the institutional machinery, or not. That window does not come again before her team lands on Day 12. Once the LC-130 arrives, the data, the framing, and the credit are no longer hers to decide, ever.

So every day is both the last day the choice is hers and a day the record is more at risk. Eleven days. A window closing on her hands. And Elena, who has spent a career talking colleagues out of premature announcements, must now decide what to do with the one signal she has been waiting thirty years to find.
