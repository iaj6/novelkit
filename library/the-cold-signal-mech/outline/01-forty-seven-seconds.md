# Chapter 01 — Forty-Seven Seconds

**One-line summary:** Elena receives a forty-seven-second signal in the receiver hut; runs Protocol Run 1; the clock starts.

**POV:** Dr. Elena Hartmann  
**Tense:** Third-person limited past  
**Setting:** Winslow Station — receiver hut, then briefly the main hab. Night, June 1991. Day 1 of polar darkness (third night of darkness overall, per canon).  
**Target words:** ~3,200  
**Chapter shape:** Bottle — single location (receiver hut), near real-time over approximately four hours  
**Ending mode:** Turn

## Goal

Establish Elena, the equipment, the receiver hut, and the routine of her solitude — then break it. The reader must finish this chapter knowing: (1) who Elena is and what her career cost her; (2) what the signal is and why it is unambiguous; (3) what three quantities are already in motion; (4) that this is a chapter about a woman beginning to understand the weight of what she is holding. Do not defer the clock to Chapter 2.

---

## Setting Details

The receiver hut: a narrow outbuilding reached from the main hab by a covered walkway. The smell of diesel exhaust seeping through the umbilical conduit. The amber numerals of the signal analyzer glowing in the dark. The space heater rattling. A single work chair, the equipment rack, the reel-to-reel tape receivers mounted above, a strip of notes taped to the frame. It is cold enough inside that Elena's breath is visible when she first enters.

The main hab (end of chapter): her bunk with the sleeping bag unzipped and folded. The galley mug. The supply shelf. The SSB radio's red standby light.

---

## Obstacle

The generator's voltage regulator fault — already known to Elena before the chapter opens — creates the first crisis: a brownout occurs during Protocol Run 1, causing a three-second power drop in the middle of the instrument run. Elena does not know whether the run is still valid or whether she needs to start over.

---

## Action Beats

1. **Opening in motion — routine work.** Elena is in the receiver hut, doing the late-evening scan she has done at roughly this time every clear night for six weeks. The team left this morning (or last night — fix the exact departure in continuity notes below). She establishes her equipment with the economy of someone who knows it without looking at it. Brief, precise establishment of the three machines: the antenna feed processor, the reel-to-reel tape recorder, the signal analyzer. The heater is on; she notes the power gauge's baseline reading. She is comfortable in this solitude.

2. **The signal arrives.** The analyzer's output changes — not dramatically, but precisely. A 47-second event in a specific band, with a particular structure that is not what noise looks like, not what interference looks like, not what the station's own RF sources look like. Elena watches it happen in real time on the analyzer display. She does not move.

3. **First-pass elimination.** Before she does anything else, she checks — methodically, in order — the possible terrestrial and natural explanations: her own station's transmitters (all quiet; she logged off the SSB an hour ago), the frequency band's known interference sources (cross-referenced against her log), natural phenomena (she rules out each in sequence, not by intuition but by procedure). This elimination takes about twenty minutes. She is not rushing. She is also not able to stop.

4. **She prepares Protocol Run 1.** Pulls the tape back, sets the analyzer's input parameters, checks the printout paper feed. The protocol is hers: she wrote the first version of it in 1983 and has revised it four times since. Running it is as familiar as breathing. It will take approximately twenty minutes.

5. **The brownout.** Midway through the run — at about the eleven-minute mark — the generator drops output for three seconds. The analyzer's display flickers; the reel slows and recovers. Elena sits absolutely still while it happens. When the power stabilizes, she checks the run's log. The run completed, but the three-second window is flagged as degraded data.

6. **She decides not to start over.** The flagged window is not in the signal's core parametric region; it is in the warm-up calibration segment before the signal's actual event. She can argue — correctly — that the run is valid. She makes a note in the log and accepts the result.

7. **The result.** The signal is anomalous: non-terrestrial, non-natural, not a pattern that probability throws even once in a hundred million years of instrument time. The analyzer's output sits in front of her on the printout. She reads it three times. Then she sets the printout down on the work surface face-up, like a document whose character has just changed.

8. **The clock enters consciousness.** She does not celebrate. She calculates. The tape: the sole physical record, in this hut, at this temperature. The printout: in her hand, but fragile. The instrument: 7 runs remaining in her estimated power budget. The generator's voltage regulator fault: one more brownout like that one costs her more margin than she can afford to spend casually. And the relay window: she counts back from the scheduled date. Six days. Maybe six and a half.

9. **She does not sleep.** She goes to the main hab — briefly, for water, for a moment of warmth — and stands in the galley with the mug going cold. She looks at the red standby light of the SSB radio. She does not pick it up. She goes back to the receiver hut.

10. **End of chapter.** She is sitting in the work chair with the printout in her lap and the signal analyzer's amber numbers still on the display from the run. The heater ticks. She is not thinking about what it means. She is thinking about what she has to do next, and in what order, and with how much power.

---

## Outcome / Turn

The turn is the signal itself and Elena's recognition of what she is holding. The chapter ends not in triumph but in the weight of a woman who has spent thirty years not saying things until they were real, now holding the one thing she cannot yet say.

---

## New Question / Pressure Raised

Who does she tell, and when? And: can she trust the tape and the power long enough to prove it again before she has to answer that question?

## Continuity Notes

- **Team departure:** The five other station members left Winslow "on the night of the signal or the following morning" (continuity.md §6). For dramatic clarity, establish them as having departed the previous morning or very early this morning — Elena has been alone for approximately one full day before the signal arrives, establishing the solitude without making it immediate.
- **Day numbering:** Day 1 is the night of the signal (continuity.md §2). The team is twelve days away (continuity.md §7).
- **Instrument run margin initial estimate:** 8 full runs available within the power budget (this is Elena's estimate before the fuel-viscosity discovery in Ch 5; it will be revised downward). Introduce this as an estimate, not a fact, to allow the Ch 5 correction to land.
- **Generator fault:** already in place before the chapter opens; Elena knows about it. The brownout in this chapter is not a surprise, but its timing — during Run 1 — is.
- **The signal's content:** never named, never described. The prose may describe the signal's shape (its duration, the structure of its frequency sweep, the parameters that make it unambiguous), never what it encodes or whether it encodes anything.
- **No second transmission:** establish clearly that the signal came once and that Elena knows — by the nature of her equipment and monitoring — that it is not repeating and will not.
