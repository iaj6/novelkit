## Goal

The final chapter is not a coda. The brief specifies: the team's arrival is the moment the decision's consequences become real in a different register — social, professional, institutional. This chapter is about the crossing of that threshold. It is short because the book does not have more room; the days are done. At least one thread must remain unresolved at the chapter's end.

---

## Obstacle

Six people walking into the station she has held alone for twelve days. The social re-entry is itself the obstacle: five other people's warmth and noise and assumptions and questions pressing into the space that has been hers and the signal's and no one else's. Elena must navigate this without either concealing everything or blurting everything. She does not do either.

## Action Beats

**Arrival**

1. 1240. Elena is already outside when she hears the engines — the four-prop drone of the Hercules, distant at first, then coming in from the northwest. She can hear it before she can see it. She stands on the snow in the full cold-weather kit, the way she always stands when a plane is coming in.

2. The LC-130 makes one low pass to check the landing strip — standard operating procedure. The ski-equipped landing gear comes down on the second approach. The aircraft settles onto the snow; the skis leave two shallow parallel tracks. The engines do not stop immediately; they cycle down slowly.

3. The cargo ramp goes down. Walczak is first out, as he always is. He scans the station before he scans Elena — his engineer's eye going to the antenna array, the equipment building, the receiver hut, the hab. Looking for what's wrong. Then he looks at her.

4. He crosses the ice. The others are behind him — four more people, cargo being offloaded. She knows their faces; they are the faces she has not seen for twelve days.

5. "How's the generator?" Walczak says. The first words. He always asks about the machine first; this is not coldness.

6. She tells him: the voltage regulator fault has been active since Day 3. The power reserve is critical — she shows him the percentage. He should install the new component as soon as the equipment is at temperature. She is accurate and complete about the machine.

7. He looks at her more carefully. He does not ask the next question yet — the cargo is coming off the plane and he is tracking four things at once. He says: "Come inside when the cargo's squared away."

**Inside**

8. The hab. Five people in it, plus Elena. The smell of bodies and cold weather gear and the food they have brought from McMurdo. The particular noise of people who have been in a crowd at McMurdo now filling a space that seats six. It is overwhelming in a way that Elena does not fully expect.

9. The others greet her — brief, warm, the practiced ease of people who have been in the field before and know that re-entry is its own kind of weather. She responds to each. She is present enough. Coffee is made; someone brought fresh food from McMurdo; there is the small celebration of supply.

10. Elena watches Walczak across the room. He is doing the equipment building walkthrough he always does when he returns — the systematic check of everything he left behind. She knows he will get to the receiver hut and see the signal analyzer's run log in the protocol folder. She does not know how long she has before that.

11. She gets there first.

**The equipment building**

12. She goes to the equipment building and picks up the evidence package from where she left it — the tape housing, the wrapped printout, the analysis notebook, the protocol folder. She carries it to the equipment building's work table.

13. Walczak comes in. He sees her at the table. He sees the evidence package. He sees the power gauge, which is reading just above its minimum safe line.

14. She opens the analysis notebook. She does not begin with the evidence summary; she begins with Run 1, Day 1. She sets the notebook on the table in front of him and says, in the voice she uses for exactly this kind of communication — patient, precise, without emphasis — "I need you to read this."

15. Walczak sits down. He reads.

16. The chapter does not show what he reads — the reader has been with Elena through all of it; the notebook contains what we know. What we do not know is what it looks like to another person, reading it for the first time.

17. **The chapter ends before Walczak looks up from the notebook.**

## Outcome / Elliptical (at least one thread unresolved)

The chapter closes at the moment the data passes from Elena's sole possession into another person's attention. The narrative cuts before Walczak's reaction, before his question, before the institutional chain takes the next step. What Elena has done with the data is now known: she has kept it, she has documented it, and she has given it — all of it, the tape and the printout and the notebook and the protocol runs — to Walczak in the first private moment after the team's arrival.

**The deliberately unresolved thread:** what will the world make of what she has handed him? Whether the claim survives peer review, whether Mather Station's corroborating data is ever properly examined, what happens when the agencies receive the information, whether the second parametric segment can be reconstructed from the tape under controlled conditions — all of this is past the chapter's close and past the novel's reach. The book ends at the moment of transfer, not at the moment of reception.

## Craft Notes for the Drafter

- The arrival must be written with the sensory specificity of someone who has been alone for twelve days and is suddenly not: the smell of other people's cold weather gear, the noise of multiple voices, the specific warmth of more bodies in the hab's thermal economy. Elena is not hostile to this; she is simply recalibrating after being alone.
- Walczak's first words ("How's the generator?") are his. They are not emotionally cold — they are the professional vocabulary of someone who knows Elena and knows that asking about the machine is how you ask if everything is okay. The drafter should let the reader feel this without stating it.
- The chapter does **not** describe the signal's content — not in the notebook, not in what Elena says to Walczak, not in any form. She opens to Run 1 and says "I need you to read this." The prose does not render what he reads.
- The **elliptical cut** before Walczak looks up is the novel's last sentence or very nearly so. It must be absolute — the chapter does not add a reflective coda after the cut. The cut is the end.
- The **face-down page** (from Chapter 13) is folded into the notebook; Walczak will encounter it when he reads. Its presence in the notebook without explanation is one element of the deliberately unresolved texture of the ending.
