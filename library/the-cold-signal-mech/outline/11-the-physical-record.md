# Chapter 11 — The Physical Record

**One-line summary:** Elena examines the printout and tape physically; two columns of the printout are ink-degraded and partially illegible; she photographs the printout with the station camera.

**POV:** Dr. Elena Hartmann  
**Tense:** Third-person limited past  
**Setting:** Winslow Station — main hab (for the printout and camera work), receiver hut (for the tape inspection). Day 10.  
**Target words:** ~2,800  
**Chapter shape:** Single scene — one extended activity (systematic physical examination of the artifacts)  
**Ending mode:** Literary fade

## Escalation (from Chapter Map)

- **(a) Day 10**
- **(b)** Printout integrity: two columns ink-degraded from cold contraction — the timestamp column and the calibration reference column are partially illegible, three specific data points unreadable. Tape dropout: visual measurement confirms 18 seconds (up from 12 seconds at Run 4 two days ago). Days to foreclosure: 2. Power reserve: 24 % → 20 % (passive overnight draw; no run today).
- **(c)** Step closer: the printout's chain-of-custody timestamp is partially illegible. The printout can no longer independently verify the receipt time of the signal without the instrument log to corroborate it. ---

## Goal

A quiet chapter in one sense — no instrument run, no radio contact, no external event — but one that reveals the physical toll of eleven days on the evidence base. The chapter's subject is the artifacts themselves: what they look like, what they show, and what they no longer show. Elena is meticulous and patient; this chapter lets the reader see that patience working on a problem that patience cannot fully fix.

---

## Obstacle

The cold has done what cold does to paper and ink. The printout — which Elena has maintained in the warmest accessible location (the equipment cabinet in the hab) — has still degraded, because the hab is not warm by any habitable standard. The ink on thermal paper at sustained cold temperatures becomes brittle and cracks along the printed lines. She cannot uncrack the ink; she can only document the damage before it gets worse.

---

## Action Beats

**Printout examination (main hab)**

1. Elena retrieves the printout from the equipment cabinet — still in its gray thermal blanket. She takes it to the galley table and unrolls it fully, weighting the corners with objects from the shelf: a can, a tool kit, a book.

2. She examines it column by column under the work light. The printout has fourteen columns: frequency, amplitude, time-of-reception (the timestamp column), phase, polarization, signal-to-noise, calibration reference, and seven derived parameter columns produced by the signal analyzer's processing.

3. The damage: **Column 3 (timestamp)** — the time-of-reception column — shows hairline fractures in the ink along three of its numerical lines. Two of the three timestamps are partially illegible; the third is a complete blank where the ink has flaked. The signal's specific time-of-reception — the number that connects this record to the antenna log and the station's timekeeping — is no longer readable from the printout alone.

4. **Column 7 (calibration reference)** — the column that records the signal analyzer's calibration state at the moment of the run — shows similar fracturing in two of its entries. These are not the signal parameters themselves, but the reference values against which the signal parameters are measured. Without them, the printed parameter values cannot be independently verified as accurate without access to the original instrument log.

5. The twelve remaining columns: intact. The signal's frequency distribution, amplitude profile, phase structure, polarization — these are readable. The core anomalous parameters are present and legible. The damage is not to the signal's characteristics but to the chain-of-custody and calibration data that anchors the printout to verifiable science.

6. Elena makes a note in the analysis notebook: which columns are affected, which entries are degraded, which specific values are unreadable. She writes them as precisely as she can: "Column 3 (timestamp): rows 4, 7 partially degraded; row 12 blank. Column 7 (cal ref): rows 2, 9 degraded."

**Photography**

7. She retrieves the station camera from the equipment building — a 35mm SLR used for site documentation and ice surveys. She has eighteen exposures left on the current roll.

8. She photographs the printout systematically: one frame per column, then three frames of the two damaged columns at close range to capture what remains legible. Twelve frames total. She uses the camera carefully — she knows about cold and camera equipment; the mechanism is slightly stiff from the temperature.

9. The photographs exist now. But photographs of a damaged document are evidence of the damage, not a restoration of the data. They show what is there and what is not; they do not supply the missing values.

**Tape inspection (receiver hut)**

10. She goes to the receiver hut. The tape inspection she does not run with the instrument — she measures the dropout manually against the timing marks on the housing, as she has been doing when she cannot afford a full run. The affected zone has grown since Run 4's twelve seconds. By her measurement: **eighteen seconds.** The binder failure is spreading toward the center of the reel.

11. She documents the visual state of the tape surface at the dropout zone: the binder has flaked completely in a narrow band, revealing the underlying tape substrate. The flaking is stable — it is not generating loose debris — but the zone of failure is now wider than the signal's second parametric segment, which is gone, and is beginning to approach the boundary of the core signature region.

12. She calculates: if the dropout grows at a consistent rate of three seconds per day (from the progression she has measured: 5s on Day 4, 8s estimated Day 6, 12s confirmed Day 8, 18s estimated Day 10), the dropout will reach the edge of the core signature region in approximately two to three days. The core signature is not gone yet. But it is the next thing to go if the degradation continues.

13. She considers running a protocol run now — but the power reserve is at 22 % and the instrument run would cost approximately 10–12 % including the stabilization procedure. She decides to hold the remaining two runs for Days 11 and 12.

**Return to hab**

14. She re-rolls the printout carefully and rewraps it in the thermal blanket. She puts it back in the equipment cabinet. She looks at the stack of photographs — exposed, not yet developed (no darkroom at Winslow; the film will go to McMurdo for development when the team arrives).

15. **End of chapter.** She makes her log entry for Day 10: "Printout: Col 3, Col 7 partial degradation. Photographs taken (12 frames). Tape: visual dropout estimate 18s. No instrument run. Power reserve estimate 20%." She puts the notebook away. She does not make tea. She sits in the quiet hab with the red standby light of the SSB radio.

## Outcome / Literary Fade

The literary fade: Elena wrapping the printout in the thermal blanket, the station quiet, the red standby light. The chapter ends with everything documented and nothing fixed. The physical record exists; it is smaller than it was yesterday.

---

## New Question / Pressure Raised

The core signature is two to three days from the dropout's leading edge. Will it survive through the two remaining runs? And: if the printout's timestamp is unreadable, how does Elena establish when the signal was received without recourse to the instrument log — which requires the signal analyzer to verify?

## Continuity Notes

- The **printout damage** (Column 3 timestamp, Column 7 calibration reference) is now canonical. The drafter and subsequent chapters must treat the printout's timestamp as **partially illegible** from this chapter forward. It is not entirely gone; two of three entries are "partially degraded" and one is blank. The core signal parameters are intact.
- The **18-second dropout** (visual estimate, Day 10) will be confirmed as **20 seconds** by the instrument in Chapter 12 (Run 5). The two-second discrepancy between visual estimate and instrument measurement is realistic and should not be treated as an error.
- The **photographs**: twelve frames on unexposed 35mm film. The film cannot be developed at Winslow; it will go to McMurdo for development on Day 12. The photographs exist as potential future documentation, not as present evidence.
- The **station camera** is introduced here as a physical object at Winslow. It should be consistent with 1991-era field equipment: a 35mm SLR with manual settings, subject to cold-related mechanical stiffness.
