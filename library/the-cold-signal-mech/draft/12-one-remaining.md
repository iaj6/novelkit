# One Remaining

She checked the fault log before beginning the stabilization. The main voltage variance column had been full since Day 9; she was writing in the margin now, compressing the notation to fit: timestamp, variance reading, duration in seconds, return-to-nominal time, each excursion on a single line in the narrower script she had developed when the standard column ran out. Five additional entries since the previous evening. Four excursions under thirty seconds. One, at 03:17, that had held for eighty-four seconds before the regulator corrected.

The regulator was still correcting. That was not the problem.

She confirmed the morning gauge reading: plus or minus eight percent. Same as yesterday, and the day before.

The LC-130 was scheduled for approximately 1300 tomorrow. She had one day. She had come to the equipment building at 0730 for the stabilization procedure — not to confirm what the prior four runs had established, but to know the tape's current state before she made any decision about how to present it. Run 5 was what would tell her. She opened the protocol folder to the stabilization log and began.

Heater shed first: the space heater to minimum draw, not off — the tape housing and instrument racks required the base temperature to remain inside the operating range — the thermostat adjusted, the indicator light shifting from its normal operating green to the reduced-draw amber. Then instrument load reduction in the sequence the procedure specified: secondary instruments, the background monitoring feeds running processes not needed for the verification protocol, the processing racks she could bring to standby without disturbing the analyzer's warm-up state. She worked through the list and initialed each item in the stabilization log as she completed it. The signal analyzer held its warm-up mode throughout, its amber numerals steady at their current display values, drawing at the minimum load consistent with readiness.

She set the fifteen-minute timer.

While she waited she took the blank run log form from the protocol folder and wrote the header — *Day 11, Run 5* — and the date in the upper left corner, and set the form on the instrument rack's top shelf where she could reach it without moving from the variance gauge. Then she picked up the fault log and reread the five overnight entries. The 03:17 excursion was the longest she had logged. Not dramatically longer than the ones before it — not a step change, not a new failure mode appearing — but longer. The excursions were not shortening.

She set the fault log down and watched the gauge.

The equipment building held its particular smell this hour: diesel exhaust seeping through the umbilical conduit from the generator on the building's south side, the faint metallic note of the instrument racks' heat exchangers, and underneath both of those the cold itself, which had a smell she could not have named in March but could name now — a dry, compound absence, the smell of air from which almost everything evaporable had been removed. The space heater at reduced draw was inadequate to lift the room more than a degree or two above the ambient. She could feel the temperature in the joints of her fingers.

At nine minutes the gauge had settled to plus or minus four-point-five percent. She noted it in the run log margin.

The generator held its load-hum from behind the insulated wall, the same note it had held on every day of the investigation. She had stopped trying to read the generator by sound before Day 4. The sound was the same whether the variance was at five percent or eight percent or failing into a brownout; the fault log was what told her the truth of it. She had learned the gauge and disregarded the sound.

At fifteen minutes the gauge showed plus or minus five percent. She confirmed the reading and initialed the stabilization log at the fifteen-minute line.

Load restoration in reverse order: monitoring feeds, processing racks, secondary instruments, one increment at a time, a pause between each step to let the reading settle before adding the next increment. The variance climbed a degree at each restoration and then stabilized within thirty seconds of the addition. When the full instrument load was back the gauge read plus or minus six percent — above the stabilization level, below the eight percent the morning gauge had shown. She watched it for two additional minutes. It held.

The power gauge read nineteen percent.

The stabilization procedure had cost roughly three points. The baseline run draw would cost another seven or eight. She wrote *13%* in the run log's reserved power field — her working estimate, which she would revise if the actual draw came in materially different. One instrument run remaining after this one. She would use it tomorrow morning, before the 1300 arrival.

She initiated the run sequence.

---

The warm-up cycle ran at its standard interval. She had timed it on the prior four runs and knew its pace; she did not count it now. The display worked through the diagnostic progression and held at the pre-run state, the instrument ready, the recording initialized.

The tape loaded. Feed tension nominal. She confirmed the position indicators and initiated playback.

Silence.

The opening seconds of the signal had been silence since Run 3 — the binder failure at the reel edge of the tape, the leading edge of the recording degraded and growing since Day 4. She tracked the opening interval mentally against the established timing, not expecting a fixed number but needing to know what it had become. Five seconds. Seven seconds. Nine.

At nine seconds the trace came in: the frequency characteristics of the first parametric segment, arriving at the established onset, not building gradually but present — the same abrupt appearance the prior four runs had each shown, the signal beginning as it had begun each time she had replayed it. She picked up the pen and wrote: *Reel-edge interval: ~9s.*

One second beyond the eight-second estimates from Days 6 and 7. She noted it and returned her attention to the display.

The first parametric segment was playing. The analyzer showed the frequency distribution she had confirmed across four previous runs: the primary anomalous parameters at their established values, the trace moving through the signal's characteristic structure with the same fidelity she had seen each time. The values were there. She watched the display and did not let herself conclude anything she had not already concluded. The conclusion had been established across four runs. A fifth run could confirm it or contradict it.

It confirmed.

She tracked the duration of the clean region against the established timing.

Then silence.

She had expected this silence. Run 4 had found a twelve-second gap at the transition to the second parametric segment; she knew the gap was there, knew it would be larger than on Run 4, and did not know by precisely how much. She watched the counter. The display dropped to baseline at the transition timing she recognized.

Five seconds. Eight. Ten. Twelve.

She wrote *12s+* in the margin.

The display remained at baseline. Thirteen seconds. Fifteen. The analyzer was reading correctly — she had confirmed the tape position and the instrument state before initiating playback. The gap had simply not ended.

Seventeen seconds. Nineteen.

At twenty seconds the calibration tail began: the reference trace, the instrument's own reference signal embedded at the close of the forty-seven-second recording, intact and reading at the established characteristics she had confirmed on each prior run. She confirmed it and let it complete.

*Signal duration: 47 seconds. Run 5 complete.*

She wrote on the run log:

*Gap (transition region → cal. tail onset): 20 seconds.*
*Run 4 (Day 8) gap: 12 seconds. Growth: 8 seconds in 3 days. Rate: ~2.7 s/day.*

She looked at the figure. Eight seconds of growth in three days. At that rate, the failure zone would require four to five more days to reach the core signature region — the portion of the first parametric segment she had confirmed clean across every run since Run 1. Four to five days. Tomorrow was one day. The core signature would survive through tomorrow morning's run.

She checked the power gauge: the needle sat between twelve and thirteen percent. She finalized the run log entry with *~13%* in the reserved field.

*Instrument run margin: 1.*

She closed the run log and filed it in the protocol folder with Runs 1 through 4.

---

She carried the protocol folder through the walkway to the main hab. The corridor hatch lifted at the handle with its established resistance — she had stopped thinking of it as resistance and had started thinking of it as the correct amount of pressure — and she pulled the door shut behind her. The hab was four degrees warmer than the equipment building. She hung her outer layer on the hook by the door.

She put water on for tea, not because she wanted it but because her hands needed a task while she gathered what she needed, and the kettle was what the galley provided for the purpose. She took the analysis notebook from the equipment cabinet and carried it to the galley table.

The notebook held the complete record of the investigation: the initial detection and protocol initiation entry from the night of June first; the run logs for Runs 1 through 4; the housing inspection cards, one per inspection, filed in order; the analysis notebook entry she had written after Run 4, in the language of a paper claim, distinguishing what could be supported from what could not; the Mather Station corroboration entry from the day after Lars's SSB contact, ending on its own line with *Status: inaccessible without disclosure.* It was not the investigation she had planned in March. She had planned to accumulate a formal submission draft in staged sections, filing each section at the relay windows as she completed verification runs. There had been one relay window. The investigation had produced what it had produced. The notebook was the record of that — not of what she had designed, but of what had occurred.

She was going to write the evidence summary in the language she would use in a paper. Not log notation. Not the abbreviated entries she had been making in the daily instrument records. The careful, qualified prose of a claim, precisely calibrated to what the data supported and what it did not, for a reader who had not been present in this station during these eleven days. This was the most important document she would produce at Winslow. She would write it correctly.

The water boiled. She poured it, left the mug on the counter, and picked up the pen.

*Day 11. Evidence summary.*

She began with what existed. The magnetic tape: physical condition as of Day 11 morning — reel-edge binder failure interval confirmed by instrument measurement at approximately nine seconds (Run 5 this morning), transition gap confirmed by instrument measurement at twenty seconds (Run 5), core signature in the first parametric segment intact and confirmed across five instrument runs, second parametric segment unrecoverable as of Run 4 Day 8. The printout: physical condition as of Day 10 physical examination — columns 3 (timestamp) and 7 (calibration reference) partially degraded, columns 1–2 and 4–6 and 8–14 intact, core signal parameters legible in all twenty-three rows of all intact columns; see Day 10 examination record. The analysis notebook: this document. Twelve photographic frames, Roll F, undeveloped, documenting printout physical condition as of Day 10 examination. Instrument run logs for Runs 1 through 5, filed in the protocol folder.

She listed each item without interpretive addition. The existence of each item was the fact.

What the instrument runs had established. The signal's duration: forty-seven seconds, confirmed across Runs 1 through 5. The primary anomalous parameters: confirmed from the first parametric segment across all five runs, values consistent run to run. The signal's non-terrestrial and non-natural character: she chose the phrasing with more attention than she had given to any other sentence in the summary — *the primary anomalous parameters are inconsistent with all terrestrial and natural signal sources within the scope of the five-point verification protocol (1990 revision), confirmed across five instrument runs.* She read the sentence back. *Inconsistent with* was the correct formulation for what the protocol's five-point exclusion procedure established; *indicates non-terrestrial character* would make a stronger logical claim than the scope of the exclusion warranted. She confirmed the formulation and moved to the next section.

What the instrument runs had not established. The complete parametric profile. The second parametric segment, required by the 1990 revision for closing three of the five alternative classifications the protocol was designed to rule out, was unrecoverable from the tape as of Run 4 Day 8. Those three alternative classifications remained formally open.

She read those two sentences back. She did not change them.

The corroborating data. Mather Station, Lars Engström, forty-seven-second anomalous event in the UHF secondary background band, timestamp approximately 2208 local June 1, received fourteen minutes prior to Winslow Station detection at 22:21:52 UTC. Filed as unresolved instrument artifact, probable RFI from maintenance cycle. For the geometry assessment, she wrote first *directed signal received first at the more easterly station* — the intuitive formulation, the one that described what the geometry suggested in ordinary language — then read it and crossed it out. The phrasing implied a propagation geometry she could not formally establish from these two data points without triangulation from both stations' full instrument logs. What the fourteen-minute offset and the sixty-kilometer baseline actually warranted was a narrower statement: that the data were consistent with propagation from a common source. Not more than that, from these numbers alone. She rewrote: *signal propagation from a common source consistent with the fourteen-minute offset and sixty-kilometer station baseline.* She confirmed it.

*Raw data inaccessible without disclosure to Engström. Not independently verified; requires follow-up.*

She looked at the last line for a moment. *Requires follow-up.* She had written it as the accurate notation for the status of the corroborating data; it was also a statement that something had not been done, in a document that Walczak would read, and a reader who worked through the sequence of entries in this notebook would understand why it had not been done. She left it as written.

*What can be claimed: a provisional statement that a signal of anomalous character — duration forty-seven seconds, primary anomalous parameters as specified in the attached run logs — was received at Winslow Station on June 1, 1991, at 22:21:52 UTC in the upper UHF monitoring channel. The primary anomalous parameters are inconsistent with all terrestrial and natural signal sources within the scope of the five-point verification protocol (1990 revision). Corroborating data exists at Mather Station, received fourteen minutes prior to Winslow detection, propagation geometry consistent with a common source (unverified; not communicable without disclosure to the recording researcher). The provisional character of this statement reflects the absence of the complete parametric profile: the second parametric segment, required for closing three of five alternative classifications, is unrecoverable from the tape.*

*What cannot be claimed: a peer-reviewed complete parametric characterization from this tape. The verification protocol's full standard, as specified in the 1990 revision, cannot be satisfied from the available evidence.*

*What one additional instrument run — Run 6 — can do: confirm the current state of the tape and the continued readability of the core signature, and produce a final verification record of the primary anomalous parameters before the chain of custody passes to the arriving team. What Run 6 cannot do: recover the second parametric segment, close the three alternative classifications that require it, or change the provisional character of the claim.*

She set the pen in the notebook's spine.

She read the summary back, paragraph by paragraph, without returning to any paragraph she had already left. Seven minutes. In the corroborating data section she confirmed the geometry sentence was in its corrected form — *propagation from a common source consistent with* — and not the crossed-out version above it. In the evidence list, she confirmed that the printout entry named the specific damaged columns rather than the vaguer notation she had initially drafted; the correction had been made during writing and the final form was more precise. In the *what can be claimed* paragraph, she confirmed that *inconsistent with* appeared in the relevant sentence rather than any stronger formulation. She found one word she wanted to change: in the same paragraph, *provisional statement* had been followed by a comma construction that was accurate but not as tight as the sentence warranted; she revised the punctuation without altering any of the assessments.

Two corrections total.

She did not add any assessment she had not already made. She did not remove any qualification she had already written.

The summary was honest. It was not the summary of a completed investigation. It was the summary of what eleven days at Winslow Station had been able to establish and what it had not — said in the most precise language she could bring to those facts. It was less than she had hoped to write in March. It was exactly what the data supported, and not more, and it said so.

She closed the notebook.

---

She carried it to the equipment cabinet in the corridor and set it beside the wrapped printout roll and the protocol folder. Three objects on the shelf. She confirmed each was in its place, and closed the cabinet.

One run remaining. The LC-130 at approximately 1300 tomorrow. She had the morning.

She came back to the galley. The mug was cold on the counter; she had poured the tea and then not thought about it once during the writing, which was not something she would usually allow. She poured it into the sink, rinsed the mug, and set it back on the shelf.
