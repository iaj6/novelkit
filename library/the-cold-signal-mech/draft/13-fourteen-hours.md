# Fourteen Hours

She was at the equipment building bench going through the afternoon fault log entries when McMurdo opened the scheduled weather contact at eighteen hundred.

The log's variance column held four readings since the morning stabilization: seven-point-three at twelve hundred, seven-point-six at fourteen hundred, seven-point-nine at sixteen hundred — and the same figure again at seventeen-thirty, the number unchanged between the two readings, which was not reassurance. She had noted the morning stabilization achievement at plus or minus five percent; that gain had eroded back by half across the afternoon. She wrote the seventeen-thirty entry in the margin notation she had been using since the standard column filled on Day 9 and picked up the handset on the first ring of the carrier.

McMurdo Ops delivered the weather brief in the register Elena had been hearing once a day for eleven days: even, professionally uninterested, carrying the particular efficiency of people who ran this traffic to a dozen stations on a schedule and could not be otherwise than routine about it. Weather at Winslow: conditions acceptable for transit. Barometric pressure stable, winds within the corridor threshold, no systems anticipated along the flight path. The forecast held through thirteen hundred tomorrow.

LC-130 departure from McMurdo: zero-seven hundred local. Estimated arrival Winslow: approximately thirteen hundred, depending on winds. Standard crew. Six passengers. Standard cargo plus the voltage regulator component.

She wrote the ETA in the contact log. *1300 hrs.*

Medical check-in: she read back the morning vitals and confirmed no medical situation. McMurdo logged it and gave the formal close. She returned it and racked the handset.

The time was eighteen fifty-three.

The arithmetic completed itself before she reached for it. Thirteen hundred arrival, nearly nineteen hundred now. Fourteen hours. She would have the morning: if Run 6 began at zero-six hundred, the result would come through by zero-seven, and for six hours after that she would have the result but not yet Walczak's weight on the walkway. She had calculated this before, in the abstract, without a confirmed arrival time. The confirmed arrival time did not change the arithmetic. It made the arithmetic concrete.

She wrote a note in the operations log — *Run 6: Day 12, 0600* — not in the correct protocol position, just in the margin below the weather contact entry, for the moment. Then she turned off the bench lamp and went to the receiver hut.

---

The wall thermometer read minus twelve point five. A degree colder than this morning. The minimum-draw heater was managing the rack temperature; it was not managing the hut. She had known this since Day 2 and had made no change to it, because the rack temperature was what the tape required and the hut temperature was what the power budget allowed, and tonight was not different from the other ten nights in that regard.

She took the inspection card from the rack holder and went through the visual check in the order the card specified. Binder stress zone: consistent with morning inspection. The failure zone had not visibly extended since then — the visual estimate agreed with the morning to within the precision of the timing marks. Housing temperature indicator: in range. She tipped the housing at the standard angle to check reel position and set it back on the rack.

She stood with the card in her hand without filing it.

The hut smelled of diesel exhaust — the umbilical conduit ran close enough to the receiver rack that the trace had been there since her first night in the hut — and underneath that the cold itself, which she had stopped trying to name as a smell and had started registering as a property of the air: the absence of everything that could not exist at minus twelve. The space heater's element ticked in its cycling rhythm, once every forty-some seconds when the ambient fell to the lower threshold and the element kicked briefly on. She had learned the interval without meaning to and stopped counting it sometime around Day 7.

She filed the inspection card.

Then she put her hands under the housing and lifted it off the rack.

She had never done this before. The rack was the tape's storage. She had understood it as such since the night of the signal, when she had unloaded the reel after Run 1 and set the housing in its position on the rack and confirmed the contact indicators and walked back through the walkway to write the run log entry. The rack was where it lived: temperature-managed, on the instrument circuit, in the correct position for feed cable access when she initiated playback.

She had never had reason to carry it out of the hut.

---

The walkway was thirty-one paces. She counted them without deciding to, moving with both hands on the housing and her elbows close to her sides. The midpoint was the coldest section, where the hatch-join gaps on both ends let enough draft through to drop the temperature below either structure's ambient. She passed through it and the cold was acute on her face and hands. Then the hab-side hatch, its standard resistance at the shoulder, and she stepped through and pulled it shut with her elbow.

The hab was warmer. Four degrees, perhaps five — not much, but the differential was immediate on her face and her hands on the housing. She stood in the entry space for a moment without moving further.

She cleared the top shelf of the equipment cabinet: the thermal blanket to the lower shelf, fitted beside the printout roll and the protocol folder, rearranged to make room. She set the housing on the cleared shelf.

From the supply crate she retrieved the auxiliary space heater — the small unit kept for emergency rack warming when the main heater required servicing — and positioned it on the floor below the cabinet with the element aimed at the lower vent gap. Turned it to its minimum setting. The indicator lit amber.

It was not temperature-controlled storage. It was warmer than the receiver hut by the margin the hab maintained in the passive configuration, and that margin was what she had to offer the tape tonight.

---

She retrieved the analysis notebook from the lower shelf of the cabinet and brought it to the galley table.

She read the evidence summary she had written that morning. She went through it paragraph by paragraph: what existed, what the instrument runs had established, what had not been established, the Mather data, what could be claimed, what could not be claimed, what Run 6 could and could not do. Seven minutes. She did not change any of it.

She turned to the next blank page and wrote: *Day 11, evening. Tape housing transferred to main hab equipment cabinet for overnight storage. Auxiliary heater positioned. Run 6 scheduled Day 12, 0600, subject to variance gauge reading at time of initiation.*

She set the pen in the notebook's spine. She looked at the entry.

From the supply shelf she took a sheet of blank paper — not the log pad, not from the notebook, the plain stock on the second shelf, the paper she used for working through a calculation she was not yet ready to commit to the notebook — and she sat back down at the galley table with it and picked up the pen.

She wrote a line. Stopped. Looked at the wall at the far end of the galley, not at anything on it. Wrote three more lines, pausing between the second and the third. The pen moved in her professional hand — the same hand that had written instrument logs for thirty years — but not in the instrument-log register and not quite in the evidence-summary register either; something between the two, specific and careful in a different way. She got to the end of the third line and did not continue. She wrote two more lines. Then she stopped without reaching the end of a sentence.

She put the pen on the table.

The page was face-down when she looked at the table again. She did not look at it from the other side. She left it on the corner of the galley table, face-down, away from the open notebook, and stood up.

---

She went back to the equipment building.

The power gauge read eleven percent.

She gave it its two seconds — enough to read the reading without reading what she expected to see — and then she did the arithmetic.

Eleven percent now, approximately nineteen hundred. Zero-six hundred was thirteen hours away. Passive draw in the station's current configuration, based on the figures she had been logging since Day 1: between one-half and one point per hour, depending on heater cycling and instrument standby loads. Thirteen hours: seven to thirteen points. Arriving at zero-six hundred with somewhere between negative two and four percent.

She revised the estimate. The passive draw figure she had been logging included the auxiliary heater in the hab she had just turned on — it would add a fraction to the overnight draw. She adjusted: arriving at zero-six hundred with perhaps two to four percent, if the overnight excursions were moderate and the instruments stayed in their current standby modes.

The stabilization procedure: three to four points. The run: seven to eight. Total for Run 6: ten to twelve points.

From two to four percent: a floor of negative six to negative ten. The automatic shutdown engaged at two percent. Run 6 would not complete.

She could initiate a run tonight. Eleven percent, minus ten to twelve: minus one to one percent. The floor was the same. The shutdown engaged at the same point.

There was one run in the budget. It was tomorrow's.

She had known this since she had confirmed the morning's result; the decision had resolved before she had needed to make it. She went to the protocol folder and wrote the Run 6 entry in its proper place in the operations log, not in the margin position she had used before:

*Run 6: Day 12, 0600. Deferral from Day 11: insufficient power reserve for two runs within 14-hour window while maintaining 2% life-support threshold prior to team arrival.*

She confirmed the variance gauge before turning off the bench lamp. Seven-point-nine. It had held at seven-point-nine since the sixteen-hundred reading, the afternoon's partial recovery from the morning stabilization neither improving nor eroding in the last four hours. The overnight excursions would move it or they would not; she would know at zero-six hundred what she had to stabilize from. The procedure had achieved plus or minus five percent from an eight-percent band this morning. It would need to do that again.

She turned off the lamp.

---

In the hab the only light was the SSB standby light, red on the console, and the amber indicator on the auxiliary heater below the equipment cabinet. She did not turn on the galley light. She could see the outline of the face-down page on the galley table in the dim — the edge of the paper, the plain underside — without going to the table.

She did not go to the table.

The bunk's sleeping bag was unzipped and folded back the way she had left it each morning of the investigation — not from carelessness, but because she had formed the habit in the first week, when she had been rising at three and four in the morning to check the fault log, and had found that re-entering a zipped bag was twelve seconds longer than re-entering an open one, and the twelve seconds had mattered. The habit had outlasted its necessity. She pulled the bag over herself without zipping it.

The generator held its low hum through the insulated wall. She had stopped hearing it as sound and started hearing it as condition, sometime around Day 5, the way she had stopped hearing the space heater's cycle in the receiver hut: you learned the interval, and then the interval became silence. It was holding its load. The fault log had its reading. In the morning she would go to the equipment building and look at the variance gauge and begin the stabilization procedure.

The tape housing was in the cabinet across the room. The standby light was red in the dark. The face-down page was on the galley table.

Fourteen hours.
