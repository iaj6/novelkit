# Worse Than Calculated

The Day 5 morning gauge read sixty-four point four liters.

She read it once, and then once more with the flashlight angled to cut the refraction in the sight glass, and wrote the figure in the maintenance log. Then she opened to the Day 4 entry: seventy-three point five liters, recorded before Run 3. She had logged Run 3's estimated fuel cost in the entry's margin — three and a half liters above the passive baseline — and below it the standard overnight passive draw — four and a half liters. She added them and subtracted from seventy-three point five. The expected Day 5 morning figure was sixty-five point five.

She subtracted sixty-four point four from sixty-five point five.

One point one liters unaccounted for.

She looked at the temperature log mounted beside the gauge panel. The equipment building's exterior-facing wall had held at minus twenty-eight overnight. She had used minus twenty-four as her baseline temperature in the burn-rate calculation. She noted the actual figure on a separate line in the maintenance log, then turned to the fuel tank.

The tank was mounted in that wall.

At minus twenty-eight Celsius, diesel fuel thickens. Not to the wax-crystal threshold — that was several degrees colder still — but the viscosity increases measurably, and the pump draws harder to maintain the flow rate the engine requires. Higher current on the pump motor, more fuel consumed per unit of running time. The technical reference she kept in the equipment building's filing cabinet — the Winslow instrument manual, updated at the station's last major maintenance cycle in 1989 — covered this in the cold-weather annex. She had read the annex when the fuel line was rerouted through the exterior wall panel rather than the interior corridor. She had approved the installation detail. She had filed the maintenance note.

She had not applied the viscosity correction to her burn-rate calculation. She had used the standard figure from the generator specification sheet, which assumed a tank in temperate conditions. The tank was not in temperate conditions. It had not been in temperate conditions since the temperature fell below minus twenty in late April.

One point one liters above expected, in a single overnight period. Consistent with a burn rate approximately fifteen percent above baseline. The exterior wall temperature had been present since the start of winter — the log ran back to April — and there was no mechanism by which it would improve. If anything, June deepened.

She sat on the equipment bench and got out the notebook.

The fuel arithmetic from Day 4 was in her regular ballpoint: the eight-run estimate, the column of figures, the annotation she had written at the end — *Assumes standard burn rate. Verify against actual gauge in 24h. Do not treat as confirmed.* She had verified against the actual gauge. She found the red mechanical pencil in the notebook's back cover pocket — fine-point, kept for corrections, distinguishable from the day's text at a glance — and uncapped it.

Revised burn rate: five point two liters per day, not four point five.

She calculated what this did to the run budget. The eight-run estimate had been derived from a discretionary fuel pool divided by the per-run cost at baseline. At fifteen percent above baseline, both the daily passive draw and the per-run cost were correspondingly higher. She divided the eight-run total by one point one five.

Six point nine six.

She wrote *6* in the red pencil below the blue-ink eight. Below that:

*Used: 3. Remaining: 3.*

She underlined the *3* twice.

---

It was not five. The figure she had been holding since Run 3 — the tracking count she had maintained since Day 1 night, revised through each run, each brownout, each recalculation — had been five. She had used five when she decided to pause the protocol runs before the gate. She had used five when she built the forward arithmetic in the inventory summary. She had used five as the available margin for everything that came after the gate.

The correct figure was three.

She was not going to let herself say this was the generator's fault or the cold's. The annotation had been there. *Do not treat as confirmed.* She had treated it as confirmed anyway — had held the eight as her working number through all the Day 4 decisions because eight was the number that fit, and three days of rounding had absorbed the one-point-one liters per day without resolving into a visible discrepancy until today, when the aggregate could no longer hide it.

She wrote below the red-pencil figures: *Gate: approx 48 h. Post-gate margin: 3 remaining runs.*

Then she looked at what she had written. The options she had been holding — running the protocol once more before the gate, if the decision required it; keeping four runs for a more complete post-gate sequence rather than three — were not closed by circumstance. They were closed by arithmetic, and the arithmetic had been available to her since Day 1 if she had applied the right correction.

She put the red pencil back in its pocket. She did not close the notebook.

---

At 0815 she returned to the main hab. She made coffee. She did not open the fieldwork notebook, which she had left on top of the equipment cabinet. The cold-weather annex to the fuel manual was in the equipment building's filing cabinet and she could reconstruct the viscosity table to whatever precision she wanted, but precision was not the missing quantity. She had the number. She put the coffee mug on the galley table and sat with it while it cooled.

At 0930 she went back to the equipment building and ran the voltage regulator output check: 216 volts, two below yesterday's reading, still within the rated band. She updated the fault interval log — Walczak's instruction from the last check-in, though he hadn't given it yet, this was anticipatory, something to do while the number settled — and noted the duration between the overnight voltage events. Sixty-three minutes from the 0307 dip to the 0410 dip. One interval. One data point. The same problem she had everywhere.

She came back to the hab. She ate a piece of bread over the sink because she was not hungry and standing up was faster. The corridor hatch let in a thin draft through the cracked seal and she had stopped noticing it some time ago, which meant the cold that came in now registered as normal.

At approximately 1100, the SSB console lit on the standby indicator and Walczak's call sign came through.

---

She sat at the console and confirmed reception, identified, reported signal quality: five by four, readable, with the high-frequency flutter she had been hearing since the ionospheric shift on Day 3.

Walczak acknowledged. His voice had a particular quality through twelve hundred kilometers of atmosphere — the higher frequencies attenuated, his vowels compressed into a register slightly cleaner than his actual speech. He was tired; she could hear that underneath the compression, the slightly longer pauses before he spoke, the flatness that entered his voice in the second week of McMurdo. He had the habits she recognized from two shared winters: he stated what he knew and asked what he didn't.

He asked about the voltage regulator fault log. How many events in the last forty-eight hours.

She said: four entries, two per night for the past two nights. All sub-alarm. Dips to 206 and 209 volts, recovery to 216, no brownout, no warm-up cycle triggered. She had begun logging the interval duration between events today: sixty-three minutes between the overnight pair.

He said: what was the interval before the fault events started.

She said: no fault events logged before Day 3. Days 1 and 2 were clean.

He said: so Days 3, 4, 5 against clear. Do the intervals trend shorter.

She said: she had one interval logged. Sixty-three minutes. Insufficient to establish a trend. Over.

A pause — four or five seconds, long for SSB from McMurdo, where transmission time was metered. She heard what she had learned to read as him writing something.

He asked about the fuel.

She said: burn rate running approximately fifteen percent above baseline. Cold fuel tank — the tank is in the exterior wall panel, which has been holding at minus twenty-eight overnight. Viscosity issue. Diesel thickens at these temperatures and the pump draws harder. She had adjusted the discretionary allocation to account for the revised estimate. Standing heating draw unchanged. Over.

He said: fifteen percent is material. What's your revised run margin.

She said: reduced from prior estimate. Still manageable. Over.

He did not press for a specific number. This was both the right professional response and, in the moment, more than she wanted to think about. He trusted her calculations. She understood now that the calculation she had built her estimates on for five days had contained an error she had annotated and then ignored, and he had no way to know this, and the word manageable had passed through twelve hundred kilometers of atmosphere and been accepted.

He said he had found a compatible part for the voltage regulator in the McMurdo maintenance stores. He had confirmed the part number against the instrument manual before this call. He would bring it back on Day 12.

She said: good.

He said: it won't help you now, I know.

She said: no. But Day 12 is soon enough. Over.

He asked how she was doing.

The SSB register permitted this question; it was within the vocabulary of the standard winterover check-in. He and Elena had shared two winters at stations where the difference between asking and not asking was the difference between a twelve-day stretch that you carried and one that carried you. When he asked, it was a real question.

She said: fine.

He said: you sound tired.

She said: I am.

He said the flight was confirmed for Day 12, no manifest changes as of the previous day's operations briefing. He would check in once more before then. He gave the standard formal close — call sign, station identifier, time of termination — and she returned it in the correct form.

The channel dropped to background hiss.

---

She stayed at the console. The standby light came back on, red, steady, exactly as it had been every night since October.

She was aware of what she had done and not done. She had told him the burn rate: fifteen percent above baseline, the exterior wall, the viscosity. She had told him she'd adjusted the discretionary allocation. All of it was accurate. She had not told him what the remaining run margin governed, or why the discretionary allocation was being managed in the first place, or what he would find when he arrived on Day 12 and opened the instrument log.

He would open it. The instrument log was part of the station commissioning review he did at the start of every working season. He would open it and find six protocol runs of the signal analyzer, verification sequence, run designations 1 through 6, spanning twelve consecutive days. He would know what the sequence was; he had helped design the verification protocol. He would look at the log and then at the tape housing and he would understand what she had been running — and for how long, and through what power margin — before she had said a word to him. And then he would look at her.

She had no calculation for this. She took the maintenance record from the console shelf and turned to the Day 5 page and wrote:

*Walczak check-in: routine. Nothing transmitted.*

She capped the pen.

The generator ran in the wall behind her at its standard pitch. Outside the building the dark held. The power reserve was at approximately fifty-one percent — nine points below where it had been yesterday morning, and she had not run the protocol once today. Forty-eight hours to the gate. Three runs remaining, not five.

The standby light held its color.
