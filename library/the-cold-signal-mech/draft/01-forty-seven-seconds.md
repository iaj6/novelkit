# Forty-Seven Seconds

The evening scan ran late. Elena had let it run — the ionospheric conditions had been good since midnight and she did not want to break the sequence — and by the time she checked the wall clock above the instrument rack it was twenty past ten and she had been in the receiver hut for nearly four hours. The heater had cycled off twice. The temperature inside had dropped to roughly minus nine, maybe minus eleven; her breath came out in small, visible clouds when she turned to the printout roll to tear off the night's log.

She was doing that — tearing the strip at the perforation with the particular sideways pull that worked — when the signal analyzer's output changed.

She did not hear it. There was nothing to hear. What she saw was a line on the frequency display that had not been there before: a narrow feature, cleanly bounded, in the upper UHF monitoring band she had been running since 2100. Not a drift. Not a broadband rise. A structure, sitting at a specific frequency with a specific shape, and it was moving — not laterally across the dial but in its own internal architecture, as if whatever was producing it was doing something in a particular order.

She put the printout strip down on the work surface.

She did not move toward the equipment. She watched.

The clock on the wall read 22:21.

The signal held.

At 22:22 it was still there, the analyzer's amber numerals steady on the reading she had seen arrive. At 22:23 the frequency display showed the same structure, shifted slightly in one parameter — a coherent shift, not thermal wobble, not a drift in the band — and then at 22:24 the analyzer's display showed nothing where the feature had been, and the frequency band was quiet, and the reel-to-reel recorder clicked softly as its transport mechanism held the last revolutions before the auto-pause engaged.

The clock read 22:25:09.

Four minutes and nine seconds between the first anomaly and the display going quiet. But the tape had been running since before she started the tear — since twenty past, since she had reset the advance at the start of the hour — and the reel's counter told her the feature had begun at 22:21:52. She worked the subtraction. Forty-seven seconds.

The hut was very quiet. The heater's fan was off. Outside, nothing.

She was not going to do anything until she had checked the obvious explanations. That was the procedure — her procedure — and the procedure was what she had.

---

The first check was her own transmitters. The station's HF and SSB units were in the equipment building, connected to their own antenna line. She had signed off the radio at 2100 and logged the close-down. She opened the equipment log to that entry and read her own notation: *SSB, VHF, all transmitters off at 2101.* The frequency she had just observed was not one the station's transmitters used. Not one any station transmitter she had ever encountered used.

She logged the check and its result. Then she moved to the cross-reference binder.

The band she was monitoring was a high-UHF channel — not one of the standard allocated observation frequencies, not because she was trying to be unorthodox, but because the protocols she had written in 1983 and revised four times since were specifically designed to watch the frequencies outside the standard allocation. Standard was where you parked when you didn't want to find anything. She had argued this in print twice and been largely ignored.

She checked the known interference sources for the band: the INTELSAT arc was off the southern horizon this week; she had the positions logged. No maritime radar returns at this frequency; the nearest ship was over the horizon by more than two hundred kilometers. No ionospheric broadcast carrier that propagated in this band at this solar minimum. She went through each in order, noted the ruling out of each, and moved to the next.

She was aware she was not hurrying. She was also aware she had been standing at the cross-reference binder for approximately twenty minutes and the temperature had dropped another two degrees while she stood.

Natural phenomena: impulsive broadband discharges from lightning would have spread across the monitored band, not resolved into a narrow feature. Auroral emissions at this magnetic latitude were broad and characteristically turbulent, not structured. She had the ionosonde data from the equipment building logged every six hours; the current reading showed a stable F-layer, no sporadic-E, no spread-F that would produce this artifact. The solar wind data from the McMurdo bulletin — she kept it pinned above the equipment rack on a strip of thermal fax paper — showed a quiet magnetosphere.

She read it again.

She was not going to write *ruled out* and mean *probably ruled out*. She was going to write it when it was true.

Twenty-two minutes into the checks, she wrote *ruled out* for the last entry in the natural-phenomena column and put the cap back on the pen.

She did not yet write the word she could not write.

---

Protocol Run 1 was hers: the first version from 1983, revised in 1986, in 1989, in 1990, and once more last March before she flew south. The revisions had been mostly subtractive — removing steps she had decided were redundant, tightening the parameter thresholds until the protocol would no longer pass a false positive. By her estimate, the current version would reject all but one case in ten to the ninth of signals she had ever processed. She had processed something over twelve thousand hours of monitoring time in the past three years alone.

She initialized the filter sweep. If the source was terrestrial — a satellite, a radar return, the station's own HF transmitter leaking through the receiver coax — it would show here.

She reset the tape transport and threaded the take-up reel through the capture head, positioning the tape at the record start. The reel moved with the slight resistance she had noticed four days ago — something in the tension mechanism, not serious yet, something she had logged. She noted the resistance again now and wrote the entry.

She initialized the analyzer clock and marked the run start in the logbook. The time was 22:49. The run would take approximately twenty minutes.

She checked the printout paper feed. The paper was a continuous roll of chart stock — the same she had brought in September, the same stock that had been sitting in the receiver hut for eight months at temperatures between minus three and minus thirty. The paper rolled cleanly.

The analyzer hummed. The frequency display settled into the run's initial calibration sweep. She sat down in the work chair.

The heater was off; she had not run it since the start of the checks because the draw competed with the analyzer and tonight she needed the instrument stable. The cold was not extreme — minus eleven or so inside — and she was dressed for it, the insulated oversuit, the boot liners, the liner gloves she wore when she needed to handle the log pages. She had done this at colder temperatures.

The display showed the calibration sweep proceeding normally. Seven minutes in, she made the first intermediate notation.

At eleven minutes and some seconds, the lights went out.

Not fully out. The generator's voltage dropped — she could hear it before she could see it, the pitch of the equipment fan dropping half a tone — and for three seconds the instrument rack's lights dimmed to amber and the analyzer's display flickered in the way that meant the processor was running below threshold. Then the generator recovered, the display came back, and the fan pitch returned to what it had been.

She sat absolutely still.

When the run completed at 23:11, she checked the run log. The log showed the brownout window: three seconds at the eleven-minute mark, during the pre-signal calibration segment. She read the flagged entry twice.

The brownout had occurred before the signal segment began. The calibration segment established the baseline parameters against which the signal was measured, but the calibration data itself was not part of the signal's parametric record. The three-second window was not in the core data.

She read it a third time. Then she made the notation: *Run 1 complete. Brownout flag in calibration segment, 11:00–11:03. Core data unaffected. Run valid.*

She did not add a qualifier to *valid.* It was valid. The core data was not in the brownout window. She had seen the same instrument log pattern twice before, in different contexts, and both times the calibration-segment flag had not invalidated the run. That was her considered judgment, and she had made it.

The analyzer output from Run 1 sat in the printout tray.

She picked it up.

The signal analyzer's output was not an image. It was a numerical record — frequency, amplitude, timing parameters, spectral properties across the band — arranged in columns on chart paper she had been reading versions of for eight years. She knew what noise looked like on this printout. She knew what broadband interference looked like and what calibration artifacts looked like and what the half-dozen things that had made her stomach drop over the past twelve thousand hours looked like when you unrolled the chart and traced the columns with a finger.

She read the printout.

The structure in the upper UHF monitoring band at 22:21:52 was forty-seven seconds in duration. It was narrowband — a spectral width two orders of magnitude below the minimum threshold for any broadband natural emitter at this frequency. Its frequency was stable across the event to within the instrument's resolution: no dispersion consistent with ionospheric propagation delay, no frequency shift consistent with Doppler from a known-orbit satellite, no amplitude scalloping consistent with multipath reflections. The internal structure — the way the signal's properties shifted during those forty-seven seconds — was not random. It was not what stochastic processes produced. It was not what any natural radio source she had ever studied produced.

She set the printout face-up on the work surface.

She read it again.

The second time she was looking for what she had missed. There was a methodology to this too: the first reading was to register what was there; the second reading was to find the counterargument. She had been doing this long enough to know that her own mind would generate a counterargument from nearly anything if she looked long enough, and that this was not a weakness but a feature she had deliberately cultivated. She looked for what she had not been willing to see the first time through.

The third time she read the printout she was no longer looking for anything. She was reading the numbers.

The numbers were what they were.

She set the printout down, face-up, like a document whose character had just changed.

---

The power gauge on the generator panel was mounted next to the equipment rack, in a housing she had to lean past the signal analyzer to read. She leaned. The needle sat at seventy-eight percent of baseline — down from the start-of-night reading of ninety-two she had logged at 2000. The warm-up cycle after the brownout had pulled more than she'd estimated. Fourteen percent in one shift, including the run.

She wrote the reading in the log.

Instrument run margin: she had estimated, at the start of the season, a maximum of eight complete verification runs before the power reserve fell below the threshold needed to sustain heating, life support, and the generator's own stability margin. She had based that estimate on the pre-brownout consumption rates. She thought about the warm-up cycle that had just cost her and revised the estimate downward by one.

Seven. Not eight. Seven.

She wrote: *Run 1 complete. Instrument run margin estimated: 7 remaining.*

The tape. It was in the receiver housing on the reel above the instrument rack. The housing had its own small heating element — designed to maintain the tape at a stable temperature — but the heating element ran on the instrument rack's power, which came from the generator. When the generator ran below margin, the housing temperature dropped. She opened the housing's temperature log. At the time of the brownout the temperature had dipped two degrees below spec and recovered. One event. She did not know how many events like that the binder could absorb.

The tape was the only copy. There was no mechanism at Winslow for making a duplicate. Nothing that would leave the station before the team returned.

The team. They had left this morning — not this morning, yesterday morning, the nine hours of pale twilight that passed for a morning in early June — on the LC-130 out of the McMurdo run, Walczak and the others, a twelve-day rotation. She had watched the aircraft's lights disappear into the north and then gone back to the hut and resumed the scan, because the scan did not stop because people left. That was what a winterover was.

Twelve days.

The relay window — the scheduled uplink to the satellite relay, the one slot before Day 12 that could carry a data packet out of Winslow to McMurdo — was in approximately six days. She counted back from the date in the operations schedule. Day 7, give or take the weather. Six days and some hours if the ionospheric window held.

She sat with the count for a moment.

Six days from now, a channel would open for forty-eight minutes. She could transmit a compressed data packet and a fax scan of the printout. The packet would arrive at McMurdo Ops and from there it would go where McMurdo Ops decided to send it, and it would be described in the language of whichever duty officer wrote the forwarding note, and it would be read by people who had not written the protocols. She had watched that process before, on smaller findings, findings that had not mattered, and she knew precisely what it produced.

She also knew that if she did not transmit in that window, the next opportunity was the aircraft. Day 12. When Walczak stepped off the ramp.

She counted the remaining days. She counted the remaining runs. She counted the remaining margin in the generator, the remaining integrity in the tape's binder.

She set the pen down.

---

The covered walkway between the receiver hut and the main hab was thirty-one paces. She knew this because she had counted it her first night at the station, the same way she had counted everything her first night — the rungs on the equipment building ladder, the steps between her bunk and the galley bench, the ceiling clearance in the receiver hut at its lowest point. Not from anxiety. From the habit of a person who likes to know the dimensions of the space she is working in.

She walked the walkway now, pulling the hatch behind her, at nearly midnight. The umbilical conduit ran along the ceiling and smelled of diesel exhaust and cold engine oil, as it always had, exactly as it always had.

The galley in the main hab had a small bench and a two-burner electric plate. She put the kettle on. While it heated she stood at the bench and looked at the corridor hatch, at the crack in the rubber seal she had filed a maintenance note about in April. The crack let in cold. Tonight outside was minus thirty-seven.

The SSB radio console was in the equipment alcove off the galley. Its red standby light was on. She had signed off at 2100.

She could reach Walczak on the McMurdo schedule. The early frequency would be open until midnight and he monitored when they were in rotation. She knew the quality of his attention on a radio call — thorough, technically exact, the kind of listener who heard what you didn't say as clearly as what you did.

She did not move toward the radio.

The kettle came to a boil. She filled the green-enameled steel mug — it had been in the hab when she arrived, she did not know whose it had been — and held it without drinking. It went lukewarm faster than it should. The corridor seal.

She put the mug down.

She went back to the receiver hut.

---

The signal analyzer's amber numerals were still on the display from the run. The printout was where she had left it, face-up on the work surface. The reel was still loaded in the housing above. The heater was still off.

She sat down in the work chair and picked up the printout and held it in her lap.

She was not thinking about what it meant — not in the sense of meaning, not in any sense she had words for yet. She was thinking about what she had to do next, and in what order, and with how much power.

The heater ticked once as the metal contracted in the cold.

Outside, nothing moved.
