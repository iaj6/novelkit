# Before the Window

The hut thermometer read minus fourteen point three. She wrote the figure below the previous night's minimum in the log — a tenth below it, within measurement noise — and turned to the tape housing.

She had known before she crossed the walkway that she would not run the protocol today. The arithmetic had settled it the way it settled everything: before she named the conclusion to herself. Three runs remaining. The relay window was opening in under thirty-six hours. Burning a run now would give her an instrument-confirmed figure for a thing she could already estimate by eye. She did not need a precise figure. She needed to know the direction, and the direction was already visible.

She unlatched the housing cover and drew the flashlight across the reel.

The timing marks were where she had placed them on Day 2 — pencil lines on the housing interior, bracketing the key segment positions: monitoring record start, monitoring record end, signal start, the parametric divisions. She had drawn them so that the tape's position relative to each mark was readable without powering the instrument, by comparing the reel's outermost layer to the calibration positions.

She found the mark for the monitoring record / signal boundary.

The binder stress zone had crossed it.

She checked from a different angle to account for the flashlight refraction and got the same reading. The affected edge had been approximately four centimeters from this mark on Day 4. At the rate she had estimated — one and a half centimeters per day along the reel — two days would move it three centimeters. The visual distance she was reading was consistent with three centimeters.

She tried to translate this into time. The dropout on Run 3 had been five seconds — at the leading edge of where the binder stress had been, as of the Day 4 physical inspection. If the relationship between affected tape length and dropout duration held proportionately at this extent, she was looking at eight seconds. Perhaps slightly less. Perhaps slightly more.

She wrote: *~8s dropout, visual est. No run burned. Gate: under 36h.* She capped the pen, latched the housing cover, and left the hut.

---

The relay carrier signal indicator on the fax terminal was gray. The window was not open. She checked the power gauge on her way past the generator panel: forty-nine percent, down two points since yesterday's close. She noted the figure in the maintenance log.

The USAP data transmission protocol was a three-section format she had used twice in the 1980s, from remote field sites where fax was the only channel available for submitting instrument records to a receiving institution. She pulled the printed specification from the equipment building's filing cabinet, opened it on the work surface beside the terminal, and began filling in the header.

Frequency band: upper UHF monitoring channel, non-standard allocation. Timestamp: 22:21:52, 1 June 1991, Winslow Station local. Duration: 47 seconds. These required no deliberation; she had had the figures since Run 1.

The amplitude profile header took longer. The compressed summary was in the analysis notebook, worked out after Run 2, and she copied the values across with the precision the format permitted — five significant figures where the field allowed five significant figures — and noted the rounding at the end of each affected line.

The first parametric segment summary required an amendment. The protocol format called for full parametric values, but Run 3 had established a five-second dropout at the segment's start, with no valid data for those seconds regardless of which run she referenced. She recorded the intact values beginning at the post-dropout position, noted the dropout explicitly — onset character (sharp; blank oxide), extent as of Run 3 (five seconds, Day 4, instrument-confirmed), current visual estimate (approximately eight seconds, Day 6). The note added four lines to the first parametric block. She formatted them within the existing field structure.

The second parametric segment had read clean through Run 3. She entered its values without amendment.

The verification protocol summary she copied from the analysis notebook directly: Runs 1 through 3, the methodology description, the judgment recorded after each run. She used her own language. She did not simplify.

That left the cover statement.

---

She typed the first version without stopping. Then she read it back.

The facts were in the right sequence. Two things were wrong. There was a sentence that characterized the signal's significance — a sentence that said what the data indicated — and she deleted it when she saw it: that language belonged to a press summary, not to a transmittal cover for a data buffer. She deleted the sentence that followed it, which had depended on the first to mean anything. She rewrote the paragraph.

She read the second version. One phrase hedged where the data did not require hedging — a softening that would read as methodological doubt rather than as appropriate caution about interpretation, and she knew from experience that the distinction would not survive the first reading by someone unfamiliar with the protocol's design. She changed the phrase. Another sentence characterized the verification methodology in a way that would invite a specific follow-up question before the receiving agency had seen the instrument documentation. She rephrased it.

She read the cover statement a third time.

She did not read it a fourth time.

The packet was complete: header, data record, cover statement, formatted and addressed to the USAP relay buffer. The initiation command was the next line. The cursor blinked.

She put on her outer layer and went back to the hut.

---

In the hut she opened the analysis notebook to Run 1 and read through the protocol notes from the beginning: the signal's recorded parameters, the verification steps, the judgment she had written at 23:15 on the night of Day 1. Then Run 2. Then Run 3. The handwriting was consistent across all three — the deliberate enlarged script she had settled into in the 1980s when her eyes required more space between the characters. She had made fewer marginal notations in Run 3 than in Run 1. That was familiarity, not less attention.

She set the notebook on the work table.

If she sent the packet, the relay buffer would route it to McMurdo Operations and then to the agency's data management unit in Washington. They would receive a compressed data summary from a scientist they had reasons to trust. They would ask for the instrument logs. They would want the tape — in person, transported on the LC-130, handled by people who had no cause to treat it differently from any other instrument record, who would assume that cold was cold and tape was tape and that standard procedures covered both. They would schedule a verification run on equipment she had not specified in the protocol, on the assumption that the tape could be moved and the signal re-analyzed somewhere with more infrastructure. The cover statement she had just written would be the first document anyone outside Winslow Station read. It would set the frame. Within two weeks it would be superseded by descriptions she had not written, in language that would simplify what she had not simplified, attributed to a scientist who had called something in and referred further questions to the program office.

She had watched this happen to other scientists. Not to data of this kind — she did not have a comparison case for data of this kind — but to results that were real, that were correctly identified, and that nonetheless got away from the people who found them. She had been the person those scientists called when the result had already been named, given a phrase that fit the press cycle but not the actual uncertainty, made simpler than the uncertainty warranted. She had spent thirty years becoming the kind of scientist who did not do that to her own work. If she transmitted now she would do it to this.

If she did not send the packet, she would let the relay window close without transmitting and have three runs, a tape at eight seconds of dropout and growing, and five days before the LC-130 landed. She would hand the notebook to Walczak in whatever moment presented itself on Day 12 — the equipment building, the galley, the covered walkway — with no preparation and no framing. He would open the instrument log. He was the only person at Winslow who could read that log and know immediately what the sequence designated. He would look at the log and then he would look at her.

She thought about Lars. His reading was in a log filed as instrument artifact, sixty kilometers east. She did not reach for the radio.

---

She went back to the equipment building.

The terminal screen was still on. The data packet was in the send queue, formatted and addressed, with the cursor blinking at the beginning of the initiation command. She had not closed the session.

She sat on the work stool and looked at the screen.

She did not delete the packet. She did not enter the initiation command. She turned off the overhead work light, leaving the terminal's screen glow as the room's only source, and went to the main hab.

She sat on the edge of the bunk with her boots still on. The sleeping bag was unzipped and folded back — she had stopped straightening it some days ago. She pulled it over her lap. She did not lie down.

The generator ran in the equipment building wall at the pitch it had held since October, no louder and no quieter. The SSB console's standby light was red, steady — exactly as it always had been.

The relay window would open before morning. The schedule was posted on the bulletin board above the terminal and she could have looked at it. She had not. She did not go back to look at it now.
