# After

The soup had come from a tin she did not remember choosing. She had taken a bowl from the drying rack, found the tin on the shelf above the cooktop, opened it, heated the contents in a small pot, and poured it, and all of these actions had apparently occurred while her attention was on something other than soup, because she could not have said precisely when she had begun to be hungry or when she had decided to do something about it. The galley clock read 16:52.

She ate standing at the counter. Not because there was any particular reason to stand — the table was there, the bench seat was empty — but because she had come to the counter and the soup was there and she had not moved. Sitting was apparently a decision she had not yet arrived at.

Outside, the wind had picked up since morning: a flat, irregular pressure against the hab wall, neither increasing nor decreasing, simply present. The generator ran in the equipment building at its usual pitch. She had been listening to the generator for eleven weeks, and what she heard now was the usual pitch. The standby light on the SSB radio was red and steady.

She finished the soup. Set the bowl in the sink. Stood for a moment at the counter with her hands on the edge, not thinking in any directed way — not reviewing the morning, not drawing a conclusion from it, not doing what she would normally do at the end of a significant event. The significant event had passed. What remained was the particular state of being present in the afternoon of the day it had passed, which turned out to have its own quality, different from anticipation and different from aftermath and not quite either.

Outside the hab windows, the dark.

---

Then the tone from the equipment building.

Low-register, below the threshold at which it could be called a sound without qualification — more like a change in the quality of the generator's output than a sound distinct from it. She had heard it twice before: once in April, once in the second week of May. Both times the tone had persisted for thirty to forty seconds and resolved on its own. She had logged both events.

This one did not resolve.

She went.

The equipment building was four degrees colder than the hab, its heater drawing from the same generator whose fault was now active. The output display read 89%, the needle moving in a slow irregular arc between 88 and 91. The voltage regulator fault light was amber. Not red: amber was monitored deviation, not instrument emergency. She had looked up the distinction in the third week of winter and had retained it without further reference.

She checked the load indicators in sequence: hab base circuit, equipment building, receiver hut. The receiver hut heater was the highest-draw single circuit. She confirmed this against the load schedule on the wall above the panel — a laminated card she had made in March for this winter's operating conditions, circuits listed in order of draw — and then shed the receiver hut heater.

The load indicator dropped. The output gauge steadied at 93%, still in fault range but no longer fluctuating. The fault light remained amber. She sat down on the bench along the west wall and settled in to wait.

Forty minutes. This was the stabilization procedure: reduce load, give the generator time to normalize at the lower draw, then restore. Forty minutes was the minimum interval. She had done this twice and read it in the manual many more times than twice, and the procedure was correct, and what it required of her right now was to sit on the bench and not do anything to the load until the interval had elapsed.

The generator held at 93%. She watched the gauge and listened to it.

What she did not think about was the window. That was not suppression; the window was simply past, the same way April was past and October was past, and thinking about what had already become fixed was a use of attention that she did not have a way to make productive. What occupied the particular attention available to a person sitting on a bench with an instrument fault to monitor and forty minutes to spend was the arithmetic of what was left.

Three runs. Five days.

A tape that had been adding approximately one second of dropout per day since the Run 3 measurement, which was itself derived from two data points — enough to establish a rate, not enough to determine whether that rate was linear or accelerating. Her working estimate put the dropout now at eight seconds. If the rate held through Day 12, it would reach thirteen seconds by the time the LC-130 landed. The signal segment's first parametric region had begun at the tape position where the dropout had started; at thirteen seconds of dropout, the leading boundary of the first parametric region would still be within the readable portion of the tape. Whether the core signature was at risk before or after that point was a question she could not resolve from two data points.

Three data points would not resolve it either, but they would constrain it. If she ran on Day 8, Day 10, and Day 12, she would have three additional measurements of the dropout rate and, by the rate's behavior across those intervals, a more defensible estimate of where the core signature stood on Day 12. Tighter bounds on a number that was still growing were not the same as good news. But they were the most she could do, and she had learned not to misidentify the most she could do.

Each run would cost seven percentage points of the power reserve at the clean-run rate. She was at 41% now. Three runs would bring it to 20%, assuming no further fault events, no brownout warm-up cost, no unanticipated draw. Assuming was the wrong word; 20% was a lower bound that depended on conditions she did not fully control.

She was aware of the receiver hut heater being shed. She would need to check the housing temperature when she restored the draw.

The output gauge held at 93%. The fault light was amber, steady.

---

At minute forty-three, she restored the receiver hut heater.

The gauge dipped to 90% and stabilized within thirty seconds. The fault light went dark. She watched it for two minutes, then picked up the log and wrote:

*Day 7 afternoon. Generator fault: voltage regulator active fault. Output: 89%, fluctuating. Stabilization procedure: shed receiver hut heater for 43 min. Post-shed output: stabilized at 93%. On restoration: dipped to 90%, stabilized. Fault light extinguished. No instruments lost.*

She looked at the entry. It recorded what had happened accurately. It did not record that this was the third fault event of the winter, or that the first two had been in April and May and had resolved without intervention, or that this one had not, or that the post-restoration output was 90% rather than the 97% baseline that had been the pre-fault standard. These were observations she could make from the three log entries together; the individual entry was not the place for them. She underlined *No instruments lost* as a matter of habit, because that was the fact with the most consequence, and habit was how she kept herself from deciding which facts had consequence based on how she felt about them in the moment of writing.

She would need to go outside to check the exhaust pipe.

---

The cold-weather kit hung on the rack beside the exterior door: insulated overtrousers, parka, balaclava, goggles, gauntlets. She had been in this kit forty times or more this winter. The sequence for dressing was fixed and she performed it in order — overtrousers first, both legs seated before the waist was secured; parka, zip checked twice; balaclava fitted before the parka collar was sealed; goggles, gasket against the face-surround checked; gauntlets last — not because she was in danger of doing it wrong, but because the sequence was correct and performing a correct sequence incorrectly was a particular kind of waste. She had learned not to rush it. Seven minutes.

She pushed the exterior door open.

The temperature was minus forty-two Celsius. There was no wind at this moment. She was aware of the wind's absence the way she was aware of a gauge reading within normal range: noted, but by the absence of the deviation, not by the presence of the norm. No wind. She went around to the south wall.

The exhaust pipe was clear. No frost accumulation at the terminus, no partial ice at the bend. The pipe was warm enough along its final section that icing had not had the opportunity to begin during the fault and recovery. She confirmed this with the flashlight and straightened.

She looked up.

The sky was not black. That was not the right word for what it was, because black implied the absence of texture, and there was texture — the stars at sixty-eight degrees south in mid-June, with no moon and no aurora, were dense enough to appear as a surface rather than as points on a background. A surface with gradation: brighter toward the galactic center, fainter in the high-declination bands, the faint lateral structure of the plane running across it at an angle that did not correspond to any angle on the ground. She had been outside in this dark enough times this winter to know that the knowledge of it was not adequate preparation for standing under it. You could carry the knowledge and still be stopped by the fact.

She stood for a moment that was longer than exhaust-pipe confirmation required.

---

What she was not going to do: use a relay channel that had closed at 0848.

That option had been present for six days and was no longer present. The decision space she had been working in that morning was not the decision space she was working in now. Eight hours was not enough time to have fully characterized what the new one contained. She had been characterizing it since the bench in the equipment building, between gauge-readings.

What it contained: hand the data and the analysis notebook to Walczak on Day 12, in whatever state the tape was in by then. Destroy the data — not as a direction she was moving toward, but as a door that existed in the same room as the other doors. Pursue some form of publication from here, which would require communication resources and documentation support and a submission window that nothing at Winslow could produce in five days. The third door was nearly flush with the wall. She could see it was there.

And Lars — the thought arrived with the specificity of something she had been carrying since morning without examining. Lars at Mather Station, sixty kilometers east, with a reading in the UHF secondary band that he had filed as artifact and that Elena had confirmed was not an artifact by telling him it probably was. Something there. But there were sixty kilometers between them and a radio protocol that did not permit the conversation the data required, and she did not follow the thought further.

She stood under the stars for another thirty seconds. Then she went back inside.

---

She removed the cold-weather kit in reverse order and hung each piece on its peg. She went to the galley and filled the kettle.

While the kettle heated, she walked the covered walkway to the receiver hut.

The tape housing temperature gauge read minus thirteen point eight Celsius — eight-tenths above the lower threshold. She had expected some temperature drop from the forty-three minutes of heater shed; eight-tenths of a degree drop from the 70% protocol norm was within the range the housing was rated to tolerate. The gauge needle was moving: recovering, at the rate she would have expected from the restored heater draw. She unlatched the housing cover and drew her flashlight across the reel.

The timing marks were where they had been that morning. Binder stress zone leading edge: three to four millimeters inside the signal-boundary mark, consistent with the two-angle reading she had logged at 05:41. Eight seconds. She held the beam at the grazing angle and read the surface. She did not need to recalculate; she had not expected to find it different. She latched the cover.

She checked the power reserve gauge on the receiver hut's auxiliary panel: 38%. Three points down from where the morning had ended, the fault response and the afternoon having spent what they had spent.

She stood for a moment with her hand on the housing's outer surface. The housing was doing what it was rated to do. The tape inside it was doing what degraded tape in the cold did. Both facts were accurate, and both were less good than they had been two days ago, and they would be less good than this two days from now.

She went back to the galley.

---

The kettle was ready. She poured the water and let the tea steep and then she sat down at the table, which was the first time she had sat down that afternoon. The mug was warm against both hands.

The tea cooled. She did not drink it. She did not open the fieldwork notebook, which was on the table's corner where she had left it after the window. She did not go to the fax terminal to look at the send queue. She sat at the table with the cooling mug and the fieldwork notebook closed, and on the SSB console, the red standby light was steady — exactly as it always had been, from the first week of winter to this one, unchanging through everything the intervening weeks had changed.

She had not made a decision. She had made a calculation. The two were different things, and she knew which one she had done.
