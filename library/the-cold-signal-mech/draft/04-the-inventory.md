# The Inventory

The minimum-maximum thermometer read minus fourteen point two at the overnight low.

She had mounted it on the lower shelf of the tape housing rack two days ago, angled so the display faced the work chair, and now she knelt on the cold concrete to read it — once, and then again for the decimal — and took her notebook from her breast pocket. *Overnight low (housing shelf, Day 4): –14.2°C. Threshold: –14.0°C.* She looked at the figure. Two tenths below. The instrument's measurement error at these temperatures was approximately plus or minus two tenths Celsius, which made the reading technically consistent with the threshold within instrument tolerance, and she noted this too, and put a bracket around both entries — the field notation she had established in 1974 for values that were numerically consistent with each other while pointing in a direction she needed to track. She had not used that notation in years. She was using it now.

She rose and turned to the housing.

The reel was as she had left it. She had verified the housing latches each morning since Day 2 — both latches seated, foam gasket compressed — and done so again before she read the thermometer. She pulled the inspection gloves from the repair kit and drew the reel from the housing with both hands, hub steady, no lateral torque.

On Day 2 she had checked the monitoring-record section only — the pre-signal tape, the outer reel layers, the zone most exposed to cold — because that was where the first binder stress had appeared and that was where the risk was. Today she unreeled further: past the monitoring section, through the transition to signal-proper, into the opening meters of the signal segment itself. The ten-power loupe against her eye, the flashlight at an oblique angle to catch surface texture rather than illuminate it directly.

The binder stress had extended.

She measured its new leading edge against the scale she had marked on the work shelf when she made the dimensional log on Day 2. The stress zone had grown by approximately three centimeters along the reel edge since then — one and a half centimeters per day. The width of the affected zone was unchanged: still confined to the outer two to three millimeters of the tape's width, still short of the primary recording track by an estimated four to five millimeters.

Under the loupe the boundary between the stressed zone and the undamaged tape was not a clean line. It was a gradient of approximately two millimeters, the binder surface becoming progressively more matte before the condition appeared to stabilize. On Day 2 she had observed the affected zone as a discrete region. Today it had a gradient at its leading edge. The gradient was consistent with active progression.

She noted the new length, the unchanged width, the gradient, and the distance from the leading edge of the stress zone to the boundary between the monitoring section and the signal segment proper: approximately four centimeters. The binder stress had not reached the signal segment. She noted this separately, in its own line, the way she noted anything that was true for now and might not be true later.

She returned the tape to the housing with the same two-handed care, seated the cover, and pressed both latches. She updated the dimensional log on the inspection card clipped to the housing back.

Then she went to the hab.

---

The printouts were in the equipment cabinet on the interior wall, wrapped face-up in the gray thermal blanket. She opened the cabinet, lifted the blanket by one corner, and unrolled Protocol Run 2's printout on the galley table, smoothing it flat with the heels of her hands.

Column by column, left to right. She had done this on Day 1 night with the reading light on in the receiver hut, and then again on Day 2 in better light, and each time she had been reading for a different purpose. Today she was reading for ink. The thermal paper was rated to minus twenty Celsius. Laboratory ratings assumed controlled indoor environments, not a winterover receiver hut where the temperature cycled between minus fourteen and whatever the heater's minimum draw permitted overnight. She had filed a maintenance note about this in April. No one had amended the spec before the team left.

The ink was intact. The frequency-stability data — the densest print format, the section most demanding on the thermal head — was legible and uniformly clear. She unrolled Run 1's printout alongside and compared them: both intact, consistent, no fading in any column across either document. She rolled them back, replaced the blanket, and closed the cabinet.

*Printout inspection, Day 4. Runs 1 and 2: intact. No ink degradation. No thermal-contraction fractures. Condition: preserved.*

She returned to the receiver hut.

---

The analyzer self-diagnostic took four minutes and twenty seconds. She had learned the duration from the three prior times she had run it and did not watch the display during the test — watching produced no information until the display changed, and the kind of attention she would need during the protocol run was not a renewable resource she could afford to spend on waiting. She sat in the work chair and reviewed the notebook entries she had made so far: thermometer, tape, printouts. Three sections. The day was already four hours old.

The chime. She looked at the display.

All subsystems nominal except the tape transport tension mechanism: elevated, consistent with every prior self-test reading back to the morning of Run 1. She had logged the elevated tension in the pre-run checklist before Day 1 as a pre-existing condition; the self-test corroborated it each time. It was not a fault condition. It was an observation without a remedy.

*Analyzer self-diagnostic, Day 4. All subsystems nominal. Tape transport: tension elevated (pre-existing; no fault condition; consistent with all prior self-test reads).*

She shut the interface, put on her oversuit, and went to the equipment building.

---

The fuel gauge read seventy-three point five liters. She logged this in the maintenance record and in the notebook both. Then she checked the voltage regulator output — 218 volts, stable, within the rated band — and opened the overnight fault log.

Two entries, both timestamped between 0300 and 0350: voltage excursions, brief recovery sequences, eight seconds maximum, neither reaching the alarm threshold. The logged voltages before recovery were 206 and 209. She wrote them down with their times and recovery voltages and then: *Two overnight voltage events, 0300–0350. Sub-alarm. No brownout; no warm-up cycle triggered. Fault counter: incrementing.* Two per night for the past two nights. She could not fix the voltage regulator. She could count the events.

Then she ran the arithmetic.

Fuel remaining: 73.5 liters. Daily burn rate at current draw — heater at 70 percent continuous, hab base heating, instrument standby, no protocol runs — she had calculated from the consumption log as approximately 4.5 liters per day. Eight days until the team's return: 36 liters of passive draw. 73.5 minus 36 equaled 37.5 liters for instrument use. Each full protocol run, including warm-up, consumed approximately 3.5 liters above the passive baseline; she had this figure from the consumption delta between days with runs and days without. 37.5 divided by 3.5 was approximately 10.7.

She wrote *~10*, crossed it out, wrote *~8*. She applied a discount to any estimate that assumed an aging Antarctic generator would maintain its calculated baseline through twelve consecutive days of polar winter, and the voltage regulator that was fault-logging twice a night was not a generator she was willing to call consistent. She underlined the eight and wrote below it: *Assumes standard burn rate. Verify against actual gauge in 24h. Do not treat as confirmed.*

Her tracking count said five runs remained after this morning's run — six before it, minus one for Run 3. The fuel arithmetic said eight. The difference between five and eight was not rounding. One of the two calculations was incorporating a wrong assumption. She wrote: *Tracking count: 5 (post-Run 3). Fuel basis: 8 (unverified). Discrepancy: review after next fuel log.* Eight was the more comfortable number. She noted that too.

She went back to the receiver hut.

---

Twelve minutes for the warm-up cycle. She sat in the work chair rather than pacing, which she had tried on Day 2 and found made the twelve minutes feel longer rather than shorter. She looked at the frost line on the east wall, which had been migrating upward since she set the 70 percent heater protocol — the convection in the hut improving as the temperature floor held — and noted its current position: upper wall panel, a third of the way from the bottom of the panel. She would look again in two days.

The chime. She loaded the tape and pressed play.

The monitoring segment ran for forty-two seconds: baseline noise, the ambient radio environment at Winslow before 22:21:52 on Day 1, exactly as it always had been — flat across all bands, the steady unstructured background of a polar location isolated enough from population centers that the UHF spectrum approximated quiet. She listened to it as she had listened on Runs 1 and 2.

The transition marker: two seconds of tonal calibration reference.

Then the first parametric segment began.

The display went flat.

She watched the output range. Nothing across the parametric segment's expected band. She looked at the counter: one second into the segment. Two. Three. Four. Five.

Output returned.

She pressed stop.

She sat with the counter reading for three or four seconds, not thinking anything she would later recognize as a specific thought. Then she rewound to the transition marker.

Played again.

Monitoring segment. Transition marker. The beginning of the first parametric segment.

One second of nothing. Two. Three. Four. Five.

Then the display came alive: the frequency sweep resuming mid-step, the output picking up cleanly at the point where the gap ended. She did not stop the tape. The rest of the segment read without interruption; the second parametric segment came up intact; the core structure read across the display in the same configuration it had produced on Runs 1 and 2. She ran the full protocol sequence to completion. Thirty-seven minutes from tape load to final cross-check. At the end she wrote the validity judgment: *Run 3, Day 4. Validity: conditional. See notebook, tape event entry.*

She read the post-run power gauge: sixty percent.

Then she made the event entry.

*Run 3 — Dropout. Parametric segment 1, seconds 0–5 from segment start. Output: zero across expected range. Blank oxide: not noise, not distortion — blank. Onset: sharp. Recovery: sharp. No output degradation before or after the dropout window. Signal resumes at second 5. Does not affect parametric segment 2, core signature, or calibration reference. First dropout event recorded. Not present in Run 2.*

Sharp onset, sharp recovery: the binder stress at the reel edge had not announced itself as a gradual attenuation. It had produced a clean cutoff at the point where the playback head encountered tape from which the oxide layer had already separated. The gradient she had observed under the loupe — the two-millimeter zone of progressively failing binder at the stress front — was the edge of the transition. Where the oxide loss was complete, the dropout was sharp.

She checked the counter position of the dropout start against the tape map in the notebook: the sketch of the reel layout marking the monitoring-record boundary, the signal segment start, the parametric segment positions, the distance annotations she had been adding since Day 2. The dropout began at approximately the position of the monitoring-record boundary — which was approximately where she had measured the leading edge of the binder stress zone to be.

---

She did the calculation.

Five seconds of dropout where Run 2 had shown none. Two days between runs. If the dropout was growing at the rate the binder stress was progressing — and the dropout boundary and the visible stress boundary were at the same position on the tape, which was not a coincidence she had another explanation for — then the rate was approximately five seconds of dropout per two days. This was one data point. One data point was a measurement, not a rate; she would have a rate when she had two. She used what she had.

At five seconds per two days, the dropout would reach approximately forty seconds into the first parametric segment — the position of the core signature's leading edge, the primary anomalous parameters that made the signal what it was — in roughly sixteen days. At the upper bound of the uncertainty range, which she set at twice the observed rate, in eight.

Eight days was when the LC-130 arrived.

She wrote: *Dropout growth estimate: 5s/2days (one data point; not confirmed rate). Upper bound: 10s/2days. Core signature at risk: 8–16 days from Day 4. Team arrival: Day 12 (8 days).*

She put the pen down. The heater held its steady draw. The display held the amber numerals of the final cross-check output.

---

She was not going to run the protocol again before the gate.

The tracking count said five runs remained. The fuel estimate said eight, but eight required the burn rate to be what she had calculated and she had annotated that estimate *do not treat as confirmed* for a reason. She would use five — the number she had been maintaining since Day 1, the one built from actual costs rather than projected ones. Five was conservative, which was the same thing as saying it was the correct number to plan with.

The gate was approximately seventy-two hours away. Running the protocol before the gate used a run to confirm what Run 3 had already established. If she decided to transmit at the gate, the remaining runs were moot. If she decided to hold and continue working after the gate, she would have five runs and five days to use them in. Either way, burning a run in the next seventy-two hours was spending a resource she did not need to spend to know what she currently knew.

*Decision: no further protocol runs before relay gate. Run 3 results sufficient for current assessment. 5 runs remaining (tracking count). 8 runs (fuel basis, unverified). Gate: ~72h.*

---

She made the summary in the last full block of clean pages in the notebook, one line per resource, working from the notes she had made throughout the day:

*Tape: dropout segment 1, 0–5s. Binder stress: ~1.5cm/day edge extension. Dropout growth: ~5s/2days (one data point). Core signature: intact as of Run 3. Rate: degrading.*

*Printout: intact. No ink degradation. No contraction fractures. Rate: no prior change.*

*Instrument: all nominal. Transport tension elevated (pre-existing). Rate: stable.*

*Power: 60% post-Run 3. Fuel: 73.5L. Run margin: 5 (tracking); 8 (fuel basis, unverified).*

*Gate: ~72h.*

She read through the list twice. Then she closed the notebook.

She carried it to the main hab and set it on top of the equipment cabinet — not inside, because the housing inspection card was binder-clipped to the back cover and she wanted it where she could reach it without opening anything. The two printouts were in the cabinet below, in their blanket, intact.

She had five runs and seventy-two hours to the gate and a tape with a five-second dropout she could locate to within a centimeter on the reel. She had the power reserve to one percentage point and the binder stress extension rate to one and a half centimeters per day. She had the dropout growth rate to five seconds per two days, with an error bound she had set herself and a team arrival date she knew to the hour.

She had not had those numbers at the start of the day.
