# Core Dropout

The voltage output needle had settled into a wider arc than the winter had produced before the fault. Plus or minus seven percent: she had written that figure in the equipment log when she first characterized it this morning, and standing at the panel now with four minutes left on the stabilization timer, the figure was confirmed. The arc completed roughly every twenty-one seconds. It had not narrowed during the fifteen-minute reduced-draw procedure; it would not narrow. What the procedure accomplished was equilibrium at the lower load, not correction of the underlying fault. The fault had been stabilized, not repaired. She had written both terms in the log because the distinction was real and she preferred her records to reflect what they could reflect accurately.

The generator time was 08:21. The timer read 00:03:47.

She had gone to the receiver hut first this morning, before the generator panel, before coffee. The tape inspection constrained everything else; that was the correct order. The housing temperature gauge had read minus fourteen point two Celsius — within the rated band, marginally below the 70% protocol norm but within the measurement uncertainty of a non-laboratory field thermometer. She had accepted it. She had opened the housing cover and drawn the flashlight across the reel at the two standard angles, and what she had found on the reel was larger than it had been on Day 4.

The binder stress zone along the outer edge of the reel — that graduated region she had been measuring since Run 2, marking its position against the pencil lines on the housing interior, tracking the advance of its leading edge toward the signal segment boundary — had widened. The zone itself: wider along the reel edge. The active gradient at the leading edge: steeper, the two-millimeter transition band she had measured on Day 4 now closer to three. She had updated the measurement card clipped to the housing's back panel. She had noted the change. The leading edge was still in the outer circumference of the reel, still in advance of the monitoring-record / signal-segment boundary mark, exactly as the degradation rate she had been projecting predicted it should be.

She closed the housing and came back down the walkway.

The needle completed its arc. 209 to 224 volts, approximately, at a nominal of 216. This was, by any reasonable definition, too wide. The fault had not been in the winter plan, and the widened variance had not been in the winter plan, and the stabilization procedure she was running to compensate for the widened variance had not been in the winter plan. None of those things changed the fact of the needle, which was doing what the needle was doing regardless of what had been in the plan.

The timer reached 00:00:00.

She moved to the analyzer's main circuit breaker.

---

The warm-up cycle ran for four minutes and twenty-two seconds, the amber numerals stepping through their self-test sequence in the prescribed order: input calibration, frequency reference, parametric filter sweep verification, playback head alignment check. She had timed this cycle in Runs 1 and 3 to confirm it was completing within specification. It was completing within specification. The self-test indicators resolved green in sequence — all of them — and the display settled to standby.

She loaded the tape.

The reel seated correctly in the transport mechanism. Spindle engaged. She ran the manual tension check: the resistance was at the lower end of the normal range, where it had been since Run 2, which she had noted in Run 2's log and again in Run 3's, and which was not, by itself, cause to stop. She initialized the playback at the signal marker — the timestamp annotation she had placed in the run log on the first night, which she had transferred to each subsequent run log in the same position — and moved back to the display.

She stood with the pen in hand, the run log open to a fresh page.

She had been in this position four times now. The first time at 22:49 on the night of June first, when the self-test had completed and the amber numerals had steadied and she had stood at the display not yet knowing whether the run would confirm what she had or invalidate it. She had known by 23:11. She had known each time; that was what a run produced: a number where there had been a question, a resolved state where there had been an open one. That was the mechanism. She was running the mechanism a fourth time.

The tape began to move.

First parametric segment: she watched the display. Silence. The opening region of the segment, where the dropout had been since Run 3 — where she had measured it, estimated it, projected its growth from Day 4 through Day 7 — was silent, as expected. She counted the seconds. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight.

The frequency distribution resolved out of the baseline and the parametric profile began to read.

She noted the values as they came through, writing in the log column by column: frequency stability, spectral density, timing parameters, phase relationship across the secondary receiver channels. She wrote quickly but without rushing; the values needed to be legible, not fast. The first parametric profile was coming through cleanly. Frequency stability: consistent with Runs 1–3, within the expected variance range for the instrument and conditions. Spectral density distribution: consistent, the characteristic shape she had first identified on the night of June first, present and unchanged. Timing parameters: matched. Phase relationship: matched.

She kept writing.

The segment ran for what she estimated, by the time counter, as approximately twenty-two seconds from the point at which the silence ended — twenty-two seconds of clean first-parametric-segment data, beginning at the eight-second mark and continuing to the segment's close. The parametric profile described in those twenty-two seconds was internally consistent and matched, in every parameter she could read from the display, the profile she had confirmed three times before.

She noted the segment's end time in the log margin.

The transition followed. She knew this transition: two to three seconds, a structural property of the signal rather than of the tape, a brief gap between the first and second parametric regions that was present because it was a feature of what had been received. She had noted its duration in Run 1's log, confirmed it in Run 2, and confirmed it again in Run 3. Three seconds. She watched it complete.

The transition ended.

She waited for the second parametric segment to begin.

The display showed: baseline. The flat output the analyzer produced when the tape was blank.

She waited. Counted to ten. The baseline continued.

Fifteen seconds.

The baseline continued.

She stopped the run.

---

She rewound to the second segment marker.

Initiated the playback.

The baseline ran. Ran. Ran. At twelve seconds — she was watching the time counter precisely — the display changed. The signal resumed: the calibration tail, the structured end of the event, the final portion of the forty-seven seconds that confirmed the event's total duration. It read cleanly from the twelve-second mark.

She stopped the run. Rewound to the second segment marker.

Same result.

She rewound a third time. Brought the tape back to the marker, started the counter from the beginning of the second segment, initiated the playback, and watched from the start. Twelve seconds of baseline. Then the resumption. Twelve seconds of blank oxide where the second parametric segment should have been.

She did not run it a fourth time.

She let the remainder of the run complete from the twelve-second resumption point: calibration tail, post-event baseline. Both read clearly. The time counter showed the event's total duration: forty-seven seconds. The instrument run counter read 02 when the tape reached the end of the signal section and the playback stopped.

She collected the printout from the output tray and did not look at it yet.

Power reserve: she checked the gauge on the way through the equipment building. Thirty percent. Eight points down from this morning's thirty-eight.

---

She carried the printout to the work chair in the equipment building and sat with it in her lap.

This was the procedure: read the log from the beginning, assuming the error was hers, going through each step in the run sequence, identifying each point at which a mistake might have been introduced, eliminating them in order. This was what she had done with every anomalous result in thirty years of field work, and it was the right approach because the procedure was the mechanism by which she could tell the difference between a real result and an operator error. She had been wrong about results before. She had been careful about this.

Stabilization procedure: completed at the full fifteen-minute interval. Variance at +/-7%, documented and within the range the procedure was designed to address. No fault light active during the stabilization window. No alarm signal.

Warm-up cycle: self-test completed within specification. All self-test indicators green. No instrument fault signal prior to initialization. The voltage variance during warm-up: within the range she had documented.

Tape loading: correct procedure. Reel seated, spindle engaged, tension within the normal range. She had logged the tension reading. No deviation from the procedure at the loading step.

Playback initialization: at the signal marker, the same marker she had used in all prior runs.

First parametric segment: the dropout at seconds 0–8 was consistent with Run 3's confirmed five-second figure and the Day 6 and Day 7 visual estimates of eight seconds. The values from the readable portion of the segment — from second eight forward — matched Runs 1, 2, and 3 within the expected instrument variance.

Transition: duration consistent with prior runs.

Second parametric segment:

She held at the second parametric segment entry in the run log. The log showed: no signal, 0 to 12 seconds. At 12 seconds: signal resumes at calibration tail start position.

She could not construct a procedure error that explained twelve seconds of blank oxide. The stabilization procedure addressed voltage variance in the instrument circuit; it did not affect tape condition. The warm-up cycle addressed instrument calibration; it did not affect tape condition. The tension check addressed the transport mechanism; it did not affect tape condition. A procedure error could explain anomalous values, distorted output, misread frequencies. A procedure error could not produce twelve seconds of blank oxide followed by a clean resumption.

Blank oxide meant the magnetic coating had failed in that region. The signal had been there, recorded by the receiver on the night of June first, and now the surface that had held the recording was gone.

She had not made an error.

She set the run log on the bench and opened the analysis notebook.

---

She opened to a fresh page and wrote at the top: *Run 4 — Day 8.*

She wrote what the run had confirmed.

*First parametric segment (dropout: 0–8s, consistent with Run 3 and Day 6–7 visual estimates):* Frequency distribution, spectral density, timing parameters, and phase relationship consistent with Runs 1–3. Approximately twenty-two seconds of clean data following the dropout onset. Parametric profile intact across all measurable parameters. Primary anomalous parameters: [she wrote them out, three lines of technical notation in the compact form she used for instrument values — eleven entries in two columns, the notation she would use if she were writing a methods section, because it was precise and she did not know a more precise form].

*Third segment (calibration tail):* Intact. Signal duration confirmed: forty-seven seconds.

*Core signature:* The structural characteristics identifying the signal as non-terrestrial and non-natural are present in the first parametric segment and confirmed across four runs.

She wrote what the run had not confirmed.

*Second parametric segment:* Unreadable. Twelve seconds of blank oxide from segment start. Confirmed on three separate playbacks. The binder failure is in the core of the tape record, not at the reel edge. The second parametric segment extends the parametric profile and provides the data required to distinguish between the three alternative classifications the verification protocol was designed to rule out. This segment is not recoverable from this tape by any means available at this station.

She read the entry from the top.

Each sentence was accurate. She confirmed this one sentence at a time, the same way she had gone through the run log.

Then she wrote the accounting.

*What the remaining evidence supports:* The signal was received. Its duration was forty-seven seconds. It was received in the upper UHF monitoring band at 22:21:52 on 1 June 1991 at Winslow Station. The primary anomalous parameters of the first parametric segment are [reference: entries above]. These parameters, considered together, characterize the signal as non-terrestrial and non-natural by the criteria of the verification protocol. This characterization has been confirmed across four instrument runs. A provisional claim on this basis is supportable.

*What the remaining evidence does not support:* The complete parametric characterization the verification protocol was designed to establish — the determination that would exclude all three alternative classifications and allow the finding to be rated as confirmed rather than provisional. The second parametric segment is required for that determination. The 1990 revision to the protocol made this requirement explicit, and the requirement was correct; the first segment alone is insufficient to close the three alternative classifications. The second parametric segment is gone.

She set the pen down on the notebook's spine.

The word *gone* was the right word. She had arrived at it without deliberation and she confirmed it now by reading the sentence. The second parametric segment was gone. It was not degraded; it was not partially readable; it was not accessible by a method she had not yet tried. It was blank oxide. She had played it three times and would get the same result on a fourth, fifth, or sixth play.

The 1990 revision to the protocol had added the second segment's requirement because she had calculated, in 1990, that the first segment alone was insufficient. She had been right in that calculation. The first segment's data — what remained of it, the twenty-two seconds after the dropout — described the signal's primary anomalous parameters precisely and without ambiguity. Those parameters were real. They characterized a real event. A provisional claim built on those parameters was a real claim.

It was not the claim the protocol had been designed to produce.

---

She closed the analysis notebook and carried the run log printout back to the receiver hut.

The protocol folder was on the shelf above the work table: manila, binder-clipped, holding four run log printouts now, plus the initial detection documentation and the three pages of instrument verification notes from the first week. She filed Run 4 behind Run 3. Returned the folder to the shelf.

She stood at the work table.

The tape was in its housing. The signal analyzer was in standby, amber numerals showing the output counter at 02. The heater ran at seventy percent. The housing temperature gauge, from the angle she was standing, read minus fourteen point one.

She had been tracking the binder stress zone along the reel edge for eight days. She had measured it on Day 2, Day 4, Day 6, Day 7, and today. She had projected its advance toward the signal segment boundary and estimated the rate of dropout growth in the first parametric segment from the same data. The measurements had been at the edge because the degradation had been at the edge. She had been watching the right location for the source of the problem she could see.

The second parametric segment was not at the edge.

She stood with the folder for a moment longer, then set it back on the shelf. She put on the outer layer from the hook beside the door and walked back through the walkway to the main hab.
