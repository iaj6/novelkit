# The Window

The clock on the galley wall read 05:11. She had not intended to be awake at 05:11, and she had known for the past hour and a half that she would be. The sleep available to her since midnight had been of the variety that is indistinguishable from lying awake: shallow, resurfacing at intervals of fifteen or twenty minutes, not restful in any accumulating sense. Some time after three she had stopped trying to distinguish between sleep and not-sleep and had lain in the dark with the condensation pattern on the ceiling, which she had been inadvertently mapping since Week 4 of the winterover, until the mapping became its own focused activity. At some point she had gotten up, found her socks on the bedside shelf where she left them each night — they were warmer off the bunk frame — and come through the corridor to the galley.

The overhead tube flickered twice, then held. The ballast was slow in this cold.

The stove pilot took four strikes to catch. She watched the first attempt fail and the second, and on the third the gas ring lit, settling into blue at lower than usual pressure. The propane tank was on the exterior rack; the regulator needed time to equalize at this temperature. She filled the kettle at the water line and set it on the ring.

The mug she took from the drying rack was colder than a galley mug should be. Not room-temperature cold — the galley held around seven or eight degrees with the generator holding — but below that, degree-below-zero cold, as if the corridor had been sharing its temperature with the shelf all night through the cracked hatch seal. The seal had been reported in November. The replacement was in the McMurdo supply manifest due on Day 12, along with the voltage regulator component and five other station items.

The kettle was slow. She stood at the stove. The generator ran in the wall of the equipment building at the pitch it had held since October. She had stopped hearing it as sound several weeks into the winterover; it had become a condition, the way the dark was a condition and the cold was a condition — a thing that was simply true about this building, this hour, the particular situation of being in it. The relay window would open at some point in the next several hours. She had not checked the posted schedule when she went to bed, and she did not look at it now.

When the coffee was ready she drank it at the counter. The mug came up to temperature by the last quarter. She put on her thermal underlayer, then her balaclava, then her outer shell, then her gloves, and she went out the equipment building's side door and into the covered walkway.

---

05:31, by the receiver hut clock.

She worked by flashlight, in the left pocket of her outer shell as it had been since Day 2. She went to the tape housing and unlatched the cover. The mechanism required a two-degree counterclockwise rotation before it would disengage — a tolerance she had documented on her first inspection and had not needed to think about since — and she performed the rotation without thinking about it. She drew the flashlight beam across the reel.

The timing marks were pencil lines on the housing interior: thin, deliberate marks placed on Day 2, supplemented after Run 3. They bracketed the key tape positions: monitoring record start, monitoring record end, signal segment start. After Run 3 she had added a mark at the dropout's confirmed leading edge — five seconds into the signal segment, at the binder stress zone's front as it had been on Day 4. She read each mark in sequence, left to right across the interior wall, before she let herself look at what she had come to look at.

She held the flashlight at the first angle and read the position of the binder stress zone's leading edge against the signal-boundary mark.

She rotated her wrist forty-five degrees and read it at the second angle, to check for refraction artifact.

Both angles gave the same result.

The leading edge was past the signal-boundary mark. Inside the signal segment.

On Day 6 she had estimated, by this same method, that the edge had just reached the boundary. What she was reading now was the edge past it, by a distance she estimated at three to four millimeters. She held the beam at a grazing angle to read the surface of the stress zone's front: three millimeters. Possibly slightly more.

She converted this to time. The signal segment ran at the recorder's standard tape speed; one millimeter of reel at the outermost diameter corresponded to a fraction of a second, a fraction she had calculated on Day 6 and did not need to look up. Three millimeters gave her eight seconds. Four millimeters, eight and a half.

She wrote in the hut log: *8s confirmed, est. 8.5 upper. Two-angle check. No run. 05:41.*

The power reserve was at forty-seven percent. A stabilization procedure and a full protocol pass before the window would draw it low enough that the terminal's load during the window could trigger a brownout. She had worked this out the night before; the arithmetic had settled it before she needed to name it as a decision. It was not a decision. It was the condition under which all other decisions had to be made.

She latched the housing cover and crossed back to the equipment building.

---

The fax terminal was in power-save mode — screen dimmed, session still active. She tapped the space bar and the screen came up. The data packet was in the send queue: one item, addressed and formatted. The cursor sat at the initiation command, exactly where she had left it.

She opened the cover statement.

In the third paragraph: *established under standard verification protocol.* She had read this phrase six or seven times the day before and left it each time. She changed it now: *established through three sequential instrument runs under the same verification protocol.* The revision added the word *three* to a document that had not contained it. Three was an accurate number. It would prompt, in a careful reader, a question she had not answered elsewhere in the document: whether three was sufficient, and sufficient by whose standard. She had not written the answer because the document was not the place for the answer. The question would arise regardless.

She read the revised paragraph. She left it.

0630.

She opened the data packet's full metadata summary and read through it from the beginning: frequency band, upper UHF monitoring channel, non-standard allocation; timestamp, 22:21:52, 1 June 1991, Winslow Station local; duration, forty-seven seconds. The amplitude profile header, five significant figures in each field. The first parametric segment — intact values from the post-dropout position, the dropout explicitly noted: onset character sharp, blank oxide; extent as of Run 3, five seconds instrument-confirmed; visual estimate Day 6, approximately eight seconds. The second parametric segment, clean, values entered without amendment. The verification protocol summary, Runs 1 through 3, methodology, per-run judgment, in her own language.

She read all of it.

She was not reading for errors; she had checked it twice the night before and once before that. It was a specific kind of attention, not checking but adjacent to checking, the state in which one reads a document not because one expects to find something wrong but because sitting in the same room with it without reading it is a different kind of difficult.

She sat at the terminal for some time after that without opening anything.

---

07:02.

The relay carrier signal indicator was dark. The window had not opened. She checked the terminal clock against her watch, found a three-minute discrepancy she could not account for, and set her watch to the terminal.

She returned to the receiver hut.

The signal analyzer was in standby: amber numerals on the top display, frequency reference, local time, backup cell status. The same configuration it held when it was not running. She stood in front of it in the dark and did not turn it on.

On Day 1, the numerals had changed at 22:21:52. She had been in this room. The secondary frequency reference display had updated first — a small calibration event, the kind she ordinarily did not stop for — but the primary display had changed in the same second, and the two changes together were not a configuration the instrument produced in its normal monitoring function. She had counted seconds without intending to. She had still been counting at forty-seven.

She thought about the data packet in the relay buffer eight hours from now. A non-routine instrument return from an isolated Antarctic winter station, received at McMurdo Operations at approximately fourteen hundred local. It would be in the queue with weather telemetry and biological monitoring returns and routine maintenance records from other stations. Someone in the operations center would open it because they opened everything that came through the buffer. They would read the cover statement first: a forty-seven-second detection on the upper UHF non-standard allocation band, verified through three sequential instrument runs under the Hartmann verification protocol, flagged as non-routine per USAP 7.3.1.

She knew the operations liaison who handled non-routine instrument returns at McMurdo Operations. She had been in those offices twice, at program reviews in 1987 and 1989. She knew his name and the name of the program officer above him, and the program officer above that. The chain was not abstract to her. It was specific offices in specific buildings, specific people whose responses to a data return of this character she could model, because she had been observing the institutional response to anomalous Antarctic data for eleven years and she understood how the chain worked: not malevolently, not in bad faith, but according to the procedures available for processing non-routine returns, and the procedures available were not designed for this.

By the time the packet reached someone who could assess what three sequential runs of the Hartmann verification protocol actually meant — who would know to ask whether the tape's current physical condition adequately supported the chain of custody the claim required, whether the dropout notation in the first parametric segment was a routine data quality caveat or a material qualifier for the claim's completeness — by that point the packet would have been processed twice. Each processing would use language that simplified the uncertainty in the ways uncertainty was routinely simplified: accurately, in good faith, in ways that would not be wrong in any individual sentence but would be wrong in aggregate. The first frame set the questions. The questions, once set, were difficult to reset.

She had watched this happen. Not to data of this character — she had no comparison case for data of this character — but to results that were real and correctly identified and that had nonetheless arrived at the public record with the uncertainty managed out of them, simplified out of them, by the first descriptions written by people who did not know the difference between what the data said and what the data had been described as saying. She had been the person other scientists called when it had already happened to their work.

07:50.

She returned to the equipment building.

---

At 0800, the carrier signal indicator went from dark to green.

No transition. The status bar updated: *USAP RELAY — CARRIER LIVE — WINDOW OPEN.* The packet queue: *1 item pending.* The initiation command was F5, the function key whose cap had worn through at the center from years of use, the F and the 5 barely legible.

She picked up the fieldwork notebook from the work surface and found the next blank line.

She wrote: *0800 — window open.*

She recapped the pen and placed it beside the notebook.

The cursor blinked in the transmission buffer. A rectangular cursor at the terminal's default one-second interval. The packet queue showed one item. The carrier indicator was green.

0812.

Twelve minutes. The cursor had blinked through all of them. She had not moved it.

She stood.

She crossed to the equipment building's exterior window — north-facing, set high in the wall above the instrument rack, the building's only glass. She could not see the satellite. She could not see anything at all: the Antarctic dark at sixty-eight degrees south in mid-June was complete in the way that no other dark she had experienced was complete. Not an absence but a quality, the sky at its most reduced state, every variable light source removed except the stars, which were dense enough at this latitude to look like grain rather than discrete points. She had been out in it — she had been looking up into it — on each of the four nights she had come out to the generator panel for the overnight voltage readings, and it had surprised her each time in the same way, with the same surprise, as if the knowledge that the dark was that dark was not knowledge she could hold until she was standing in it.

The window showed her its frame and, faintly, in the lower corner of the glass, the reflection of the terminal's status bar: the green carrier-live indicator, the packet queue text, small and backwards in the dark.

She came back to the terminal and sat down.

The indicator was still green. The packet queue still showed one item.

0822.

She had not moved the cursor. She was aware of this the way she was aware of the generator's sound in the wall — not as a thing she had decided, but as a thing that was simply the case.

She stood. She went through the interior door and across the covered walkway.

---

The receiver hut. The tape housing, the signal analyzer in standby, its amber numerals. She put her hand on the outside of the insulated box. The surface held the temperature differential the heater's intermittent schedule had managed over twelve days — cold, but not the uncontrolled cold of the hut's ambient at night; inside the box, a degree or two warmer than the room's air. The housing was doing what the spec said it would do.

If she transmitted, the agencies received one record: a compressed data summary of a forty-seven-second detection verified through three instrument runs, with a dropout documented at five seconds instrument-confirmed and estimated at eight seconds as of this morning, the second parametric segment clean, the core anomalous parameters in the primary frequency analysis intact as of Run 3. The agencies would want the tape. They would issue instructions for its transport on the Day 12 flight according to the standard procedures for recovered magnetic media from Antarctic operations, which were written for the conditions such media was routinely recovered in. They were not written for reel-to-reel magnetic oxide under binder cold-stress conditions specific to the degree of degradation at this storage interval. There was no procedure that was. There was her note in the cover statement's footer, and there was the word *please* she had written in the first version next to the handling note and then removed, because *please* was not the right register for a formal transmittal cover.

If she did not transmit, she had three runs and five days. The dropout was at eight seconds and the rate of its growth she had two instrument data points to bound, with bounds loose enough that she could not say, with the precision her protocol required, whether the core record would still be intact on Day 12. She could run twice more before the instrument margin reached zero and get tighter bounds. Tighter bounds on a number that was still growing were not the same as a good number. But they were the most she could do.

She thought about Lars. Forty-seven seconds on the secondary UHF band, fourteen minutes before her detection, at a propagation geometry consistent with a common source. Two stations, two instruments, two frequency bands, a timing offset that matched the propagation delay at sixty kilometers. She had told him it was probably ionospheric, on Day 3, on the SSB net, with call signs at either end of the exchange and a formal close, with the transmission audible to anyone on the open band. He had said: *yes, that is what I assumed.* He had accepted it because he had no reason not to; his data in isolation was not inconsistent with an ionospheric explanation. Her data in isolation was inconsistent with any natural explanation she could construct, but she had not told him that. She had not told him she had data of any kind that bore on his reading. She had offered him a plausible alternative explanation for his own anomaly, and he had taken it, and the log was sixty kilometers east in a cabinet under a notation that meant *not this, moved on.*

She could not tell him over the radio. Not in the register of call signs and formal closings, with the transmission audible to the open band. Not without the printout in his hands, not without the propagation geometry and the timing data in front of both of them, in the same room, where the conversation the data required could take the form the data required.

She had been sixty kilometers from an independent confirmation since Day 3.

0838 by the hut clock.

She went back.

---

She sat down at the terminal. The carrier signal indicator was green. The packet queue: *1 item pending.* Ten minutes.

She opened the cover statement and read it through from the beginning.

The header. The frequency band, the timestamp, the duration. She read them in order.

The third paragraph. The revision she had made this morning was there: *three sequential instrument runs under the same verification protocol.* She read the sentence. She read the sentences after it. She read to the paragraph's end.

The first parametric segment summary. Dropout notation. Five seconds instrument-confirmed, eight seconds visual estimate.

The second parametric segment. Clean.

The verification summary. Runs 1 through 3. Methodology. Per-run judgment.

The final paragraph.

The last sentence.

Nothing had changed. The document was what it had been when she had read it the third time the night before and not read it a fourth.

Her hand was on the work surface, to the right of the keyboard.

0848.

The carrier signal indicator went dark.

The terminal's status bar updated: *USAP RELAY — CARRIER LOST — WINDOW CLOSED.*

The packet queue: *1 item pending.*

Elena sat. Her hand was no longer near the keyboard.
