# Sixty Kilometers East

She was writing the morning variance figure when she noticed how little space remained in the column.

The maintenance log was a commercial scientific format — columns pre-printed in millimeter increments, four pages per week, the same size she had been using in field conditions for thirty years because it was the correct size. The voltage variance column had nine entries now, one per day since the winter's first morning check, and when she opened the log to add the Day 9 reading she could see that perhaps three or four lines remained before the column would run out and she would continue in the margin.

She wrote the figure: **+/- 8%.** She drew the direction arrow: **↑.**

The needle was wider in its arc than yesterday. She had timed three cycles before writing the figure — the arc ran approximately twenty-two seconds per cycle at the current load — and the extremes had settled at 209 and 227 against the nominal 218 volts. Plus or minus nine and a half volts. At 218 nominal, that was eight and a half percent, rounded to eight in the log because she was working from visual observation and false precision was worse than the appropriate rounding.

Plus or minus eight. One percentage point wider than the figure she had written in the same column yesterday.

She moved to the secondary gauges. Power reserve: twenty-six percent. Four points down from the thirty she had logged at the end of Run 4 — overnight passive draw, consistent with the established rate for the current operating conditions. She wrote it in the reserve column and left the direction arrow off; the direction was not in question.

Then she ran the arithmetic at the panel, because the panel was where it needed to be done. Each remaining protocol run required a fifteen-minute stabilization procedure before initiation, designed to hold the voltage variance inside the analyzer's safe operating range during the draw. The procedure cost three to four percentage points of reserve above the run itself — she had measured this on Run 4. Three to four points of stabilization plus eight points of baseline run cost: call it eleven to twelve per run total.

At twenty-six percent: two runs, at eleven to twelve points each, left a floor of two to four points. That was the margin. Two runs, barely, if the variance did not widen further, if no brownout occurred during a run and triggered a warm-up cycle.

She had done this arithmetic yesterday with the previous day's figures. The result had been the same: barely. The variance was one percentage point wider today, which had tightened the barely without changing its name.

She closed the log and set it on the panel shelf. No instrument run this morning. The Lars contact was scheduled at 1400, and spending a run before the contact would be committing a resource to an outcome she hadn't confirmed she needed. She went through the remaining readings in the order she had established: fuel gauge (43.8 liters, consistent with the 5.2-liter-per-day burn rate), heating circuit draw (within the 70% protocol range), receiver hut building monitor (operational, no alarm). She recorded each reading and left the instrument margin column blank.

---

The receiver hut was minus 14.1 when she pushed through the door, the heater running at seventy percent and the instrument rack in standby and the amber numerals on the signal analyzer steady in the standby mode they had been in since Run 4 yesterday. She unclipped the tape housing, opened the cover, and took the flashlight from the work table.

Two standard angles. First angle at the leading edge of the binder stress zone; second angle across the zone's widest extent along the reel edge. The zone had progressed — she could see it, the gradient slightly wider at the leading edge, the zone's overall extent along the reel edge more visible against the clean oxide on either side. She updated the housing inspection card: *Day 9, visual check, zone larger than Day 8, consistent with established progression rate.* 

She did not write a dropout figure. The last instrument-confirmed figure was Run 4's confirmed eight-second dropout in the first parametric segment. Without a run today she had no number beyond the visual estimate, and the visual estimate was of the stress zone's physical extent, not the dropout's acoustic duration. A layer of inference she did not trust enough to record as measurement. The inspection card notation meant she had a direction but not a number, and in her protocol a direction without a number was a provisional assessment.

She filed the provisional assessment and went back up the walkway.

---

In the equipment building she opened the field reference binder to the antenna orientation notes from the setup week in March. The sky region she had been monitoring on the night of June first: azimuth and elevation fixed in the protocol, right ascension and declination of the field center entered in two columns during setup, the bearing from Winslow to the field's center at the relevant hour derivable from those coordinates and the time of observation.

She had looked at these notes before — in the days after Day 1, when she had been trying to determine whether the sky region she was monitoring was in any geometric relation to the location of Mather Station. The bearing was consistent. She confirmed this again now, checking her entry against the orientation notes, then checking the compass bearing to Mather Station against the geographic reference entries someone had written into the binder during a previous season.

Without Lars's precise timestamp, his antenna geometry, his calibration state on June first, and the raw log trace from his secondary background channel, she could not turn bearing consistency into a calculation. What she had was a direction from Winslow to the monitored sky region, a direction from Winslow to Mather Station sixty kilometers east, and the fact that these two directions were not in contradiction. She had known this since Day 2. She could still not do more with it than she could do then.

She closed the binder and set it back on the shelf.

She made coffee, sat at the work table, and did not open the analysis notebook or the contact log or any document that required something added to it. She had spent nine days in this equipment building and she was practiced at waiting without turning the waiting into an occasion. The generator ran at its current load and the voltage needle completed its arcs, twenty-two seconds each, in the rhythm she could time without looking. Outside the small windows of the equipment building: the Antarctic dark, complete and consistent, minus thirty-eight on the exterior sensor.

The time was 11:22.

The contact window would open at 1400.

---

At 1354 she sat at the SSB console and clicked the frequency live.

"Winslow Station, Winslow Station. This is Mather Station. Do you copy."

Lars Engström's voice through the speaker: five by four, the same quality it had been on every contact. She gave him Winslow's report.

Weather first. His wind event had cleared forty-eight hours ago — pressure 29.9 and holding, surface drift light, antenna systems stable since the gusts passed. His overnight low: minus thirty-six. She gave him hers: stable, minus thirty-eight, no precipitation, barometric pressure 29.8 and rising.

He gave her his week's science summary. The atmospheric chemistry results had been within baseline predictions across all monitored compounds. His week-fourteen extraction was complete and the initial mass spectrometer read had come in consistent with the six weeks of baseline preceding it. Equipment calibration holding. Four weeks remaining on his winterover schedule; he expected to reach his program targets. He said it had been a productive season by the data, if a quiet one.

She told him about the generator: Day 7 fault, amber regulator light, variance widened to eight percent now, stabilization procedure required before each remaining instrument run, replacement component arriving on the twelfth. He asked: main unit or backup? Main unit. Did she have what she needed to manage the remaining days? She said she had two runs remaining and would manage them carefully. He said he was sorry the fault had come when it had.

There was a pause in the traffic. The SSB carrier held steady.

Lars said: "I've been going through the instrument logs this week for the monthly report. I wanted to follow up on something I mentioned a few weeks ago."

She had the contact log open and the pen in her hand. "All right," she said.

"The anomalous reading in the UHF secondary band. The report requires me to formally document or formally discard anomalous readings, so I went back to it and timed it from the log trace." On Day 3 he had said: about a minute, perhaps slightly less. She had been working with that timing window since. "I want to give you more precise parameters."

"Go ahead," she said.

"Duration: forty-seven seconds." He said it without emphasis, the same way he said every technical figure. "I should qualify that. The logging interval on the secondary background channel is two seconds, so there's inherent timing uncertainty. I'm stating it as forty-six to forty-eight seconds, center value forty-seven. That's the best estimate the log trace gives me."

She said: "Repeat the duration."

A brief pause — the radio pause of someone confirming they had heard the request correctly. Then Lars repeated it: "Forty-seven seconds. Forty-six to forty-eight, center forty-seven. Two-second logging resolution."

She wrote in the contact log: *47s.* She kept the pen on the page. "Continue," she said.

"Frequency: 432.275 megahertz. The secondary background logger is a passive reference system — it runs continuously, not targeted at any program band. The shift-review software flags events above a threshold at shift end; that's how this reading came to my attention." The detection method she had known from Day 3. The frequency she had not had. "Timestamp: approximately 2208 local on June first. I've cross-checked against adjacent entries in the primary data record. I'm confident in a two-minute window centered on 2208, within the logging interval resolution."

She looked at the contact log. She looked at what she had written: *47s. 432.275 MHz. 2208 June 1.*

She had received her signal at 22:21:52.

"Has this been entered in your formal station log?" she said.

"As an unresolved instrument artifact. I ran full diagnostics on the secondary background system — no equipment fault found. The timing was consistent with a maintenance cycle we ran that week, which is my working attribution. I'll note it in the monthly report as unresolved anomaly, insufficient data for definitive classification."

She said: "What frequency exactly."

He gave it again. She wrote it again, below the first entry, the same pen pressure she used for generator readings and fuel figures: **432.275 MHz.**

"I had something similar here," she said. "I think you're right about RFI."

"Good to know it's not just us," Lars said. "Strange month."

Standard traffic close. Call signs exchanged, signal quality confirmed, formal sign-off. She wrote the contact end time: 1421. She set the handset in the cradle.

She did not move the hand from the cradle for a moment. Then she moved it toward the transmit key and held it near the key without pressing it. Then she moved it back to the table.

She clicked off the carrier. The standby light went from green to red, as it always did.

---

What Lars had in his secondary background channel: a forty-seven-second event at 432.275 megahertz, timestamped approximately 2208 on June first, detected by a passive monitoring system running continuously through the winter, attributed provisionally to local RFI from a maintenance cycle, filed as unresolved artifact in the formal station log, documented for the monthly report as unresolved anomaly, insufficient data for definitive classification.

She had received a forty-seven-second signal at 22:21:52.

Thirteen minutes and fifty-two seconds between those two timestamps. Within Lars's stated two-minute precision window, and within her own instrument's precision.

Mather Station was sixty kilometers east on a bearing she had checked against the field reference binder this morning and confirmed was consistent with the direction of the sky region she had been monitoring on June first. A signal received first at the station further east, at a different frequency band on a different instrument in a different electronics chain, fourteen minutes before the station sixty kilometers to the west received it on a primary monitoring instrument in the upper UHF band: the geometry was consistent with a directed signal from a source bearing east, the beam's arrival at each station a function of that station's position. She had not run the geometry; she could not run it without Lars's raw log trace and his antenna geometry, and she did not have those. But the geometry did not contradict her.

Not proof. Consistent. Two stations. Two instruments. Two frequency bands. Two independent detection events, fourteen minutes apart, sixty kilometers of Antarctic ice sheet between them.

She went to the work table and opened the fieldwork notebook to the analysis section.

She turned to the page after the Run 4 entry and wrote at the top of the fresh page:

*Mather Station (L. Engström). 47s event. 432.275 MHz, UHF secondary, passive background logger. Timestamp approx. 2208 local June 1. T-14 min relative to Winslow detection (22:21:52).*

*Filed: unresolved instrument artifact, Mather station log. Attribution (Engström): possible RFI from maintenance cycle. No equipment fault on diagnostics.*

*Geometry: Mather 60 km east. Bearing consistent with monitored sky region. 14-minute offset consistent with common source, directed signal. Not verified. Raw log data inaccessible.*

She looked at the last line and added: *Status: inaccessible without disclosure.*

She read the entry sentence by sentence.

The SSB console was six feet away. The frequency dial was still set to the contact frequency. The carrier was off; she could switch it live in a moment and reach Mather Station before the contact window closed.

She could say: *Lars, I need the raw log trace for the secondary background channel, June first, 2200 to 2230.* He would ask why. She had said, not fifteen minutes ago, that she thought he was right about RFI. A request for the raw log trace was not consistent with someone who thought they were looking at RFI, and Lars would hear this the way he heard everything on the frequency — not with suspicion, but with the precise attention of a scientist working alone in the dark who had learned to parse radio traffic carefully because everything that mattered came through it.

He would ask why. She would have to answer.

Nine days of not answering.

She looked at what she had written in the contact log: *I had something similar here. I think you're right about RFI.* She had said it in the contact register. The same register she used for the barometric pressure and the heating circuit draw and the contact end time. Lars had heard it and moved to the traffic close, because there was nothing in that register to suggest he should not.

She could not un-say it. She could only say something after it that would require explaining the thing before it, which would require explaining everything before that.

She did not go to the console.

She took the contact log page and folded it once and placed it inside the back cover of the fieldwork notebook, behind the housing inspection card. She closed the notebook.

Three days to foreclosure. She knew the number without writing it.

She walked through the equipment building and out the far door into the main hab corridor, and the door sealed behind her with the sound it always had, which was the same sound it had made on every day of the nine days she had been here alone, and the number at the bottom of what she had not done was three.
