# The Last Run

The generator was holding. Her watch read five thirty-one.

She pushed back the sleeping bag and sat up.

---

The galley was in its winter-night setting: amber overhead at minimum output, the red standby light on the SSB console, the auxiliary heater's amber indicator below the equipment cabinet. She did not turn on the working lights. She went to the counter and ran the gas burner and stood with her hands flat on the counter while the water heated.

The mug she used was the same one she had been using since Day 1. She had noticed this on Day 6 and had made no change to it. In six hours or so the galley would have other people in it — other mugs, other voices, the ordinary occupation of a crew returning from a rotation. She held the counter and listened to the water.

This was the last morning she was the only person in this station. She had known it since writing *1300 hrs* in the contact log the evening before, and she knew it again now, in the way that knowing something before sleep and knowing it after are the same thing and not the same thing. The hab had the quality it had held for eleven nights — the single-occupancy silence, the sleeping bag on one bunk only, the standby light the only color in the dark. By afternoon it would be gone.

She poured the coffee and held the mug.

The face-down page was on the corner of the galley table. She did not go to it.

---

She lifted the tape housing from the equipment cabinet's top shelf. The indicator was in range: the auxiliary heater had held its minimum through the night, keeping the cabinet several degrees above the hab's ambient at the lower shelf. She confirmed the indicator and put on her coat and carried the housing through the hatch.

The walkway was thirty-one paces. At the midpoint, where the structure had a gap at each joint, the cold held its own corridor — a few degrees sharper than either end, the draft finding the gap from both directions. She had crossed the walkway several hundred times since the first night and had stopped registering the midpoint as anything other than distance. At the midpoint she was equidistant; past it she was closer to the hut. The cold-start thermometer above the equipment-building hatch read minus forty-two. In the receiver hut the wall thermometer inside the door read minus thirteen point one.

Two degrees colder than last evening.

She set the housing on the rack, confirmed the contact indicators, and switched on the space heater. The element cycled on. She watched the rack-temperature indicator through three heater cycles and went to the equipment building.

---

The fault log held six overnight excursions.

The two entries in the early morning hours — four-fifteen to four-twenty-six, and four-forty to four-forty-nine — were the longest she had logged since the first week: sixty-one seconds and forty-nine seconds respectively, the regulator correcting more slowly in each case than it had been through the middle of the investigation. The cold-start variance at zero-six-hundred: plus or minus nine percent.

The worst of the twelve mornings.

She made the margin notation, set the log down, and ran the stabilization procedure.

Heater-load shed — the main hab's strip heat switched to battery backup, its draw removed from the generator's resistive load. Instrument racks to standby minimum. The variance gauge moved: seven point three at the first check interval, six point eight, six point two. She held the configuration. In the minutes that followed she stood at the bench and watched the gauge through its intervals. The overhead lamp was on the battery circuit; it did not flicker. The fault log sat where she had left it.

The gauge held at six point two through a full cycle interval and settled back to six point five. She did not adjust. Below six was where she had been operating from in the early part of the investigation; the compounding cold and the regulator fault had moved the floor upward through the run. She held the configuration and waited.

On the next interval the gauge reached five point five and held.

She confirmed the reading through two consecutive intervals before restoring the instrument circuit. Five point five, stable.

Twenty-two minutes. She had been stabilizing in fifteen most mornings since she had learned the fault pattern. This morning had taken twenty-two.

Power gauge after stabilization: approximately seven percent. The run would cost another four to five points. She would come out at approximately two to three percent — above the two-percent automatic shutdown, which was what the arithmetic required, and not significantly above it.

She went back to the receiver hut.

---

The thermometer inside the door read minus eleven point four. The rack indicators confirmed the instrument was at operating temperature. She mounted the tape housing in the feed mechanism, confirmed the tape contacts, and initiated the warm-up cycle.

The analyzer's amber display ran through its sequence: diagnostic sweep, calibration reference, filter check, pre-run checksum. She had watched this sequence five times. She watched it a sixth time, in the same order, at the same pace. Each step completed as specified. The display showed ready.

She started the run.

---

The tape fed through. The opening interval: nine or ten seconds of reel-edge silence, the leading absence before the signal content began. She had measured it at nine seconds in Run 5 and did not try to improve on the number today. The silence was there and then it was over, the way a record's lead-in precedes the music you already know is coming.

The first parametric segment.

She sat with her hands in her lap.

She had heard this five times. The frequency distribution in its characteristic envelope. The amplitude profile at the primary anomalous parameters, unchanged across six runs and twelve days. The phase structure tracking from run to run across tape that had been losing material at a measurable rate since Day 8. The signal existed in the part of the tape the binder had not yet taken. That part had not changed — tapes degrade but do not alter what they preserve — and what was preserved in the first parametric segment was the same as it had been on Run 1.

The five-point protocol had been designed to establish these parameters definitively. Six runs had done that, for this segment.

She did not annotate. There was nothing new to annotate.

The first parametric segment completed.

---

The transition gap.

She had been watching the counter at this moment since Run 4, when the gap first appeared as something to measure. Twelve seconds in Run 4. Twenty seconds in Run 5.

She watched the counter.

Twenty-three.

When the calibration tail began she confirmed against the analyzer's internal log: twenty-three seconds, the two measurements in agreement. Consistent with the progression she had been logging since Day 8. The calibration tail ran through. Intact. Forty-seven seconds total. The display settled to its post-run idle state.

Instrument margin: zero.

---

The run log printed line by line. She listened without looking at it, and when the printer stopped she pulled the sheet from the tray. The thermal paper was warm from the print head. It did not stay warm.

She opened the analysis notebook.

*Run 6 complete. Day 12, 0640. Dropout: 23s (consistent with progression). Core signature: intact. First parametric segment: complete. Second parametric segment: absent (as of Run 4). Instrument margin: 0. Power reserve: approximately 3%. This is the final run.*

She wrote the date, initialed the entry, and set the pen in the notebook's spine.

She sat in the work chair.

The space heater cycled. The receiver hut held its diesel smell, its cold, the amber glow of the analyzer's idle display. She had spent a significant portion of the last twelve days in this chair. She had not tracked how many hours.

She closed the analyzer's housing, working the latching mechanism with the deliberate pressure it required at low temperature. The latch engaged. She filed the run log in the protocol folder in its correct position — after Run 5, in the slot she had left — and closed the folder. Runs 1 through 6, twelve days, complete in the format the protocol specified.

---

She carried the evidence package to the main hab in two trips: the tape housing and protocol folder first, then the wrapped printout and the analysis notebook. On the equipment cabinet's top shelf she arranged them in order — the tape housing at the left, the wrapped printout roll beside it, the protocol folder, the analysis notebook. On the lower shelf, in the documentation kit, the twelve frames of exposed film sat in their housing. The antenna log and station timekeeping records remained in the equipment building on their standard shelf, where they had been since Day 1.

She stood at the cabinet.

The housing, the wrapped printout, the folder, the notebook — the full record of twelve days on a single shelf. The photographs on Roll F documented a printout that had been more legible when she photographed it than it was now. The evidence summary in the notebook said, in the language of a method section, what the evidence was and what it was not. All of it waiting for thirteen hundred.

She had not thought of anything she had not thought of.

The record was what it was. It would not improve before thirteen hundred.

---

She went to the galley table and picked up the face-down page.

She turned it over and read what she had written. Six lines, stopping mid-sentence in the third. The handwriting was her instrument-log hand — the same hand that had produced run logs for thirty years — but in a register that was not quite a log and not quite the evidence summary; specific and careful in a different direction. She went through the lines from the beginning, at the pace they required. When she reached the sentence she had not finished, she read to where it stopped and did not go further.

She folded the page once along its horizontal center.

She opened the analysis notebook to the last written entry and slid the folded page into the space between that entry and the back cover. She closed the notebook and returned it to the shelf with the rest of the evidence package.

---

She put on her coat and went to the equipment building.

At the SSB radio console she pulled out the work chair and sat. The red standby light was steady. She confirmed the frequency setting — unchanged from the eighteen hundred contact the evening before — and picked up the handset.

She pressed the warm-up control. The red indicator shifted from standby to active, the carrier rising in the background of the channel.

She held the handset.

The LC-130 was five hours out.
