# Anomalous

The power gauge on the generator panel read sixty-seven percent.

She logged it in the maintenance record without stopping to calculate — she had already done the arithmetic in the walkway, between the hab hatch and the equipment building door. Sixty-seven from seventy-one was four points overnight, no instrument run, just the base draws of the space heater in the receiver hut and the hab's base heating circuit and the instrument racks on standby. Four points for sixteen hours of Antarctic polar winter with nothing running except what could not be switched off without losing temperature stability or warm-up advantage. She wrote: *0748. Power 67%. Overnight passive draw: 4% (16h baseline). No instrument run.*

She noted the voltage regulator output — 218 volts, stable, within the rated band — and checked the fault log for overnight events. The log was blank. No brownout flag, no thermal-trip notation, no regulator recovery sequence. The generator had run through the dark without incident, which was what a generator was supposed to do and was not something she could rely on as a design assumption.

She initialed the entry and set the clipboard on the panel hook.

Four days to the relay window. Day 3 was where she was, Day 7 was where the window opened, and four multiplied by twenty-four was ninety-six hours, which was the number she had been aware of since waking and was still aware of now, held in the way she held numbers she could not yet use. She did not write ninety-six hours anywhere. Writing it would not change it.

The SSB contact with Lars Engström was scheduled for 1400.

---

The receiver hut thermometer: minus fourteen.

She had crossed the walkway at 0753 and the walkway had been as cold as it always was — the same diesel exhaust from the umbilical conduit overhead, the same contraction of the metal at the joints. In the hut the space heater ran at the 70 percent setting she had established on Day 2 morning. The thermometer showed that this setting, held through ten hours of unmonitored operation, had produced minus fourteen, which was the temperature the 70 percent protocol was designed to produce. She was not surprised. She was, still, specifically relieved each time the thermometer confirmed what the protocol predicted.

She pulled the housing temperature sensor record. Thirty-minute logging interval; overnight column read in sequence from the bottom. Low point: 0224 hours, minus fourteen point one Celsius. The housing had tracked the hut temperature through the night, as it had since she had understood that it would. She noted the overnight low in the inspection log.

She did not start the signal analyzer. She did not open the tape housing.

She checked the housing's external seal — the foam gasket, all four latches compressed against the door frame — and then the secondary amplifier output connection at the left end of the rack. She had established this as a daily verification point on Run 1's morning, when she had learned to take nothing for granted in a cold that worked on connections the way cold worked on connections. The seal was seated. The connection was firm.

She took her personal fieldwork notebook from the breast pocket of her oversuit and sat in the work chair. Not the station log — the station log was for operational entries formatted for the USAP administrative chain, a future reader who would want data and dates and time-stamped signatures. The notebook was her own: spiral-bound, the cover worn at its upper left corner from thirty years of insertion and extraction from a breast pocket, whose first pages showed the cramped leftward handwriting of her thirties and whose recent pages showed the slightly enlarged, deliberate script she had settled into by the time she had stopped writing for audiences and started writing for her own reference.

She wrote for four minutes. She was aware of the duration because she had the habit, in field conditions, of noting entry length. Then she closed the notebook and returned it to her breast pocket.

The hut made no sound except the thin hiss of the heater's element and, at intervals, the tick and small creak of the roof panel at its northern weld — the signature of this roof at temperatures below minus twelve, exactly as it always had been since October when she had logged it and concluded it was structural and stopped logging it. She sat for another minute without moving.

The two printouts were in the hab equipment cabinet. She had put them there yesterday morning, wrapped face-up in the gray thermal blanket. Nothing had opened the cabinet since. She confirmed this as a fact she knew, not a visual check. They were where she had put them.

She went back across the walkway at 0811.

---

The SSB console was at the far end of the equipment building, below the east-facing porthole. The porthole was a circle of black — it would remain so until July gave way to August, and August gave way to the first rim of twilight that was technically not yet light. She pulled the operator's chair and sat.

The Mather contact was at 1400. She had five hours and forty-nine minutes.

She picked up the maintenance log from the panel hook, reviewed what she had written — the power gauge entry, the voltage reading, the fault notation, her initials. Nothing to add. She set it down. She looked at the handset in its cradle. The coiled cord to the transceiver panel. The red standby light above the mount.

She thought, without yet putting it into language she could dispute, about what it would mean to tell Lars Engström what she had. Not what it would mean for the data, or for the question of who framed what first — those were downstream problems, and she had a working practice of not advancing past downstream problems to get to the present one. What she thought about was the shape of the contact itself. The net had a structure: station status, weather conditions, maintenance, science update, close. Both of them used this structure. There was no slot in that structure for what she was sitting on, and the structure was not a thing she had designed and not a thing she could rewrite at 1400 this afternoon.

There was also the question of what Lars would do with the information if she gave it to him. This was a question she could not answer, because she had not given it to him, and she had a practice of not pre-writing conversations she had not yet had. Lars was careful and methodical and she trusted him more than she let on in the standard register of the net. This was also not relevant to what she could say over a radio.

She went to the galley and put the kettle on. While it heated she stood at the bench without eating anything and thought about nothing specific, which was its own kind of discipline. She brought the mug back to the equipment building and sat at the console with the radio on standby. The clock showed 0836.

She had not reached a conclusion about Lars. She had not needed to by 0836 and the quality of whatever she arrived at by 1400 was unlikely to be improved by arriving at it sooner.

---

At 1357 she switched the transceiver from standby to monitoring and ran the antenna orientation check — the HF antenna was fixed, but the contact log required the notation. Background on the Mather net frequency: the standard HF texture of a winter polar band, the variable hiss with its occasional discrete burst, nothing that flagged in the way a signal flagged.

At 1400 she keyed the handset.

"Mather Base, Mather Base, this is Winslow. Winslow calling Mather. Over."

The carrier came up after approximately two seconds — the propagation path from Mather, sixty kilometers east through the polar atmosphere, plus the standard processing delay. Then Lars Engström's voice, with the flutter that sixty kilometers of ionospheric path introduced at these latitudes: not distortion, but a kind of thinning, the upper register compressed, the low frequencies attenuated. She had been hearing Lars through that thinning for the entire winter and she knew the difference between HF and Lars.

"Winslow, this is Mather. Good afternoon, Elena. I read you five by four. Over."

"Good afternoon, Lars. Five by four here as well. Winslow station status: all normal. Generator stable. Instrument systems on standby. Over."

"Mather status normal. I will note for the record: we had a wind event two days ago — gusts to approximately forty knots, sustained over five hours. No structural damage to the instrument pad or the antenna mast. Wind under ten knots since yesterday evening. Barometer at twenty-nine point seven five this morning, rising. Over."

She logged the Mather weather report in the daily net record. "Copy on the wind event. Winslow barometer at twenty-nine point six, also rising. No wind events in the last thirty-six hours. Over."

Lars reported ice conditions at Mather — new sea-ice formation along the eastern exposure of the instrument pad, two days' accumulation, consistent with the season — and noted that he had reinforced the antenna mast guy-wires after the wind event as a precaution. He asked about the HF antenna at Winslow.

"Nominal," she said. "I checked the mast connections before the team left on Day 1. No movement since. Over."

"Good. And your generator — you mentioned on our last contact the voltage regulator had been giving intermittent readings. Over."

"It has been stable since then," she said. "Overnight fault log clean. The brownout during my last protocol run did not affect the primary data. Over."

"I am glad to hear it. Generator problems in a winter alone are not comfortable." A brief, standard pause — the ionospheric half-second, not a conversational hesitation. "Over."

"No," she said. "They are not. Over."

Lars's science update: seven weeks into the winterover season and his atmospheric chemistry program had produced what he described, with the specific flatness she had learned to read as his version of irony, as results that were thoroughly routine. "Seven weeks and I have very little to show for it that a graduate student in April could not have predicted," he said. "Ice-core gas concentrations within my baseline range, no anomalous spikes in any of the monitored volatile compounds, equipment running within calibration. Routine, which is what one hopes for and then, upon achieving it, finds leaves something to be desired." A pause. "Over."

"Atmospheric chemistry is a program designed for patience," she said.

"It is. As is yours, I would think. Over."

"As is mine. Over."

The standard silence at the end of the science update — the place in the contact where, if both parties had said what there was to say, one of them began the formal close. Elena waited.

"One other thing," Lars said. "It is probably nothing. I mention it only because it was a small oddity at the time. Last week I had some anomalous instrument readings — a short-duration transient event in a side band I was not closely monitoring. My shift-review software flagged it. I ran my instrument diagnostics afterward: no fault condition, no hardware issue. I filed it and moved on. My working assumption is instrument artifact. Over."

"What kind of anomaly?" She had been maintaining a tone across the previous twenty-six minutes. She maintained it now.

"A signal in a UHF frequency range I was not targeting — my secondary background logger, not a primary program instrument. A transient; brief; caught only because the shift-review software was watching the band. I ran the full diagnostic suite. No fault condition in the instrument. My suspicion is RFI from our own station equipment during a maintenance cycle we ran that week. Over."

The pen was in her hand. She had not written anything in the net log since the ice-condition entry. She was not writing now. "Roughly how long did it last?"

There was the propagation half-second. "About a minute, perhaps slightly less. It was not something I spent time on." A brief pause, then: "Over."

"I have been seeing some side-band anomalies as well," she said. "Probably the ionosphere. Over."

"The ionosphere has been producing unusual conditions this winter at these latitudes — the monitoring forecasts have not consistently matched the observed disturbance pattern. It is plausible. Over."

"Yes," she said. "Over."

She signed off. Standard traffic close: she read the weather summary into the net log, he acknowledged, she gave the station identification, she signed off from the frequency. She racked the handset.

The carrier dropped. The HF background hiss closed with the squelch. The clock on the wall showed 1429.

Twenty-nine minutes on frequency.

---

She did not move from the chair.

Lars had said: last week. A UHF frequency he was not targeting. A short-duration transient. About a minute, perhaps slightly less.

She worked through what she knew about Mather Station's monitoring configuration — what she had gathered from three seasons of SSB contact and one face-to-face briefing at McMurdo, before the current winterover. Lars's primary instruments were the atmospheric chemistry suite: gas analyzers, isotope ratio sensors, auxiliary recording hardware for the program's baseline. His secondary background logger was a passive system, not a program instrument. It ran continuously, logged data to tape as a reference channel, and he would not examine it closely in the normal course of his program. The shift-review software watched for events above a threshold and generated flags for the shift-end review. He had noticed the transient because the software had flagged it.

For the software to flag it, the event had to have exceeded the threshold in that band. It had been large relative to his normal background.

Mather Station was sixty kilometers east. A signal originating from a source sufficiently distant from both stations — not from either station's local equipment, not from a satellite in low orbit, not from any source she could locate in the terrestrial catalog she had been running since the night of Day 1 — would reach Mather and Winslow along paths that differed by an interval determined by the source's bearing and the stations' separation. This was a calculation she had not run. She had not needed to run it before Day 1. She did not have the input parameters to run it now.

Lars had said: last week. He had said a maintenance cycle ran that week, and that was his RFI hypothesis. He had not given her the date of the event. He had not given her the frequency within the UHF secondary band — only that it was a band he was not targeting, which described a significant range. He had not given her the event time.

What she had: about a minute, perhaps slightly less. UHF secondary band. Passive logger. The flag threshold had been exceeded. Filed as instrument artifact. A week ago.

The day that was a week ago was consistent with a window that included Day 1.

She could not run the comparison she needed to run. She did not have the source geometry — she had never had it — and Lars had not given her the parameters that would let her compare his event to hers. She could not ask for those parameters without explaining why she was asking. She could not explain why she was asking without telling him what she had. That was not a conclusion she had reached; it was the shape of the thing.

The questions she had asked — what kind of anomaly, roughly how long — were the most she could ask in the SSB register without directing his attention somewhere she was not ready to direct it. She did not think his attention had been directed. He had answered without emphasis, the way a careful scientist reported something he had already decided was not significant.

She went to the galley and put the kettle on. She stood at the bench while it heated. The corridor hatch seal let in the cold at its lower edge, as it had every day since October when she had written the maintenance note that no one had yet acted on. She was aware of it in the way she was aware of all the station's small failings: logged, not resolved, not within her capacity to resolve before the team returned.

She poured the water into the green-enameled mug and held it with both hands. Her hands ran cold even here, even with the galley's base heating running.

Lars had filed it. The notation was in the Mather Station instrument record — in his handwriting or in the shift-review software's automated entry, with whatever parameters the logging system had captured — sixty kilometers east, in a station she could not reach overland in winter. The log entry existed whether or not she was standing in her galley thinking about it. It would exist tomorrow, and the day after, and on Day 7 when the relay window opened, and on Day 12 when the LC-130 landed. It was not going anywhere.

She set the mug on the drying rack. She did not go back to the console.

About a minute, perhaps slightly less. He had not spent time on it.
