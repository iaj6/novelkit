# The Plane

She heard it before she saw it.

The four-prop drone came from the northwest — faint at first, distinguishable from the generator's note only by pitch and by the way it moved across the silence rather than held in one place. She had been listening to the generator for twelve days. She did not need to decide what this was.

She was already in full cold-weather gear: the insulated suit, the outer shell, gloves doubled, the gaiter pulled to her chin. She had come out at twelve twenty and had been standing at the edge of the landing strip apron since then. Both hands at her sides. The sky held its flat blue-gray noon light — the light that arrived around eleven and held until two, the closest this latitude and this month came to what elsewhere would be called day. There was no wind. At minus thirty-eight with no wind, you stood still and the cold arrived evenly, without insistence, and you stayed in it for as long as was required.

The sound enlarged.

Then she could see it: a shape against the gray that grew in the way that meant size rather than approach. The four props were identifiable at this distance by their configuration, the Hercules pattern, the regular spacing she knew from a dozen arrivals at half a dozen stations over thirty years. She watched it come in on the low-pass heading — one circuit to check the strip, the same procedure it had always been. The aircraft crossed overhead with a sound that was very loud for a moment. Then it was past, climbing south, setting up for the second approach.

She stood. The sound receded and came back on a different angle.

The ski struts came down on the second approach — she could see the shape change at the fuselage at this altitude — and then the aircraft settled onto the strip: not an impact but a finding, the skis locating the surface and drawing two shallow parallel tracks that appeared simultaneously behind the point of first contact and extended back into the snow. The engines cycled down. They held at a ground-idle note that was lower than the flight pitch and occupied the air above the strip in a different way.

She had not moved. The cargo ramp at the rear of the aircraft began to descend.

---

Walczak was first out.

He came down the ramp and stopped at the bottom and read the station. The antenna array. The equipment building. The receiver hut. The hab. Left to right, the engineer's inventory, looking for what twelve days had changed or broken. She had seen him do this twice before at other stations on other returns, and he did it the same way each time — the same order, the same pause at each element, the same economy of attention. His cold-weather gear was the Antarctic issue: the red outer shell, the insulated gloves. The hat with the fraying left brim.

Then he looked at her.

He crossed the snow toward her. Behind him the others were coming down the ramp — four people she knew by name and face and the specific way each of them moved in cold-weather gear, a knowledge that was simply present the way such knowledge is, before any decision about what to do with it. Behind them the cargo was starting to come off the aircraft, the coordinated activity of the supply offload that ran on its own schedule.

He stopped in front of her. His breath was a cloud in the still cold air.

"How's the generator?" he said.

She told him: the voltage regulator fault had been active since Day 3. The power reserve was critical — she named the percentage, which was just above the minimum safe line. The new component he had brought in the equipment case should be installed as soon as the bay was at temperature. That was the first priority. She was accurate about this and complete.

He looked at her. The look was the one he gave an instrument reading that was real but had not yet resolved — not skeptical, not alarmed; simply attending to it. Then a voice from the ramp called for the cargo manifest and his attention divided and came back, and he said: "Come inside when the cargo's squared away." He moved toward the ramp.

---

The hab filled.

Five people, where there had been one for twelve days. The smell was the first thing: cold-weather gear that had spent twelve days in McMurdo's social infrastructure — the galley, the bunkhouse corridors, the particular composite of warmth and occupancy that accumulates in Antarctic kit stored indoors rather than at minus thirteen in an outbuilding. It released in the hab's warmth as they came through the corridor hatch. The smell was specific and it was everywhere in the room at once.

The noise was the other thing. Five people in a space built for six is not loud — field people modulate for small spaces; these were field people — but it occupied the hab's full acoustic register in a way that one person had not. She had been living inside a particular quality of silence for twelve days and had not tracked it as silence while it was happening. She tracked it now, in the first minutes of the hab filling, by the specific texture of what was replacing it.

They greeted her. Each of them, briefly. The practiced ease of people who had been in Antarctica before and knew that returning to a winterover station was its own kind of event and did not require more than what was offered. She answered each greeting by name. Someone had brought fresh bread from McMurdo — the foil crinkled as it was unwrapped; the smell reached the galley counter before the loaf did. Someone else had brought a large coffee thermos. There was the small ceremony of supply: the mugs from the shelf, the thermos passed around, the specific occasion of coffee that was not made on a camp-stove pot.

She stood near the counter and was present enough.

Walczak had set his kit bag beside his bunk and was doing his return check — not the cargo check the others were still running, but the station check that was his: the overhead conduit junction above the south wall, the equipment cabinet seals, the power indicator at the base of the main rack. She tracked this without watching him directly. He would finish in the hab and go to the equipment building. In the equipment building he would see the fault log — the six overnight excursions, the two long entries in the early morning hours, the cold-start variance column. He would see the power gauge on the equipment panel at just above the minimum safe line.

She had not left the protocol folder in its usual position. She had moved it, along with the rest of the evidence package, before the plane landed.

She refilled her mug and left the hab.

---

She had carried the evidence package to the equipment building work table in two trips: the tape housing and protocol folder first, then the wrapped printout roll and the analysis notebook. She set each item in the order she had established on the equipment cabinet's top shelf when she arranged them after Run 6 — the order in which she had been thinking about them for days — and stood on the far side of the table.

The power gauge on the equipment panel read just above the minimum safe line.

The fault log was on its clipboard above the generator panel. The fault column had entries she had not yet told him about. He would see them.

She stood on her side of the table and waited.

---

Walczak came through the hatch four minutes later.

He did the left-to-right read of the equipment building — instrument racks, fault-log clipboard, SSB console — and his eyes reached the work table and stopped.

He looked at what was on the table.

Then he looked at the power gauge. He held his attention on it long enough to read it accurately, long enough to confirm that the number she had told him on the strip was the number the needle was actually indicating. The needle at just above the line.

He looked at her.

She opened the analysis notebook. Not to the evidence summary. To the first entry — the heading, the date, the time, the initial log for Protocol Run 1, which was Day 1 at twenty-two forty-nine — and she placed it on the table in front of him. She said, in the voice she used for exactly this kind of communication — patient, precise, without emphasis — "I need you to read this."

Walczak sat down.

He set his right hand flat on the table beside the notebook, the way she had seen him settle in for a long instrument log. He looked at the top of the page.

He began to read.

The space heater in the corner cycled on with its usual sound. The overhead fluorescent held its steady light. Outside the single window the Antarctic afternoon was flat gray. The generator ran its note through the wall — the same note it had held for twelve days, the same note under every run, every fault log entry, every hour she had spent in this room — and it was holding it now, for both of them.

He read.
