# The Physical Record

She unrolled the printout on the galley table and weighted the corners — condensed milk tin, tool kit, field reference binder, protocol folder — then positioned the work light to fall across the paper's full width from the left. The thermal blanket went over the back of the chair. She took the laminated examination card from the protocol folder's side pocket and placed it to the left of the printout.

Fourteen columns. The examination card listed them in the order the signal analyzer produced them: frequency, amplitude, time of reception, phase, polarization, signal-to-noise, calibration reference, and seven derived parameter columns from the analyzer's processing. She had printed the card from the original template during the setup week in March, as she had done at the start of every winterover season in which this protocol had been active. She confirmed the column order against the printout's header row and began.

Column 1 was frequency. She ran the two-pass method: first pass down through all twenty-three data rows, checking legibility and completeness; second pass on the eight rows she had marked on the examination card as carrying the signal's peak values. Both passes returned the same result. Column 1 was intact.

Column 2 was amplitude. The same method, the same result. She wrote in the working pad: *Col 1, Col 2: intact.* The signal's frequency and amplitude values were there, all twenty-three rows, all eight flagged rows. She moved to column 3.

Column 3 was the timestamp column: time of reception in UTC, to the hundredth of a second, one entry per acquisition row. Without it, the printout was a document containing measured values with no independently verifiable record of when those values had been acquired. The timestamp was the printout's link to the antenna log in the equipment building's document cabinet, to the station's master clock, to the formal chain that allowed a reader who had not been present to verify that the data had been produced by this instrument, at this station, at the time and under the conditions the protocol required. The timestamp column did not record the signal. It recorded the claim that the recording was legitimate.

At row 4, she stopped.

The ink along the printed numerals showed a fracture pattern — hairline cracks running perpendicular to the direction of print. This was the failure mode thermal paper produced when a substrate contracted in sustained cold: the ink surface, slightly less flexible than the paper beneath it, fractured along its weakest lines rather than flexing. She had documented this pattern in the protocol's failure-mode appendix after seeing similar damage on printout returns from earlier Antarctic deployments, in conditions less extreme than Winslow in June. The row 4 timestamp was partially legible. The first two characters — the hour and minute digits — were intact. Three characters were interrupted by the fracture lines, their correct values inferrable from the progression of adjacent rows, the timestamps incrementing at a consistent interval. The sixth character was absent: the ink had separated from the substrate along the fracture line and taken that position with it.

*Col 3, row 4: partially degraded. Characters 3–5 fractured, recoverable by context. Character 6 absent.*

Row 5 and row 6 were clean. At row 7 the fracture pattern returned, broader. The cracking at row 7 ran across a wider portion of the cell, with the same perpendicular orientation but more extensive surface area — possibly because that row's position fell on a stress line in the paper, a structural differential the cold had amplified over nineteen days of the printout's storage in the equipment cabinet. The row 7 timestamp was legible in roughly half its characters. The remaining half were interrupted at positions where adjacent context did not uniquely constrain the correct digit; the timestamp sequence incremented from row to row in a way that bounded but did not determine what the fractured characters should be.

*Col 3, row 7: partially degraded. Approximately 50% of characters legible. Remaining characters not recoverable.*

At row 12, there was no ink.

She moved the work light to a low oblique angle — the method for detecting the reflectivity differential between intact ink surface and bare paper substrate — and looked at the row 12 position. The paper was structurally present and in its correct sequence location. What had been printed on its surface on the night of June first was not there. The thermal base material caught the oblique light cleanly: smooth, off-white, without printed characters or fracture residue or even the partial texture that remained when ink cracked but did not fully lift. The ink at row 12 of the timestamp column had separated from the substrate entirely and was gone.

*Col 3, row 12: blank. Ink absent.*

She confirmed rows 13 through 23 in the two-pass method and found them intact. Then she moved through columns 4, 5, and 6 — phase, polarization, signal-to-noise — in fourteen minutes and found no degradation in any of the sixty-nine cells she examined.

Column 7 was the calibration reference column. The signal analyzer recorded its calibration state at each acquisition interval: the internal reference oscillator reading, the measured noise floor, and three additional calibration parameters that together described the instrument's operating condition at the moment of measurement. These values did not measure the signal. They recorded the conditions under which the signal was measured — the reference frame against which the raw signal data was calibrated. A reader who wished to verify the accuracy of the measurements in the adjacent columns, rather than accepting the analyzer's own self-assessment of its output, needed column 7's reference values to calculate the calibration corrections the protocol specified. The calibration column was not optional verification; it was what made verification possible.

Row 2 of column 7: hairline fractures, the same pattern as column 3 row 4. She held the loupe over the damaged area and examined each character position in sequence. The reference oscillator value was legible, as was one of the three additional calibration parameters. The noise floor figure and the remaining two additional parameters were interrupted by the fracture lines at positions that were not recoverable from adjacent context.

*Col 7, row 2: partially degraded. Reference oscillator and one additional parameter legible. Noise floor and two additional parameters interrupted.*

Row 9: more extensive damage, and a different character. The fracturing ran diagonally as well as perpendicular to the print direction, indicating contraction stress along more than one axis — a pattern she associated with printouts that had experienced temperature cycling as well as sustained cold. Under the loupe the cracking showed a mosaic structure: separated tiles of ink surface, the gaps between them exposing the paper, the tiles themselves carrying fragments of characters that were no longer connected to one another. She examined the row for two minutes. The values were not recoverable.

*Col 7, row 9: extensively degraded. Multi-directional fracturing. Values not recoverable.*

Columns 8 through 14: she spent thirty-four minutes on these seven columns in the two-pass method, checking each row and the flagged rows for the signal's anomalous parameters. All intact. The frequency distribution across the signal's forty-seven-second duration, the amplitude profile, the phase structure, the polarization state, the signal-to-noise progression, and the seven derived parameter values from the analyzer's processing — legible in all twenty-three rows of all seven columns.

She put the pen down.

The core of what the printout showed was present. The damage was not in the signal's characteristics. It was in the columns that placed those characteristics in a verifiable chain: the timestamp connecting the printout to external records, the calibration reference certifying the measurement conditions under which the data had been produced. The printout's capacity to independently certify its own provenance — to stand alone as a document, without requiring access to the antenna log and the instrument log in the equipment building — was diminished. What remained was the signal's measured parameters, and the documentation that the measured parameters were damaged.

She transferred the working pad notes to the analysis notebook:

*Day 10. Printout examination. Column 3 (timestamp): rows 4, 7 partially degraded; row 12 blank. Column 7 (cal ref): rows 2, 9 degraded. Columns 1, 2, 4–6, 8–14: intact.*

She read the entry, confirmed it against the working pad, and set the pen in the notebook's spine.

---

The station camera was in the field documentation kit on the second shelf of the equipment building — a 35mm SLR, the standard field documentation model, used in March for the antenna alignment record and the receiver rack configuration photographs. She retrieved it from the kit, confirmed the frame counter through the small indicator window: eighteen exposures remaining on the current roll.

She carried the camera back to the galley and positioned it above the printout at sixty centimeters, the 50mm lens on manual focus at the standard documentation distance. The focus ring moved smoothly enough, but the aperture ring required firm pressure — the cold had stiffened the detents, the mechanism functioning correctly at reduced tolerance. She set the aperture, confirmed the exposure through the viewfinder, and began.

First pass: the full length of the printout in overlapping frames, left to right, covering each section with enough lateral overlap that no column fell entirely at a frame edge. She advanced the film between exposures with the winding lever. The lever had developed resistance since March — the lubricant thickened, the mechanism operating correctly but less fluidly than it had during the setup week, when the equipment building had been above minus fifteen and the machinery had moved with more willingness. She advanced each frame with steady, deliberate pressure, feeling the ratchet engage and complete before moving the camera.

Second pass: close range on the damaged columns. She adjusted the focus to forty centimeters and photographed column 3 in two frames — one covering the row 4 and row 7 positions in sequence, one placed on the blank space at row 12, where the oblique light made the absence most visible against the intact rows above and below. Then column 7 in two frames, both focused on the damaged rows.

She checked the counter: twelve exposures used, six remaining.

What the twelve frames on the roll documented was the printout as it existed this morning. When the roll was developed — McMurdo, after the team arrived on Day 12, the first opportunity — the photographs would show the intact columns and the damage in columns 3 and 7: the fracture patterns in the cells she had examined, the interrupted characters, the blank space at row 12 where the oblique light showed nothing but paper. The photographs would confirm that the damage existed and establish its approximate extent.

They would not supply the missing values. A photograph of an absent character showed the absence. A photograph of a fractured cell showed the fracture and the fragments that remained, not the value the fragments had composed before the ink separated. The photographs were a record of what the printout had become, not a record of what it had originally contained. Both were true; they were not equivalent. She had done the documentation available to her, which was what there was to do.

She rewound the roll and noted the camera's state on the working pad — *Roll F: 12 frames used, 6 remaining. No darkroom at Winslow. McMurdo on arrival.* — and returned the camera to the documentation kit.

---

The walkway to the receiver hut was thirty-one paces. The hut was minus fourteen-point-three when she opened the door, the space heater at its standard draw, the signal analyzer in standby with its amber numerals steady in the dim.

She was not going to run the protocol today.

The power reserve was approximately 22 percent by the morning gauge reading — the overnight passive draw had pulled it down two points from the previous evening's estimate. Each remaining instrument run required the fifteen-minute stabilization procedure, designed to hold the voltage variance inside the analyzer's safe operating range during the draw. At the current variance of plus or minus eight percent, the procedure cost three to four points of the reserve before the run began. Eight points for the run itself at baseline rate. Eleven to twelve total. Two runs remaining: one for Day 11, one for Day 12. The floor after both, if the variance held and no brownout added to the cost, would be somewhere between two and four percent. The range was not generous enough to commit one of the remaining runs on a day when conditions were not optimal.

This was not an optimal day. She was measuring.

She unclipped the tape housing from the rack and opened the cover, then positioned the flashlight at the standard first angle for the housing inspection.

The binder failure zone was visible at once — the band of lighter-colored surface where the magnetic binder had separated from the polyester substrate, the base film exposed in a strip against the darker oxide on each side. She had been measuring this zone's extent since the first confirmed dropout on Run 3, Day 4, using the timing marks printed on the housing's internal frame: two-second intervals, indexed to the reel's angular position. She located the failure zone's leading edge, measured its position relative to the nearest timing mark, and calculated the offset. Then she moved the flashlight to the second standard angle — the oblique position that sharpened the boundary between failed and intact oxide — and measured again.

Eighteen seconds.

Both measurements agreed within half a mark.

She held the housing at the work table for a moment. Then she opened the housing inspection card and wrote: *Day 10, visual est., 18s. Zone wider than Day 8. Leading edge approaching boundary of first segment core region.*

The progression she had been logging: five seconds confirmed on Run 3, Day 4. Eight seconds estimated by visual inspection on Days 6 and 7. Twelve seconds confirmed on Run 4, Day 8. Eighteen seconds now, estimated by the same visual method.

The intervals between successive measurements: three seconds in two days, then four seconds in two days, then six seconds in two days. The most recent interval was the largest yet. She used it as the working rate — three seconds per day — because it was the most recent data point and because underestimating the rate was the error she could not afford to make. At that rate, the failure zone's leading edge would reach the boundary of the first parametric segment's core signature region in approximately two to three days.

The core signature was intact. She had confirmed this on Run 4 and had not been able to confirm it since, because confirming it required a protocol run and she had budgeted the remaining runs for tomorrow and the day after. What she had today was a visual estimate and the arithmetic derived from it. The arithmetic said two to three days. Run 5 tomorrow would give her the instrument measurement.

She clipped the housing back in the rack and filed the inspection card.

---

She rewrapped the printout in the thermal blanket when she returned to the hab — both ends folded, the roll placed back in the equipment cabinet with the same careful grip she had used to carry it to the table that morning. She stood for a moment at the open cabinet, looking at the wrapped roll on the shelf. Then she closed the cabinet.

She transferred the Day 10 log entry from the working pad to the station log:

*Day 10. Printout: Col 3, Col 7 partial degradation (see examination record). Photographs: 12 frames, Roll F. Tape: visual dropout estimate 18s. No instrument run. Power reserve estimate 20%.*

She read the entry and confirmed each item. Then she closed the log.

She did not make tea. There was a period at this hour when a task block ended and the next one had not yet begun, and she usually made tea in that period. She left the kettle on the shelf. She went and sat in the chair beside the work table.

The hab held the quiet it had held for ten days: the heating circuit at its base draw, the generator's load-hum from the equipment building, the periodic tick of the corridor hatch contracting in the cold. The hatch seal was cracked at its lower corner — she had noted it in the first week — and the sound it made when the metal moved was a small, irregular interval she had stopped registering as a sound and had started registering as time passing.

The analysis notebook was closed in front of her on the table. She did not open it.

Across the room the SSB radio's standby light was red on the console, throwing its small disk of color on the wall. The disk sat at the angle it always held against that wall. The light was what it had been on the first night in this hab, the same red, the same position, and the physical record of the signal it did not know about was smaller tonight than it had been that night and smaller than it would be tomorrow night.

She sat with that and did not reach for the handset.
