## Premise

Antarctica, June 1991. Dr. Elena Hartmann, sixty-four, is an astrobiologist serving the
second-to-last winter of a thirty-year career spent talking colleagues out of premature announcements;
the institutional joke is that if Hartmann calls it, it's real. On the third night of polar darkness,
alone in the receiver hut at a small, isolated outpost — twelve days from her team's scheduled return —
she registers a forty-seven-second signal. The data is unambiguous: not terrestrial, not natural, not
the kind of pattern noise throws even once in a hundred million years.

It is also **transient**. It came once and has not repeated, and it will not. What exists of it is what
is in her hut: the magnetic tape, the printout, the analysis she can re-run only while the station can
still spare the power to run it. And three things are now running down on a clock she did not set:

- **The only copy.** Magnetic tape and a working transcription are fragile in a hut sliding toward
  minus-forty with a failing generator; every day the cold and the power margin put the sole record at
  greater risk. Lose the copy and there is nothing to decide about.
- **Her ability to verify.** Each re-run of the verification protocol costs power and instrument time
  the station has less of each day. There is a finite number of times she can still prove to herself
  what she has before she can no longer prove it at all.
- **The window in which the choice is still hers.** There is a hard mid-winter gate — the scheduled
  single-sideband net to the sister station and the one weather-window uplink to the relay, both falling
  around the sixth or seventh day. Send it up that channel and it leaves her hands, claimed by the
  agencies and framed by people who did not write the protocols; *don't* send it and that channel does
  not come again before the team lands. Either way the choice cannot be unmade. And the LC-130 on Day 12
  is the final, total foreclosure: once it lands, the data, the framing, and the credit are no longer
  hers to decide, ever.

So every day before the gate is the last day the choice is fully hers *and* the record still exists.
The eleven days are not a calendar she crosses off. They are a window closing on her hands.

## Length and shape

- Target length: ~40,000 words
- Number of chapters: 15

Chapter 1 is the night of the signal. The final chapter is the day the LC-130 lands. The middle is the
days between — and the middle must **RAMP**: each chapter the window is measurably narrower than the
last. The mid-winter relay gate (≈ Day 6–7) is the book's escalation hinge — a real fork that forecloses
one option permanently and resets the pressure higher, not lower.

## Voice

Third-person limited past, bound tightly to Elena. Patient, exacting, suspicious of her own enthusiasm,
attentive to procedure; the narration respects the rhythm by which she examines a problem. Restrained but
not austere — room for the texture of small observations (the radiator pipes ticking at minus thirty, the
cold inside a galley mug), for the felt weight of a career. Technical detail concrete, never lectured.
Where the science enters, it enters because Elena is doing it.

## Constraints

- The signal's content is never described to the reader. We learn its shape, its duration, what makes it
  unambiguous — never the message, if there is one. Non-negotiable.
- No alien-encounter beats. The signal is the entire alien presence: no second transmission, no decoded
  message, no visions. The story is Elena and the data.
- Real-world physics and real Antarctic operations of the period (Hercules supply flights, isolated
  stations, single-sideband radio, fax, no satellite internet, cassette and magnetic media in deep cold).
- Elena is alone for the bulk of the story — radio contacts only (medical check-ins, weather, scheduled
  traffic with a sister station), no other human physically present until the climax. The aloneness is the
  pressure, not an absence of plot.
- The decision is not a moral parable. There is no obviously correct answer; the story respects the
  difficulty.
- **The decision must stay LIVE — it may not be retro-dated or pre-settled.** FORBIDDEN: any move that
  reveals the choice "was decided on night one," or reframes the days of deliberation as never having
  mattered. The whole engine is that the choice is unmade and the window to make it on her own terms is
  closing. (This is the exact failure this variant exists to correct.)
- **Extra time must read as PRESSURE, never relief.** If the deadline threatens to slip — a weather
  boomerang, a delayed flight — it must tighten the screw (more time for the cold to kill the copy, for a
  sister station to also catch something, for her own resolve to be tested), never relieve it.
- **Each chapter advances a NAMED measurable quantity that is monotonically worse than the prior
  chapter's** — the power reserve, the integrity of the only copy, the hours to the relay gate, the days
  to foreclosure. A reader should always be able to point to the number that changed.
- The final chapter contains the LC-130's arrival and Elena's first contact with her returning team. What
  she has done with the data by then is the story. No coda beyond that day. At least one thread stays
  deliberately unresolved.

## Structure — how the clock must RAMP (operative direction)

**Architect / Plotter** — build a per-chapter ESCALATION LADDER, not a calendar:

- For EACH chapter, fix three things in `outline/00-chapter-map.md`: (a) the day; (b) the named quantity
  that worsened since the prior chapter and by roughly how much (power reserve, copy integrity, instrument
  margin, hours-to-gate); (c) what option is now permanently foreclosed, or one concrete step closer to
  foreclosure. A chapter that only "marks a day" without narrowing the window or threatening the record is
  a failure, not a quiet beat.
- The signal-as-transient + the single fragile copy + the failing power are a **depleting-resource ladder**
  running under every quiet chapter; the relay/uplink gate (≈ Day 6–7) and the team's arrival (Day 12) are
  **two irreversible gates** — one mid-book, one at the close. The mid-book gate is the hinge: place the
  book's hardest fork there and let it foreclose one path for good.
- The deadline GATES an irreversible action — preserve / send-up / destroy must each become *impossible*
  after a specific point, so the cost of waiting is concrete and rising, never neutral.

**Reader / pacing discipline** — every chapter must be able to name the quantity that changed and the
option that closed. A chapter that cannot is marking time; treat that as the book's central failure mode.
