# The Sale Contract

Booth had left the administrator's second parcel on the side of the desk — not quite at the centre and not at the edge, as he always placed things he considered likely to be unwelcome, as if the middle distance were the appropriate location for something he had no wish to be asked about. The brown paper was addressed to Prescott in Hadfield's careful hand, the knot tied in the practised way of a man who had been managing documents for thirty years and tied them the same way every time.

Prescott opened it after Booth had gone upstairs to the copying room. Four items: the mortgage instrument, the conditional sale contract, a bundle of banking correspondence bound in red tape, and the administrator's monthly account for March. He set them in order of origin — mortgage oldest, monthly account most recent — and began at the beginning.

---

The mortgage had been Garside's matter from the first. The instrument number at the head of the page was in his hand — the clean, deliberate numerals he had used for every instrument through this office since the year he established the register, each digit made the same way across three decades of files. The attestation clause below the covenants was set in the form Garside had preferred, the signature block indented a full measure from the covenant text and the witness clause arranged above it in a single clean sentence. Eight thousand five hundred pounds secured on the mill's plant and machinery and its goodwill, held by the County Bank in Blackburn at four and a half per cent, with the interest payable at each of the quarter-days in the year. The mortgage was made in the spring of 1921, when the mill had needed capital for plant renewal and Josiah had put up the security against the estate's credit rather than draw from his own reserves. The principal obligation was the estate's, not Walter's in his personal capacity, which was a distinction that mattered for the title but not for the question of who had depended on the estate's solvency to hold his position together.

Hadfield's February letter, already in the file, had confirmed that the most recent two quarter-days' interest had been met from the estate's liquid capital rather than from the mill's operating income. The mill's operating income had been insufficient to service the debt. The principal sat where it had sat since 1921: untouched, the whole sum outstanding, waiting on the sale. Under the conditional contract, the purchaser was taking the estate subject to the mortgage, with discharge from the proceeds of completion forming part of the closing transaction. If the sale completed.

---

The sale contract was a Manchester firm's work, commercial property men, and they had drafted it with the methodical confidence of solicitors who expected no difficulty with the condition they were inserting. Prescott read it through again from the recitals, which described the vendor's title by reference to a grant of representation that had not yet issued and that would not issue until the action was resolved. The condition was in clause twenty-four.

*Completion of this Agreement is in all respects conditional upon the vendor producing a grant of representation establishing his title to the said estate and hereditaments.*

Two memoranda of extension were clipped behind the contract: the first had been agreed in January, granting eight further weeks; the second was dated the fifteenth of March and granted eight more. A note from Thurston & Marsh to the purchaser's solicitors accompanied the second memorandum, advising that a further extension was not anticipated. The purchaser had been generous twice. Whatever patience had been available appeared to have been used.

Without the grant, the contract could not complete. Without the action being resolved — in one direction or the other — the grant could not issue. The administrator could collect the estate's income and pay its debts, but could not distribute or convey. The mill sat in this position as it sat in every other: the same short-time working, the same wage bill, the same mortgage interest being drawn from capital that was not being replenished. The purchaser's patience ran in one direction. The estate's capital ran in the other. The action ran between them.

---

The bundle of banking correspondence was nine letters from Walter Eccleston to his bank manager, Mr. Holt, running from the spring of 1924 through to January 1927. Five replies from Holt were tucked into the appropriate envelopes; four letters had no reply, or none that had been preserved. Prescott untied the red tape and set the letters in sequence.

The picture was consistent across three years — a record of how a man had explained his position to himself and to others under increasing pressure. The early letters explained. The codicil was a temporary arrangement, made in a soft mood by an old man who had spoken since of putting the matter right. The mill was carrying a mortgage, but mills carried mortgages; that was not peculiar to this mill. Walter's expectations were what they had always been, which was to say that he expected Josiah to do what Josiah had assured him he intended to do. The arrangement would not stand; it had not stood in any practical sense; the inheritance would come through as it had always been understood that it would.

Holt's replies grew shorter. By early 1926 he had begun asking questions about security. By the end of 1926 his replies were two lines where they had once been half a page, and they asked for answers Walter had already given once and could only give again. Walter gave them again, in longer versions of the same assurance: the estate would come through, the grant would follow, the bank should be patient.

Among the nine letters was one dated the eleventh of June 1925, written after Holt had put the security question to him directly. Prescott placed it on the desk.

---

*Dear Mr. Holt, — You ask what further security I can offer beyond the mortgage. My uncle has made a codicil, as I told you, in favour of my late cousin Mrs. Fairhurst and her issue, but I am assured the arrangement will not stand; he made it in a soft mood and has spoken since of putting it right. My expectations are otherwise what they have always been. — Yours faithfully, WALTER ECCLESTON*

---

He clipped it in sequence with the rest of the letters and opened Hadfield's March account.

---

The March figures were not an improvement on February's. Gross income from the mill was down sixteen pounds on the previous month; the wage bill was met; the March quarter-day on the mortgage interest had been paid from the estate's capital rather than its earnings, the same as the two quarter-days before it. The net loss against projected income was a little over three hundred pounds, and Hadfield's estimate of reserves — calculated on his February assumptions and not revised — showed approximately three months of liquidity remaining at the current rate of depletion. He noted this without recommendation. What happened after the estate's capital was exhausted was not a question he was authorised to answer from his position, and Prescott had no mechanism to answer it from his.

---

Prescott assembled the exhibits. The mortgage instrument first — the earliest document, the foundation of the financial position. The sale contract, with the two extension memoranda clipped behind it and the Thurston & Marsh note behind those. The nine letters from Walter to Holt, in date order, with Holt's five replies where they belonged. Hadfield's monthly account at the back, the most recent piece of the record.

He clipped the bundle and noted the reference on the cover sheet. One hundred and eighty-five days to the hearing; he had written the figure that morning, and it sat in the margin of his working notes where he had put it, the same way the earlier figures sat in earlier margins, each one smaller than the one before it.

Context was not proof. Motive explained the forgery; it did not establish that the forgery had been made, or by whom, or how. The proof was in the text of the second codicil and in the text it recited, and the argument that would be built from those two texts had not yet been assembled into the brief. It was the most work still remaining.

He stacked the financial exhibits and set the brief to one side.
