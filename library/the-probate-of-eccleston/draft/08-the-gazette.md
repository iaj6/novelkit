# The Gazette

Mr. Booth had the list on Prescott's desk by nine o'clock on Monday morning: forty-three firms in the county, alphabetical, addresses verified against the current Law Society directory. Two columns on the right — one for the date the letter went, one for the date of reply.

"I've left out the two Clitheroe firms," Booth said. "They're not in the main directory, but they practise. And Rawlinson and Peel have a Garstang office that acts for several valley estates."

"Add them."

"That brings it to forty-six."

Prescott had been working on the notice since the previous week. The difficulty was scope — narrow it too much and a firm might read itself out of the question on a technicality; broaden it too much and the replies would come back freighted with qualifications that required letters in return. The final text, as Booth read it back, was:

*Any solicitor who has acted for, or received instructions from, the late Josiah Eccleston of Eccleston Hall, Ribchester, in connection with any testamentary instrument, codicil, or revocation of any testamentary instrument, at any time after March 1924, is invited to communicate with Garside & Prescott, Solicitors, Winckley Square, Preston.*

Booth suggested removing the comma after the second *instrument*. Prescott agreed. The notice went to the Gazette offices by the afternoon post.

The circular letters went out on Thursday, the third of February: forty-six letters on the firm's headed paper, each signed by Prescott. He had added a last paragraph asking each firm to reply even if its answer was entirely negative, so that the record of the canvass would be complete on every name.

---

The first replies arrived the following week. Most were brief to the point of curtness: *We have no file for the name Eccleston and have received no instructions from any person of that name in connection with any testamentary matter.* Four in the first post; six on Thursday; five more by the end of the week. Booth ticked them off the list.

By the end of the second week there were eighteen replies. One of them required a note. A Lancaster office had a 1919 file — a conveyance on which they had acted for the other party, in a matter entirely unrelated to the estate and that would have been immaterial even if it had concerned Josiah Eccleston directly. Prescott wrote the explanation in the margin and set it with the irrelevants.

He was looking, throughout, for the exception: the firm that answered with care, saying it had a file and that the matter was not without complication, and that its senior partner would be glad to speak with Prescott at his convenience. None of them was the exception.

Booth managed the list from his own desk — ticking each name, noting the date, setting aside those that required a letter in reply. He did not need to be told what to do with any of them. By the third week, thirty-one had replied; the rate of replies eased. Two in a post, then one, then a gap of a day.

---

By mid-February, thirty-one firms had replied and fifteen had not.

Two hundred and thirty-five days to the hearing.

The remaining fifteen would answer in their own time.

---

The Gazette produced nothing.

By the time the last circular reply had come in, the advertisement had been running for three weeks and no response had appeared in its column. Prescott had not expected one. A solicitor who had taken testamentary instructions from a man of Josiah Eccleston's standing, and had subsequently seen his name in the circular letter — the address, the dates, the explicit request for a reply even if negative — would not have waited for the Gazette to prompt him. He would have answered the letter. Prescott made a note of the date and the absence and placed it in the file.

---

The last expected reply arrived on the twenty-fourth of February.

A Blackburn firm Prescott did not know by name — not in any county matter he could place, not in the principal directories he consulted — confirmed in two sentences that they held no file for Josiah Eccleston and had received no instructions from him in connection with any testamentary question. The letter was in the proper form.

He took Booth's list from the desk. Forty-six names; forty-six ticks in the reply column.

"That's the last," he said.

Booth looked across from his side of the room. "I held back the second Clitheroe firm for two or three days," he said. "Their reply came in on Friday. I thought you should see the Blackburn letter first, in case there was anything in it."

The second Clitheroe firm's reply was on Booth's desk — two sentences, no file, no instructions — and Prescott read it and set it with the others.

He pulled the file toward him and began to write.

---

*The London Gazette advertisement, placed the week of the 3rd February 1927, produced no response of any kind. Circular letters were addressed to every firm of solicitors known to practise in the county of Lancaster — forty-six firms in all, including three valley offices not listed in the principal directory — and every firm has now replied. The substance of the replies, taken together, is as follows: no firm in the county was instructed by the late Mr. Josiah Eccleston, or by any person acting or purporting to act on his behalf, to draw any testamentary instrument, codicil, or revocation of any testamentary instrument at any time after the execution of the first codicil dated the 15th March 1924. No firm received any communication from Mr. Eccleston touching his testamentary affairs after that date. The canvass is complete; the result is negative in every case.*

He dated it the twenty-fourth of February, 1927, and placed it at the front of the file.

---

Forty-six firms, and every one of them kept its files. Most of them kept their files to the level that the matter required; some kept them to a level somewhat above it — the standard Prescott had grown up in, which supposed that you could not always know, at the time you were building the record, exactly what the record would later be required to show. On the question of Josiah Eccleston's testamentary affairs after March 1924, all forty-six were agreed: nothing. No instruction received, no instrument drawn, no file opened.

The cover story required a solicitor who had taken instructions for a codicil in the summer of 1926, drawn the instrument, overseen its execution, and subsequently vanished from the professional record of the county without a file entry, a letter-book carbon, a bill rendered or paid, or a reply to the circular letter when the whole county had been invited to write. There was no such solicitor.

The profession had not done this. The door was locked.
