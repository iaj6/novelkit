# The File

The papers from the Hall came by registered post on the twenty-second, packed in a cardboard box with the administrator's covering note on top: one pocket diary, 1926; one bundle of correspondence, inward and outward, January to October; one set of household accounts. Prescott had not requested the accounts.

It was two hundred and fifty-seven days to the hearing.

He lifted the diary out first. Small, leather-covered, a brass clasp stiff from disuse — the kind sold in any stationer's, a shilling and sixpence, renewed each year without much deliberation. Josiah Eccleston's name was written inside the front cover in a hand that was still his own but smaller, and somewhat less firm, than the hand in the instruments in the strong-room.

He opened it to the first of January and began.

The entries were sparse from the start. January: a mill appointment on the eighth — the subject noted (looms on short time; questions about the dye-house roof), Walter's name not in it. February: another mill appointment; a doctor's visit on the nineteenth — Dr. Cullis, two words only, suggesting something routine; and a letter acknowledged from a cousin in Harrogate. March: a note from the magistrates' clerk, one of Josiah's cases forwarded to a colleague in Clitheroe. The entry had the air of a letter that had not been unexpected, and had not been unwelcome.

The mill appointments grew fewer in spring. The last was on the fourteenth of May: *W.E. — mill — morning.* Four words. After that, nothing about the mill.

The entries became sparser and more domestic. Beckett here and there — the steward, the same name as in the strong-room register. A note about the south field. The insurance broker.

Prescott turned to August.

The fourteenth was a Saturday. The entry read: *Hot again. Beckett here this afternoon — the south field question — resolved.* And below, in a slightly smaller hand: *Neck troubling me this morning.*

That was the last entry. After it: blank pages, clean, through September and October and the remainder of the year.

Prescott read the entry a second time. It was the record of an ordinary day: no appointment beyond Beckett, no instrument made or directed, no reference to any testamentary act. A diary did not prove a negative. But it was a record, and the record said nothing.

---

He set the diary aside and lifted the correspondence bundle.

The administrator had divided it — formal on the left, household on the right. Prescott worked through the formal side first. Three letters to and from Garside & Prescott, all already in the firm's letter-book and accounted for there. A letter from a Ribchester solicitor on a tenancy matter. Two from the estate's insurance brokers. Nothing to or from Thurston & Marsh; nothing from any other firm on any question touching the will or the codicil; nothing that had not already been known from the letter-book side.

The household side he read more quickly. Family correspondence; replies to local matters; the magistrates' clerk again. He came, near the bottom of the right-hand bundle, to a letter in Josiah's hand — but a different hand from the instruments: softer, unguarded, the hand of a man writing to someone he was glad to write to. Addressed to Miss Nora Fairhurst, Bank Parade, Preston. Dated the third of April, 1926.

He read it carefully. Not long — three-quarters of a page, a correction in the second paragraph, a postscript about a book he had promised to send. It said nothing about the estate's disposition, nothing about instruments or codicils. But near the end Josiah had written:

*I am very glad we have the matter settled between us, and I mean it to stay settled. Your mother knew it. You know it. That is what I wished.*

He made a note of the letter and set it to one side for the exhibits bundle.

---

He had read the instrument register in full in December, and knew what it contained. He took it from the strong-room now for the same reason he had written the letter-book note the previous week: to fix the finding as a record item. He ran his eye down the Eccleston column from 1884 to the end.

The original lease of Vale Mill, 1884. A mortgage, 1889, discharged 1892. The deed of gift of the Ribchester farmland, 1903. The lease extension of 1908. The will, third of June 1919. And, in Garside's invariable hand, the last entry in the column: *First Codicil — J. Eccleston dec. — 15 March 1924.*

Nothing after it. Forty years of instruments for one estate, and then a line. He closed the register and returned it to the strong-room.

---

When he came back to the table, there was one more item in the bundle — flat at the bottom, below the register extracts and the attendance notes, easy to miss. A letter in Garside's hand, on plain notepaper rather than the firm's standard paper. Dated the fourteenth of March, 1924.

He sat down and read it.

Three sentences. The codicil was enclosed, properly engrossed and ready for execution; Mr. Eccleston would need to sign it in the presence of two witnesses, both present together at the same time, neither of whom took any benefit under the instrument; Garside would attend at the Hall if preferred, or if Mr. Eccleston wished to arrange the witnesses himself, the office would be glad to receive the executed original at his convenience.

Three sentences, and each one exact. The witness requirement stated in full for a man who had been signing instruments since 1884 and had not required instruction in forty years. The note about the executed original — not a request but an offer, *the office would be glad to receive it*, as if Garside had thought about the document's custody before the letter left his desk.

It had been written for the record. Not for Josiah.

Prescott held it a moment, then set it with the exhibits.

The whole file had that quality. Each letter a fraction more precise than the matter strictly required; each attendance note kept at the same level whether the occasion was routine or not; the register built to a standard that had no source in any statute or practice requirement, but had its source entirely in the man who had built it. The file was the work of a man who had never distinguished between what required a record and what deserved one.

And the file was going to prove a forgery.

---

He wrote the working note in the hand he used for formal file items, and read it back once before he dated it.

*No private record or correspondence contains any reference to any intention to revoke or alter the first codicil after its execution in March 1924. No instrument in the firm's register was made in the name of Josiah Eccleston after that date. The survey of instruments is complete: every instrument made by Josiah Eccleston for a period of forty years was made through the firm of Garside & Prescott, and the last was the first codicil.*

He dated it the twenty-seventh of January, 1927, and placed it at the front of the working file.

---

The strong-room smelled as it always did: clean paper, iron shelves, the dry compound of ink aged past sharpness into something denser and quieter. He set the register in its place, checked the order of the cabinet, and closed the door. The lock turned with its usual certainty.

He turned off the lamp and went out into Winckley Square, and home.
