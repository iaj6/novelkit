# The Hall

The track to Eccleston Hall branched from the Ribchester road above the mill, taking the hillside at a steeper gradient so that the Hall gate stood at an elevation where the mill below was foreshortened and the valley opened wide to the west. Prescott hired a motor at Ribchester station — the second week of June, clear weather, the Ribble low and clean below — and the driver brought him up a raked gravel approach that curved before the south front of the house.

He stepped down and looked at it.

Two storeys, late Georgian stone, good proportions: not a great estate, but a house built with some idea of its own character. The grounds were maintained — lawns cut, kitchen garden occupied — but with fewer hands than the place had evidently required; the coach-house stood open and empty, and there was one figure visible in the walled garden and no one else. The administrator pendente lite met the minimum domestic expenses, and the minimum showed. Josiah had managed the Hall and the mill below it for sixty years and had died in its east bedroom in October; now the estate sat without instructions, and the house was kept until someone told it what to do next.

Elias Beckett met him at the front door and took him through to the library.

Beckett had been steward at the Hall for fourteen years. He was in his late fifties, deliberate in manner, and he arrived in the library with a clothbound household journal set before him on the table and already open. Prescott had brought a clerk from the Preston office; the deposition was to be taken formally, sworn, and filed.

"From about the middle of May last year," Prescott said, once the clerk had recorded the preliminary matter, "was Mr. Eccleston in the habit of going out?"

"No." Beckett placed one hand on the open diary without turning the page. "His last outing was the fourteenth of May. He went to the mill — Simms took him in the motor, as he always did — and he came home by four. I have it here." He turned the journal and showed the entry.

*Mr E. to the mill, with Simms. Home by four.*

It was the last such entry for many pages. What followed was domestic — deliveries, the doctor's visits beginning in June, household receipts.

"The doctor's visits increased from June?"

"Weekly by June. Twice a week by August."

"In your judgment," Prescott said, "could Mr. Eccleston have made a journey to the mill in August? Twelve miles?"

Beckett did not answer at once. "He could not," he said. "He could not have done it without Simms and the motor, and I should have known — nothing leaves this house without my being aware of it. In any case, by August the journey itself was beyond him. He had his wits, right to the end — I would not have you think otherwise — but the road to the mill and back would not have been possible for him. Not in August."

"You would have known if any such journey had taken place?"

"Nothing went out in August," Beckett said.

Prescott asked next about the September visit.

"The nineteenth," Beckett said, and turned without searching to the correct page of the journal. "Mr. Garside came, and another gentleman from Preston — yourself, I take it. Half past ten. They went into the drawing room with Mr. Eccleston and the family. The deed-box; the papers inside; about an hour. They left before midday. Mr. Eccleston was in the drawing room the whole time. He could not have left the house that day without my knowledge."

He showed Prescott the page.

*Sep. 19 — Mr. Garside and another from Preston, half past ten. Drawing room. Instruments. Family. Gone by noon.*

Prescott read it and returned the journal.

The strong-room register, in Garside's hand, carried the same event on the same date: the first codicil fetched, read entire in the presence of the testator and the assembled family, returned the same afternoon. The Hall's household diary and the strong-room register in Preston confirmed the same hour from opposite ends of twelve miles — one record kept in a solicitor's basement, one in a household journal on the valley road. Garside had noted the September visit with more precision than a courtesy call required.

The remaining questions were thorough and took some time. Between May and October: had Mr. Eccleston ever left the Hall unaccompanied; had the motor ever gone out for his use without Beckett's knowledge; had any visitor arrived who had removed him from the premises. The answers were consistent and did not require repeating. Beckett signed the deposition and the clerk witnessed it.

Mrs. Hargreaves, the cook — nine years at the Hall — was brought in next. She had been on the premises throughout August and had left it only twice, on half-days she could specify. Mr. Eccleston had not gone out; she was certain of it; nothing of that kind had happened. She signed where the clerk indicated and returned to the kitchen without further remark. Ellen Bradshaw, the housemaid, two years in her position, required less time still: Mr. Eccleston had not left the house in August; she had no uncertainty on the point.

Prescott took the afternoon train back to Preston with the three depositions in the briefcase.

Dr. Hartley's response was waiting, forwarded that morning by the administrator: certified extracts from the medical notes for August 1926, covering four visits to the Hall, the fifth of the month through the twenty-seventh. Shortness of breath on exertion, restricted movement, prescription adjusted twice. No outing or journey mentioned at any visit. The doctor had added a line in his own hand:

---

*In my professional opinion, Mr. Josiah Eccleston was not capable of making a journey of twelve miles in August 1926. Such a journey would have been medically inadvisable in the strongest terms. I am satisfied on the evidence of my own clinical observations that he did not make one.*

---

Prescott placed the certified notes and the doctor's letter behind the three depositions and sat down with a sheet of paper.

---

*Door (b) — Josiah's presence at the mill on the date of the second codicil — CLOSED.*

*Depositions from Mr. E. Beckett (steward, fourteen years' service), Mrs. E. Hargreaves (cook, nine years), and Miss E. Bradshaw (housemaid, two years), all of Eccleston Hall, Ribchester, each sworn before a commissioned clerk; corroborated by certified medical notes of Dr. T. Hartley, M.B., of Ribchester: the late Mr. Josiah Eccleston did not leave Eccleston Hall for any external destination after 14 May 1926. He was physically incapable of travel to Vale Mill, Ribchester — approximately twelve miles from the Hall — from June 1926 onward. The second codicil is dated 14 August 1926. On that date Mr. Eccleston was confined to the Hall, in failing health, and the journey was not possible for him. The second codicil cannot represent a visit by the testator to the mill office on the date it bears.*

---

He made one amendment — *in failing health, not capable of such a journey* to *in failing health, and the journey was not possible for him* — and initialled it.

One hundred and twenty-five days.
