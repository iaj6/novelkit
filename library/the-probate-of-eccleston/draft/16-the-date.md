# The Date

The hearing brief had been growing for three days. Mr. Booth had laid the exhibits in numbered order on the long table in the outer office — each document confirmed against the schedule, the index number pencilled in the upper right corner, the relevance notation entered in the right-hand column. Prescott had been working through them in sequence, transferring the verified bundles to the briefcase that would travel to London at the end of the month. It was the second week of July; the square outside was light until nine, and the office without its winter coal fire felt opened out, the iron grate cleaned and cold.

He had accounted for twenty-two exhibits. The twenty-third was the firm's notice letter of the second of November 1926 to Thurston & Marsh of Church Street.

He laid it on the desk and read it as a document rather than as something he had written.

The letter gave notice of the will and of the first codicil, the codicil described as *dated the 15th March 1924 which materially affects the disposition of the residue.* Those were the words: a date, and a bare statement of material effect. No text of the codicil; no identification of the beneficiary; no operative language. It was what solicitors wrote to other solicitors at the outset of an estate matter, when what was required was notice rather than disclosure.

The letter-book carbon confirmed the transmission. The copy was in its correct position in the letter-book, dated and addressed, sequenced between the first of November and the fourth. Garside had established the letter-book system the year Prescott had come into the office as an articled clerk, and nothing about it had varied since. The letter of the second of November had been sent; its contents had reached Thurston & Marsh on or about the third; from shortly after that date the date of the first codicil — *the 15th March 1924* — had been in their client's hands in writing.

Before the second of November, what had been available?

The September reading. On the nineteenth of September 1926, Garside had fetched the first codicil from the strong-room and driven to Eccleston Hall; the strong-room register recorded the fetch and the same-day return in Garside's hand. In the drawing room at the Hall, Garside had read the instrument entire to the assembled family: the gift, the date, the form of words, the revocation of clause 4. Prescott had been in the room, carrying the deed-box. Walter Eccleston had been in the room. The date of the first codicil had been spoken in that room: *the fifteenth of March 1924.* Walter had heard it once, in September, amid a great deal else.

Five weeks later, in the days before R5 arrived — in the days the despatch-box stood at the mill — memory of a single occasion was the only source. After the second of November, memory was supplemented by writing.

Prescott drew the second codicil from exhibit envelope two and read the recital.

---

WHEREAS by a codicil to my said will dated the fifteenth day of March 1924 I gave the residue of my estate unto my niece Margaret Fairhurst and her issue absolutely NOW I HEREBY REVOKE the said codicil in its entirety and I confirm my said will dated the third day of June 1919 in all respects as if the said codicil had not been made.

---

The date in the recital was *the fifteenth day of March 1924.* The date in the firm's notice letter was *the 15th March 1924.* The same figures in two conventions: the abbreviated form a notice letter would use, and the written-out style a drafter would set into the recital of an executed instrument. The correspondence was exact.

The operative words in the recital were *unto my niece Margaret Fairhurst and her issue absolutely.*

The firm's notice letter had not given operative words. It had given the date and the bare fact of material effect. The letter-book record showed that no text of the operative clause of the first codicil had been transmitted from this firm in writing; the profession canvass of the previous August had established that no solicitor in the county had been asked to advise on the codicil's terms. If the operative words in the recital had reached the drafter from any written source, that source was not in evidence and was not in the record.

The operative words of the executed first codicil were *unto my niece Margaret Fairhurst of Bank Parade Preston or her issue absolutely.* The recital's version — *and her issue* — was not those words. It was the version that appeared in exhibits already filed: in Walter Eccleston's letter to his bank manager on the eleventh of June 1925, in Walter's own hand, fourteen months before the codicil's stated date; and in the attendance note recording Nora Fairhurst's account of what Walter had told the children in early May. Three instances of the same version across three years. The version was consistent; it was Walter's version.

Prescott set the notice letter and the second codicil side by side and took up a fresh sheet.

The working note occupied two pages.

---

*Working note — exhibit 23 (R5, notice letter, 2 Nov. 1926) — 16 July 1927*

*The firm's notice letter to Thurston & Marsh of 2 November 1926 (R5, exhibit 23) gave the date of the first codicil as "the 15th March 1924." It gave no operative text of the codicil. The letter-book carbon (exhibit 7) confirms transmission on 2 November 1926. The date of the first codicil was not in writing from this firm or from any other established source before that date.*

*The recital of the second codicil (R3, exhibit 2) recites the first codicil as "dated the fifteenth day of March 1924" and as giving the residue "unto my niece Margaret Fairhurst and her issue absolutely." The date in the recital ("the fifteenth day of March 1924") corresponds exactly to the date in R5. The operative words in the recital ("and her issue") do not correspond to the operative words of the executed first codicil ("or her issue" — R2, exhibit 1). They correspond to the version documented in the hand of Walter Eccleston as at 11 June 1925 (R4, exhibit 14) and in the attendance note of 13 May 1927 (exhibit 21).*

*The recital reproduces the element of the first codicil that was supplied in writing by this firm's correspondence (the date). It does not reproduce the element that was not so supplied (the operative text); it reproduces instead a version of the operative words documented as Walter Eccleston's own, derived from a spoken account and not from the executed instrument.*

*Taken alone, this does not establish authorship. Combined with the foreclosures established by exhibits 1–22 — the machine, the presence, the witnesses, the profession, the firm's own record, the habit, the despatch-box, the September reading — it establishes that the recital was composed by a person who had the date from R5, had the operative words from memory of a spoken account, and had no access to the executed first codicil at any point.*

*A.P., 16.vii.27*

---

He filed the working note behind exhibit twenty-three. He returned the second codicil to its envelope and set it back in its position. Twenty-four was the extract from the strong-room register.

Ninety days to the hearing. The asymmetry was in the record: the date had been in writing; the text had not.
