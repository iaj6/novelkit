# Chapter 3 — The Habit

The Eccleston index ran to three pages.

Prescott spread them flat on the table beside the window. Pale November light lay on the paper from the square. The list was in Garside's hand from the first entry to the last: a matter number at the left margin, a brief description in the centre column, a date at the right. It had begun in 1884. He set his pen down and read from the beginning.

The first entry: *Eccleston, J. — conv. of mill premises, Ribchester, March 1884.* Below it, in close sequence, a second conveyance, then a deed of covenant over the mill machinery, then a first will — made in 1891, superseded. Garside's hand had been somewhat looser in those early years; by 1900 the columns were fully regular and the descriptions exact. The entries continued without break or interruption for forty years. They ended with a codicil executed in March 1924.

Prescott pulled the older cabinets first.

The conveyance of 1884 was in its original envelope, folded square, on rag paper without tear or stain. A young man buying his first mill premises had come to a solicitor recently qualified, and both men had since become, in their respective trades, what they were. The purchase of Eccleston Hall in 1898 occupied two drawers of deeds and a correspondence packet. The mortgage of 1921 — Blackburn bank — carried a note in Garside's hand on the inside cover of the instrument: *Prepared on client's instructions, 17 March 1921. Terms agreed with lender.*

Below the mortgage, the Hall title deeds ran to three separate bundles, each tied and labelled, all correctly lodged.

He found the earlier wills in the bottom drawer. Two, both superseded: 1891 and 1904. Each had been drawn by Garside & Prescott, executed on the firm's premises, held in the strong-room until replaced. The 1919 will — the standing instrument, now in the strong-room — had replaced the second of these. The will of 1904 bore the same Winckley Square address as the present firm; the will of 1891 bore Garside's name alone.

Prescott turned to the correspondence.

Josiah's letters to the firm ran to two packets, sorted by year. They were brief and direct, written in a plain hand that had grown slightly uneven in the final years — the hand of a man who had not found correspondence convenient and had nonetheless kept it orderly. He wrote when Garside wrote to him; he confirmed, instructed, accepted. His replies ran to a paragraph or two: the matter agreed, the instruction given, the arrangement understood. In forty years of correspondence there was not a single letter that did not have a legal purpose.

Prescott read through the letters of 1924 and 1925. Then the letters of 1926, which ran only to June. In none of them, from the day the first codicil was executed in March 1924 to the last letter Josiah had written before his illness, was there any mention of a changed mind. No dissatisfaction with the arrangement for Mrs. Fairhurst's children. No instruction — or shadow of an instruction — to reconsider what had been settled in that March.

The attendance note for the fifteenth of March 1924 was in the file behind the first codicil copy.

---

> 15 March 1924. Instructions from Mr. Josiah Eccleston at the office. Client has renewed relations with his niece Mrs. Margaret Fairhurst of Bank Parade Preston and desires to revoke clause 4 of his will of the 3rd June 1919. Codicil revokes clause 4 entire and gives the residue to Mrs. Margaret Fairhurst or her issue absolutely; all other provisions confirmed. Settled, executed, and attested in due form. Instrument to strong-room. *Or her issue*: collateral; substitutional form required. — H.G.

---

Prescott made a note on the working file cover — *corroborating exhibit: attend. note 15 March 1924, H.G.* — and set the original back.

Two pages further in the packet was a letter Josiah had written in late April 1924, six weeks after the codicil was made. It thanked Garside for his attendance in March and said, briefly, that the arrangement had been the right thing to do. Three lines on the matter and nothing equivocal. Garside had filed it inside a small folded card rather than flat in the packet as the rest lay — not the ordinary practice; the card kept the letter clean and flat. Prescott read it and set it face-down.

The file was well kept.

He was writing the finding in the working note when Garside came through the room.

"What have you found?"

"Nothing outside the firm," Prescott said. "Every instrument from the first conveyance in 1884. Two superseded wills, both drawn here. No exception in forty years. His correspondence from March 1924 to June 1926 contains nothing to suggest any wish to change the first codicil."

Garside had his coat and was going somewhere. He paused in the doorway.

"Josiah was not a man who did things unofficially," he said. He moved on.

Prescott wrote it in the working note. He dated the note, set it at the front of the file, and returned the Eccleston papers to their drawers as the afternoon light failed at the windows.

The index lay at the top of the file, three pages in Garside's hand from 1884 to 1924, the last entry in the same hand as the first.

There was nothing in forty years of this client's business to suggest that he had ever made a legal instrument outside this firm, or without a solicitor present to see it made.

The working note said that. The file would say it. Prescott set them together on Mr. Booth's tray and went to compose the afternoon post.
