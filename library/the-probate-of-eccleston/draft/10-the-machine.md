# The Machine

The post arrived at twenty minutes to ten. Mr. Booth set it on the desk with the usual arrangement — circulars to the left, correspondence to the right — and then stood a moment longer than was necessary, which was his means of indicating that something in it required attention before anything else did.

"Manchester," he said. "By this morning's first."

Prescott looked at the envelope. His own name on the outside, the second line Garside & Prescott, then Winckley Square, and above the return address, printed in small capitals: *A. J. Croft, Document Examiner, Manchester.* He had engaged Croft in the last week of January, on the recommendation of a London firm that had used him twice; the comparison specimens — from the mill-office machine, from every solicitors' office the canvass had identified, and the necessary confirmation that the Hall contained no machine — had been arranged with Mr. Hadfield's assistance in the first fortnight of March.

Mr. Booth withdrew.

Prescott cut the envelope. The report ran to eleven pages. He had not expected eleven pages; he read from the first line regardless.

Croft identified himself for the record: practising as a document examiner since 1912, first trained in London, thereafter in Manchester. The examination covered: the propounded second codicil, marked as Exhibit B and provided by Thurston & Marsh; comparison specimens taken from the typewriting machine at Vale Mill office, Ribchester, on the twelfth of March 1927, by arrangement with Mr. Hadfield, administrator of the estate; comparison specimens from the typewriting machines at eight solicitors' offices in the county, supplied through the canvass and by separate arrangement; and a written confirmation from Mr. Hadfield, supported by a household statement, that no typewriting machine was present at or had been kept at Eccleston Hall.

Prescott made a mark beside the Hall confirmation. It was not a disputed point, but having it formally included placed it in the examiner's report rather than leaving it as an absence from the record — which was a different thing, evidentially.

Croft's method was set out in two stages. First, typeface design: the identification of the machine by the characteristics of its typeface, which varied by manufacturer and by series. Second, individual machine characteristics: the particular defects and displacements acquired by a specific machine over its working life, distinguishable from those of any other machine of the same manufacture.

On typeface design, the report was brief and precise. The typewriting of the second codicil was produced in the face of the Underwood Standard series — a face distinguishable, by the design of its descenders and the relative weight of its serifs, from the Remington, the Royal, and the L. C. Smith specimens in the comparison set, and distinct also from the two Yost machines identified among the solicitors' office specimens. The mill-office machine was an Underwood Standard. None of the comparison specimens from solicitors' offices was produced on an Underwood Standard. On the question of manufacture and series, the second codicil was consistent with the mill-office machine and with no other machine examined.

Prescott read that paragraph twice. What it said was what he had known in the mill-office doorway, looking at the machine from a distance of eighteen inches with his pocket-book in his hand; but knowing was not demonstrating, and demonstrating was the eleven pages.

The individual machine characteristics occupied six of the remaining pages. Croft had identified four concurrent defects in the typewriting of the second codicil, each of which appeared consistently in the mill-office comparison specimens and none of which appeared in the specimens from any other machine:

First: the lower-case *e* slug was displaced upward by a fraction, producing in the codicil's typewriting — and in the mill-office specimens without exception — a raised *e* perceptible in every line. Second: the full-stop key bore wear across the entirety of its face, the left impression lighter than the right, the pattern consistent across both sets of specimens. Third: the lower-case *d* had lost the upper terminus of its left-hand serif, leaving a squared end in place of a tapered one, visible under magnification in the codicil and reproduced in identical form in the mill specimens. Fourth: the lower-case *r* was displaced fractionally upward and to the left of its true base-line position, producing a letter whose right foot did not align with the surrounding characters — again, consistent between the codicil and the mill specimens, and absent in every other machine examined.

No individual defect appeared in any specimen from any solicitors' office. The four defects appeared collectively and individually in the second codicil and in the mill-office specimens only.

Croft's conclusion was set out on a separate page:

---

*It is my conclusion, reached from the foregoing examination and based upon both typeface design and individual machine characteristics, that the typewriting of the propounded second codicil was produced upon the Underwood Standard typewriting machine located in the office of Vale Mill, Ribchester. I was unable to identify any other machine examined as the source of the typewriting. The examination of typewriting specimens does not establish the identity of the operator or the date of production: those are matters beyond my evidence.*

---

Prescott read the last two sentences a second time, then set the report flat on the desk.

They were correctly stated. An examiner who claimed more than his evidence supported was an examiner whose cross-examination counsel would enjoy. The limits were as useful as the finding: they told Prescott where the examiner's evidence ended and where the record's other elements would need to carry the weight.

The weight they would carry was already in the file, or nearly so. Josiah's 1926 diary ran to the fourteenth of August — hot weather, the steward, a note about his neck — and was blank thereafter. Naylor the foreman's daybook gave the fourteenth of May as the last occasion Josiah had set foot in the building where the machine stood. What Croft could not say — when, and by whom — the depositions from the Hall would address: the household staff who had accompanied Josiah on his journeys in his last months, and who would testify that after May he had not left the Hall unescorted and had not been brought to the mill. Those depositions were being arranged.

Walter had told him, at the meeting in November, that Josiah must have typed the codicil himself — or had it typed — on one of his visits to the mill, then laid it in the despatch-box without telling anyone. The version required a visit; there had been no visit after May. The version required a machine; there was one machine, and it had stood in a room Josiah had not entered in the three months before the codicil's date. Croft could say the machine was the mill-office Underwood Standard and that no other machine was its source; the record already said the rest, and the depositions would close it.

The window gave onto the square. A solicitor's clerk crossed the flagstones below, folder in hand, walking with the specific pace of a man carrying something he had not read. Thurston & Marsh were not visible from this room; they faced the other way along the square, toward the garden in the centre, which at this time of year was bare. Prescott was not especially sorry that their windows pointed away from his.

He turned back to the desk and lifted the report again.

Croft had appended two supplementary pages on the paper. The stock used for the second codicil was a plain rag-content writing paper of medium weight — the kind kept for general office correspondence, unremarkable in quality. It was not consistent with the heavier, superior-quality engrossment paper standard in a solicitors' firm for the preparation of testamentary instruments; a sample of Garside & Prescott's own engrossment stock was appended for comparison. The papers were of different character and weight. The paper used for the second codicil was consistent with the class of plain writing paper maintained in mill and counting-house offices for routine correspondence, and with the specific stock observed in the Vale Mill office. Croft's supplementary note was as careful on its limits as the main report had been: consistent with, but not independently proving.

Prescott set the report aside and took a fresh sheet.

The note he wrote was eleven pages reduced to three sentences; that was the work.

---

*The second codicil (exhibited as B in this action) was typed upon the Underwood Standard typewriting machine at Vale Mill office, Ribchester. This finding is made by Mr. A. J. Croft, document examiner, Manchester, on the basis of typeface design and four individual machine characteristics concurrently identified in both the codicil's typewriting and in comparison specimens from the mill-office machine; no other machine examined produces a corresponding match. No typewriting machine was present at Eccleston Hall. Croft's report and supplementary paper note are filed at this page.*

---

He dated the note. In the left margin, in the smaller hand he used for working figures, he wrote: *200 days.*

The door was shut.
