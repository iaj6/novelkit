# The Ninth Word

The last of the evening light had gone from the square by half past eight, and the gas lamps had come on. Mr. Booth had been home since six. The coal fire was out — it had not been lit since March — and Prescott had opened the window against the July heat; the sounds of the square came in through it, the cobbles and the pigeons settling, and the faint warm smell of a summer evening in a northern town.

He had the brief spread across the desk in three sections: the narrative, the exhibit schedule, and the amended affidavit of due execution still wanting Mitchell's countersignature from London. Counsel would have everything by the first of August if Prescott kept to the timetable. He was going to keep to the timetable.

Before anything left the office, he did what Garside had always done and what Prescott had done himself since Garside's desk became his: he read every citation in the narrative against the original document from which it was drawn. The brief cited the first codicil at exhibit one. Exhibit one was in the strong-room. He took the key from its hook.

---

The strong-room was in the basement: stone floor, iron shelves, the registered documents of thirty years in bundles and deed-boxes at their marked positions. The Eccleston deed-box was on the second shelf — black painted steel, *ECCLESTON* stencilled in white on the lid — and he had been down for it often enough in the past eight months that the shelf knew his hand by now.

The strong-room register sat above, in its place. Garside's hand went back twenty years in that ledger. He did not open it. He knew what was in it; he had known since December.

He lifted the deed-box and carried it upstairs.

---

He opened the box on the desk. The first codicil — the executed original, on good rag paper, in the strong-room since the day of its execution in March 1924 — was in its wrapper at the top. He set it to the left of the desk.

From the action file he drew the propounded second codicil, the instrument produced by Thurston & Marsh. He set it to the right.

He picked up the second codicil and read from the recital.

---

*WHEREAS by a codicil to my said will dated the fifteenth day of March 1924 I gave the residue of my estate unto my niece Margaret Fairhurst and her issue absolutely NOW I HEREBY REVOKE the said codicil in its entirety and I confirm my said will dated the third day of June 1919 in all respects as if the said codicil had not been made.*

---

He put it down and took up the first codicil. He read the operative gift.

---

*...unto my niece Margaret Fairhurst of Bank Parade Preston or her issue absolutely...*

---

He put the first codicil down.

He read the recital again from the gift clause forward, slowly. He stopped at the ninth word.

The recital said *and*. The executed instrument said *or*.

He sat with both documents in front of him and said nothing, because there was no one in the room.

---

Any careful codicil, revoking an earlier instrument, recited that instrument — and took the wording of the recital from somewhere. The recital before him had the date right: *the fifteenth day of March 1924*, word for word, the date the firm had given to Thurston & Marsh in the notice letter of the second of November. The firm had given them nothing else. Not the address of the legatee. Not the operative clause. Nothing beyond the date.

The recital did not have the operative word from the executed text. It had the word a man would carry who had been told about the codicil, once, in speech, and had never read the instrument itself. The spoken sentence and the signed paper were not the same; they had not been the same since March 1924.

He got up and went to the exhibit cabinet.

---

Exhibit fourteen was Walter Eccleston's letter to Mr. Holt, his bank manager, dated the eleventh of June 1925. He had filed it himself, three months ago, as part of the financial evidence. He had not read it since.

He read it now.

---

*Dear Mr. Holt, — You ask what further security I can offer beyond the mortgage. My uncle has made a codicil, as I told you, in favour of my late cousin Mrs. Fairhurst and her issue, but I am assured the arrangement will not stand; he made it in a soft mood and has spoken since of putting it right. My expectations are otherwise what they have always been. — Yours faithfully, WALTER ECCLESTON*

---

The eleventh of June 1925. Fourteen months before the date of the instrument found in the despatch-box.

He returned to the desk and laid all three documents in a row: the executed first codicil, the propounded second codicil, exhibit fourteen. The recital said *and*. The executed text said *or*. The letter — in Walter Eccleston's hand, in the file, written in June of 1925 — said *and*.

He stood for a moment, looking at the three documents.

---

He gathered the exhibit, replaced the first codicil in its wrapper, and returned the deed-box to the second shelf. Then he came back upstairs and sat at the desk.

He drew a sheet from the writing pad.

---

*The recital of the second codicil (R3, exhibit 2) cites the first codicil as giving the residue 'unto my niece Margaret Fairhurst and her issue absolutely.' The executed instrument (R2, exhibit 1) says 'or her issue.' The variance is between R2 and R3's recital. A.P., 18.vii.27.*

---

He set the sheet in the working file. The eighteenth of July. Eighty-eight days.

He sat at the desk for a while: the square outside; the office around him; somewhere below, the strong-room register in Garside's hand on its shelf.

Then he took out a fresh sheet of paper and began to write the addition to the brief.
