# The Witnesses

> SIGNED by the said JOSIAH ECCLESTON as and for his last codicil in the presence of us both
> present at the same time who at his request in his presence and in the presence of each other
> have hereunto subscribed our names as witnesses:
>
> THOMAS BIRCH, Carrier, of Ribchester in the County of Lancaster.
>
> FREDERICK HALLETT, Gardener, of near Salesbury in the County of Lancaster.

Both men were in the instrument index.

The index was a bound ledger, red boards worn soft at the corners, in Garside's hand from its first
entry in 1906 to the last he had made before the autumn. It recorded witness by name, trade, address,
instrument number, and date — so that finding any given specimen was a question of reading a column
and walking to a shelf. Thomas Birch, carrier, of Ribchester: four entries, the earliest in 1912, the
latest in March 1921. Frederick Hallett, gardener, near Salesbury: one entry, the same March 1921
instrument.

Prescott noted the instrument numbers and wrote to the Ribchester registrar that afternoon. He
instructed Booth to pull both instruments from the file.

---

The registrar's reply arrived four days later. Thomas Birch, carrier, of Ribchester, had died on the
twenty-eighth of September 1926. The cause given was influenza. Prescott wrote by return for a
certified copy of the death certificate, and it came within the week.

He opened his attendance book and wrote the date.

The instruments Booth had found carried Birch's signature on both: the deed of cottage tenancy from
November 1914 and the property schedule from March 1921. The 1914 hand was angular and firm at the
ascenders; by 1921 it had settled, the loop of the g broader, the B somewhat taller. Consistent with
itself across seven years. Prescott had been present at the 1921 signing. He packaged both originals
with a covering note and posted them to Croft in Manchester, together with a photographic copy of the
attestation page of the propounded codicil.

The March 1921 property schedule bore one further signature as witness, below Birch's: Hallett's hand,
shorter in body, the letters more angled, a single specimen. He sent it to Croft under a second cover.

---

He had written to Atkinson at Ribchester on the same day as the registrar — Atkinson whose practice
was agricultural conveyancing and the kind of local inquiry that required knowing the farms east of
Langridge Moor.

Atkinson's reply took nine days and ran to two pages. Hallett had worked the previous spring and
summer for a farmer named Ogden on the Salesbury road — kitchen garden and light farmwork. In the
first days of October 1926, Hallett had given notice. He had left no address with Ogden, none with
any neighbour Atkinson could find, none with the post office, which had handled one redirected letter
and could not say where it had gone. Atkinson had then made inquiry at the shipping offices in Preston
and had found that in the second week of October 1926 a Frederick Hallett of Ribchester had taken
an assisted passage from Liverpool to Quebec aboard the *SS Aurania*.

No fixed address in Canada.

Prescott added the departure date to his attendance book below the first entry. Then the next two:
the last week of October, Josiah's death; the first days of November, the despatch-box opened with
Prescott present, no codicil in it. Six days at the mill. The four dates made a column and described
a shape, and the shape described a thing, and he drew his pen through the space where that thing
would have been written, because the brief was not a place for what was not evidence.

---

Croft's two reports arrived in the same cover, their dates of composition ten days apart. They were
written in the same form.

> *In the matter of the Estate of Josiah Eccleston, deceased. I have examined the attestation
> signature attributed to THOMAS BIRCH on a photographic copy of the propounded codicil and
> compared it with the two authenticated specimens submitted: the cottage tenancy deed of November
> 1914 and the property schedule of March 1921, both originals. The signature on the propounded
> instrument is consistent with both specimens in general formation, pen pressure, and the
> development that seven years' interval between specimens would naturally produce. Given that my
> comparison rests upon a photographic copy of the propounded instrument rather than the original,
> and that two authenticated specimens only are available, I am not in a position to offer a
> positive identification. I cannot exclude the possibility of a skilled reproduction executed from
> comparison material of comparable quality to that submitted to me. — H. Croft, Document
> Examiner.*

The second report ran in the same terms. The Hallett attestation, compared against the single
1921 specimen, was consistent in general formation and character; a single specimen did not establish
the full range of a hand; Croft was unable to form a view on natural variation or development across
the intervening period; he was not in a position to offer a positive identification and could not
exclude the possibility of a skilled reproduction.

He put both reports in the file.

---

There was a practical consequence to Hallett being alive rather than dead, which was that a commission
might be applied for. If the Probate Division granted it, a judicial officer in Canada would take
Hallett's evidence on the question of due execution — if Hallett could be located. Prescott wrote to
Mitchell in London with the full position: the witness's identity; his last confirmed employment;
his departure from Ribchester in the first days of October 1926 and his assisted passage aboard the
*Aurania*; Atkinson's inquiries and their result; the confirmed absence of any traceable address.
Mitchell's reply came within the week. The power to issue a commission existed; the difficulty was
personal service, which required an address, which was not available; the commission, if granted,
would take time the calendar did not have. Nevertheless Mitchell recommended applying, disclosing
the difficulty in full.

Prescott agreed, and Mitchell filed the application in the last week of April.

One hundred and seventy-two days to the hearing. The draft of the application was in the file —
the instrument, the witness's identity and last known whereabouts, the absence of a fixed address
in Canada, the submission that his evidence on due execution could not otherwise be obtained. It
was a well-drawn piece of work.

He noted the date of filing in the margin of his attendance book and placed the file on Booth's
tray for the shelf.
