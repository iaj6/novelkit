# Counsel's Brief

The brief had been on course for the first of August. The addition had required another fortnight.

Prescott had drafted the variance section on the night of the eighteenth of July and rewritten it twice in the days following. The difficulty was not in stating the variance: three sentences covered the fact itself. The difficulty was in stating, with the precision counsel would need in court, what the variance proved, what remained to be shown by the rest of the record, and why the combination was sufficient. The brief would be argued in London by a man who had not spent the better part of a year building the record behind it, and it had to stand on its own.

He had put the argument at the front, before the exhibit schedule and the foreclosure section, because that was the correct order for what the brief was trying to do.

The recital of the propounded second codicil quoted the first codicil's gift clause as giving the residue to Margaret Fairhurst *and her issue*. The executed first codicil, on rag paper in the strong-room since the day of its execution, said *or her issue*. A recital that misquoted the instrument it recited had not been taken from that instrument. The question before the court was not whether the recital was wrong; it was where it had been taken from instead.

The nine sections that followed answered the question by exclusion. He had arranged them not in chronological order of investigation but in the order that built, foreclosure upon foreclosure, from what the paper trail showed to what the witnesses confirmed to what the firm's own records established. Each section named one innocent explanation, cited the exhibit that eliminated it, and stated plainly — in a sentence or two, no more — what the elimination proved and what it did not. None of the nine sections was conclusive in isolation, and the brief said so. Together they removed every source for the recital except one.

The authorship section came last in the argument, before the handwriting evidence, and Prescott had written it with the same care he had used for the variance paragraph. He could put it plainly because the record permitted him to put it plainly: the recital reproduced the date of the first codicil in the formal drafter's style — *the fifteenth day of March 1924*, word for word — which was the date as given to Thurston & Marsh in the firm's notice letter of the second of November 1926. The firm had given them nothing else: not the address of the legatee, not the operative clause, nothing beyond the date. The recital did not reproduce the operative word from the executed text. It reproduced the word from a document already in the record — exhibit fourteen, Walter Eccleston to his bank manager, the eleventh of June 1925, in Walter Eccleston's own hand. Fourteen months before the stated date of the instrument alleged to have been found in the despatch-box.

He had written the conclusion of that section twice before he was satisfied with it. *Whatever else remains uncertain about the provenance of the second codicil, the source of the word in its recital is not in question. It is in the record, in the hand of the defendant, before the event it is said to record.*

The brief said what the record permitted it to say, and the record was sufficient.

---

He spent the morning of the ninth of August going through the exhibit schedule against the strong-room register, confirming that every document cited in the brief had a matching custody entry and a clean chain from execution to the present. The September 1926 entry was in Garside's hand, as all the entries before December had been: instrument fetched, date and purpose given, who was present, same-day return. More precise than a routine visit required. It had not been a routine visit, and Garside had written it as though he understood that, which he had not — could not have, in September 1926, with Josiah still living and Walter still the expected heir. He had written it that way because it was his habit to write it that way, and the habit was now the proof.

Prescott checked the entry against the citation in the brief and moved on to the next exhibit.

---

The handwriting evidence occupied a single paragraph in the brief, because a single paragraph was what it warranted. He had the two opinions side by side: Mathers for the plaintiff in solemn form and Whitmore for the defendant, each a document examiner of twenty years' standing, each in direct and frank contradiction of the other. He had written the paragraph standing at the window before he sat down to draft it fair.

---

*The plaintiff retains the opinion of Mr. A.V. Mathers, document examiner, who finds the signature on the propounded second codicil not genuine to the hand of the testator. The defendant has produced the opinion of Mr. G.T. Whitmore, document examiner, who finds it genuine. The two opinions are directly irreconcilable; neither examiner has indicated any cause for doubt in his conclusion. Counsel is asked to note that the plaintiff's case does not depend upon either report and will not be advanced as though it does. The evidence on authorship is in the text and in the exhibits as described above.*

---

He had shown the paragraph to Mr. Booth, who read it and returned it without remark.

---

The letter from Thurston & Marsh arrived on the morning of the twelfth. Mr. Booth brought it through with the post and set it apart on Prescott's desk — the return address had been sufficient — and Prescott put down his pen.

It was signed by Marsh himself. Three paragraphs: a note that the action was proceeding to hearing on the fixed date; a formal inquiry as to whether the plaintiff in solemn form intended to proceed on all exhibits as filed; and a final paragraph expressing a professional hope that any late evidence which had come to Prescott's attention might be communicated to the defence in the usual way, so that the hearing might proceed without inconvenience to either side.

It was very carefully written.

Prescott passed it across to Mr. Booth, who read it once.

"They know something is coming," Mr. Booth said.

Prescott took out a sheet. The reply drafted itself in twenty minutes: confirming that the plaintiff intended to proceed in solemn form on all exhibits as filed; no late evidence; the plaintiff looked forward to the hearing on the fixed date. Professional, exact, offering Marsh nothing he did not already have, because there was nothing to offer. He signed the letter when it came back typed and filed the exchange in the action file, in date order, behind the brief.

Mr. Booth returned to his desk without further remark, which was how both of them preferred it.

---

The brief was sealed on the fifteenth of August. The instruction letter to counsel ran to three pages: the structure of the argument set out; exhibits enclosed in schedule order; three original instruments to travel separately under registered cover to London for the hearing — the will clause from the strong-room, the executed first codicil from the deed-box, the propounded second codicil from the court file. Before he wrote that line, Prescott had Mr. Booth bring the Eccleston deed-box and set it open on the desk, and he went through it against the inventory himself.

The deed-box held exactly what the custody record said it held.

He signed the instruction letter — the last document he would send to London before the hearing — and it went with the parcelled brief for the first post. The originals of R1 and R2 were wrapped under their registered numbers separately, acknowledgment required on receipt; R3 would travel with Thurston & Marsh, as the propounded instrument.

Every exhibit in the record was accounted for. Every custody entry was clean.

---

Mr. Booth came back from the post office at half past eleven with the registered mail receipt, which he set on the corner of the desk. Prescott noted it and wrote the dispatch into the file.

The brief was in London by now or would be before morning. Counsel would have it on the sixteenth.

Fifty-two days to the hearing.

He set the receipt in its place in the file, closed the folder, and took out the next correspondence.
