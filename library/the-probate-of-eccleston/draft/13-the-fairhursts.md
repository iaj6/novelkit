# The Fairhursts

The parks were greener than in November. Prescott walked from Winckley Square in the early afternoon, the river visible below Miller Park where the path curved west, the trees in full leaf and the light long enough to reach the far bank. He had not particularly noticed it was spring until today.

The Fairhurst house on Bank Parade showed its south face to the afternoon. The stone was less shuttered than it had been in autumn, the terrace looking lighter, almost sandy, in the May light. He arrived at a quarter past two and Nora Fairhurst opened the door before he could use the bell.

She showed him into the parlour. The room was recognisable — the dark sideboard, the pair of chairs either side of the empty grate — though the curtain at the window was drawn back today and the light came in across the parks. She went to call her father.

Edgar Fairhurst came in with George a step behind him. George was taller than Prescott had remembered, or had grown since November; he sat in the chair at the window while his father and Prescott arranged themselves at the table.

---

The April account was three pages from Hadfield: a loss of just under three hundred pounds against projected income; mortgage interest from capital for the third consecutive quarter-day; the mill working three and a half days in a normal week, wages stable. Prescott set the sheets between them.

Edgar read with his elbows on his knees and did not speak for a moment. He had learned to read a balance sheet in the course of a legal action he had not expected and would not have chosen.

"Is this typical?" he said.

"It is consistent with the preceding months," Prescott said. "Hadfield expects no improvement before late summer."

Edgar looked at the sheets a moment longer, then set them aside. "I suppose I am asking how confident you are."

"The record is building. The hearing date is fixed. The firm is confident, and when I am in a position to say more than that, I will say it."

He sat back in his chair.

George turned from the window then.

"What happens to the mill?" he said. "If the action fails."

Prescott looked at him. Sixteen; and he had put the question in the terms his father had not.

"The estate passes to Mr. Walter Eccleston under the second codicil," Prescott said. "His sale contract completes upon a grant of representation establishing his title. When that happens, the mill is sold. The proceeds go to Mr. Eccleston and, in large part, to his creditors."

"The men lose their jobs."

"In all probability, yes."

George turned back to the window. He said nothing more.

---

Nora brought the tea things through and poured without being asked. They worked through the remaining items — a letter requiring Edgar's signature on the administrator's quarterly renewal; a question from Mitchell in London about the form of the children's instructions, which Edgar answered clearly and in three sentences.

One hundred and fifty-five days to the hearing. Prescott wrote the figure in his attendance book and said nothing in the room.

He was gathering the signed papers when Nora said, setting down her cup, that Walter Eccleston had come to call last week.

Prescott looked up.

"He wrote first," she said. "He asked whether he might — he said he understood if not — and Father said he supposed there was no reason he should not. He came on a Tuesday."

"And was it—"

"He was kind. He seemed genuinely fond of Mother. He told us what Mother had been to receive — what the codicil meant for us. He said grandfather had made a codicil giving everything to Mother and her issue, which meant us. He said grandfather had always meant to put it right, and that he was sorry it had come to this." She was quiet a moment. "He was sorry in the way people are when they have done something they believe they had to do. It was not performed."

Prescott wrote the substance: Walter's visit; Tuesday, approximately the eighth of May; what Walter had said about the codicil's terms. He asked whether she was sure of the date and she said the eighth or the ninth, and he wrote the eighth with a small query beside it. He moved on to the matter of the signed letter.

---

The letter was in order. He sorted the papers and clipped them into their sequence. Nora sat with her cup.

"I think Mother would have been uncomfortable," she said, "being made the subject of an action. She was not a person who liked to be the reason for anything. She would have been glad it was being done and would not have wanted to know the detail."

"That is not unusual," Prescott said, which was true, and insufficient.

"I hope the record is enough."

"I believe it is."

"Grandfather wrote to her," Nora said. "When he made the codicil. I found the letter after she died, in a box she kept in the wardrobe. The tone of it was—" She stopped. "It was kind. It was not a man saying he had done something to settle an obligation."

Prescott knew the letter. He had carried it out of this house in November, Exhibit A in Garside's envelope. He said: "Yes."

He fastened his bag and stood. He declined tea — he had a brief to review that evening — and Edgar showed him to the door.

---

The light was still long over Miller Park. He went back the way he had come, the river running below in the afternoon between the trees, the path along the top of the bank occupied in a loose and leisurely way that seemed to belong to a different order of time from the one in which he was working. An old man on a bench with a newspaper folded against the wind. Two children crossing the path with a dog that briefly considered Prescott and decided against it.

He had the letter in the brief. He had had it since November — Garside's envelope, the fold marks of a document kept in a wardrobe rather than a file, read and returned to rather than put away. He had read it then and filed it under A and thought of it as a document, which it was.

He walked to the station. The train ran south along the river for twelve minutes, and he sat with his bag on the rack above him and the evening beginning outside the window, and he did not think about anything in particular until the town came back around him and he got off at Preston.
