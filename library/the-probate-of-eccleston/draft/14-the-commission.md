# The Commission

The letter from Mitchell came by the first post on Tuesday. Booth brought it up from the letter-rack and set it on the desk above the Hadfield figures Prescott had been working through — London postmark, Mitchell's own hand on the envelope — and Prescott opened it with the small bronze knife he kept for correspondence, which had been Garside's.

The refusal was in the first paragraph.

---

*The court has declined to issue the commission to examine Mr. Frederick Hallett. The ground stated is that the applicant has been unable to supply a fixed address in Canada at which the commission could be served, and the court will not issue a commission that cannot be executed. A fresh application would require a verified address. The decision is not appealable on these facts.*

---

He read it a second time. He set it on the desk.

He rose and walked to the window. The square below was fully in leaf — the lime trees in front of the courthouse had come on considerably in the past fortnight — and a postman was working along the opposite pavement at a pace that did not suggest urgency. A woman was crossing below with a basket, looking at nothing in particular. He stood at the window and allowed the thing to settle.

The witnesses were done. Both of them, from both directions: Birch was dead, and his death was in the registrar's certificate; Hallett had gone to Canada without an address, and the court could not reach him there on the available materials. Mitchell had submitted the application with the address difficulty disclosed in full — there had been no other way to proceed with credit — and the court had done what courts did when the mechanism of service did not exist. Four weeks had passed from the application in the last week of April to this Tuesday in May: four weeks that could not be recovered. There had been a hundred and seventy-two days on the clock when Mitchell had filed; there were a hundred and forty now. The difference was the commission application, entirely.

A man who sat down immediately had not quite finished admitting the thing to himself. He stood a while longer at the window and admitted it.

The attestation was permanently closed, from every direction that involved a living witness. Neither man could now be examined, whether to break the second codicil's due execution or to support it. The defence's case rested on the silence of the witnesses: unassailable, because nobody living could speak to what had or had not happened in August 1926 in the presence of two men who were, respectively, dead and somewhere in Canada. Prescott's side was in the same position, on the other face of the same coin — neither attestation could be confirmed or broken, because neither attester could be reached. It was not a defeat in any sense the brief could name; it was a closed door, and the brief had not been built on this door. But there was a difference between choosing not to lean on a route and having it closed behind you.

There was a shape to the situation, however.

Six weeks ago, after entering the four-date column in the attendance book — Birch dead on the twenty-eighth of September, Hallett gone in the second week of October, Josiah dead in the third week, the despatch-box inventoried in early November — he had drawn his pen through the space where the inference would have gone. He returned to that space now. The two witnesses named on the second codicil had been chosen from among the men who had genuinely witnessed Josiah's instruments in years past: men in Garside's index, men whose authentic signatures were therefore on file, men against whose known hands Croft could take a fair comparison. Both had become unavailable in the same autumn, within days of each other. Both had been newly unavailable — one dead, one gone — in the weeks before the despatch-box stood six days at the mill in October.

A document executed in August, before the influenza had run its course, before the passage to Quebec, would have named them without foreknowledge that either would be gone.

Unless someone had composed it afterward, and dated it to August.

He did not write this in the attendance book. He had already drawn a pen through it once, and that had been right: a thought crossed out does not become evidence by revival. What it might become — with authorship established on independent grounds and each innocent explanation foreclosed by document rather than argument — was something counsel could set before the court in the proper order. Not today. In due course.

He returned to the desk and drew a sheet of paper from the drawer.

He wrote to Mitchell. The commission had failed; the witness avenue was definitively closed; they were to proceed on the footing that the case must be made on the typewriter evidence and the text. He asked Mitchell to confirm his understanding of the position and to advise on what form the text argument should take in the brief — what it would be required to establish, and in what sequence it would need to be put. He noted the calendar: a hundred and forty days to the hearing; the brief must be complete within eight weeks at the outside.

He read the letter through and made one correction: *are proceeding* to *must proceed*. He sealed it and carried it through to Booth's tray.

"Midday post," he said.

Booth looked up from the letter-book. "Right," he said, and returned to it.

The commission letter went into the file in its correct position: behind the application, ahead of Mitchell's April note. Prescott clipped the folder and squared it to the left of the desk. The file grew in the way files grew when a proceeding was properly kept — one document at a time, each in its right place, the record of everything attempted and everything concluded, the corridor of closed doors. A solicitor who left an avenue unexamined in solemn form proceedings could not afterward explain himself, which was why the application had been made, and why the commission letter was now in the file; both reasons remained sound. The avenue had been examined. The result was in the record.

What remained was the text.

A hundred and forty days. He wrote the figure in the margin of Mitchell's letter before filing it.

He took the Hadfield figures from the desk and settled back to the columns he had been working through when the post came in.
