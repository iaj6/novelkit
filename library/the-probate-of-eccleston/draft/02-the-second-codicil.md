# Chapter 2 — The Second Codicil

Prescott was halfway through the Bank Parade letter when Mr. Booth appeared in the doorway with the morning post.

The Thurston & Marsh envelope was on top. It was thicker than a simple acknowledgment — thick enough for an enclosure.

The covering note was on their good paper. Their client, it advised, had lately found among his late uncle's personal papers a codicil to the will, dated the fourteenth day of August nineteen-twenty-six, which he believed had been made privately by the testator in the final months of his life. A copy was enclosed. In these circumstances, they trusted that the will might now be proved in common form in the ordinary way; they would be glad to hear how their client might proceed.

Garside read it at his desk, held out his hand for the enclosure, read that too, and set it face-down on the blotter.

"The letter-book," he said.

Prescott took the letter-book from the cupboard. The carbon of the firm's notice letter was two pages in — dated the second of November, the typing clean.

> Dear Sirs, — We act in the estate of the late Mr. Josiah Eccleston of Eccleston Hall. We hold his will dated the 3rd June 1919 and a codicil thereto dated the 15th March 1924 which materially affects the disposition of the residue. We shall be glad to receive your client's proposals for proving the will in proper form. — Yours faithfully, GARSIDE & PRESCOTT

He closed the letter-book and set it on the table.

Garside turned the enclosure over and read it aloud:

> WHEREAS by a codicil to my said will dated the fifteenth day of March 1924 I gave the residue of my estate unto my niece Margaret Fairhurst and her issue absolutely NOW I HEREBY REVOKE the said codicil in its entirety and I confirm my said will dated the third day of June 1919 in all respects as if the said codicil had not been made.

Outside the window Winckley Square lay quiet in the November light.

"Dated the fourteenth of August," Prescott said.

"Yes."

The despatch-box had been inventoried in the last week of October, a week after the death, with Prescott recording each item as it came out and Walter present beside the table. No codicil among them — his attendance note was in the file. After the inventory, the box had stood at the mill, in Walter's keeping, for six days. The codicil had been produced after the box was returned to the Hall.

"The firm received no instruction to draw this," Prescott said. "No request for the text of the first codicil. No inquiry as to its terms."

"We gave them the date in the notice." Garside's forefinger rested on the letter-book. "I thought it proper. That is all we sent."

Prescott took the copy to the window. The paper was good foolscap, typed on a machine he could not immediately place as belonging to the firm or to any office associated with it. The signature — *Josiah Eccleston*, in dark ink — was in a formed hand of the right general character; it offered nothing that announced itself as certainly genuine or certainly not. Below the signature, an attestation clause: *Thomas Birch*, carrier, of a Ribchester address; *Frederick Hallett*, gardener, of a lane near Salesbury.

"Neither of them at their given addresses, I should think," he said.

"That remains to be determined." Garside was reaching for his overcoat; he had a meeting elsewhere. "Write to Bank Parade this afternoon. Mr. Fairhurst must enter a caveat before any other step is taken, and he must do it promptly. The instrument has two witnesses and a date and a signature that will not be settled quickly." He had his hat. "Nothing to Thurston and Marsh beyond acknowledgment. Nothing at all."

---

Walter Eccleston called at the office that same afternoon.

No appointment had been made. Mr. Booth brought his card in without comment, and Prescott arrived in the front room a moment later to find Walter already settled in the chair opposite Garside's desk, coat and hat set aside. He had the ease of a man who had spent twenty years at home in other people's offices. He greeted Garside and Prescott both. He said he had come himself because it seemed the right thing to do, given the long connection.

He was fifty or thereabouts — broad in the shoulder, a dark wool coat, the considered quiet that came from managing men through a long stretch of difficult years. He had known Josiah Eccleston all his working life and had known this firm almost as long.

His account was orderly. He had gone through his uncle's insurance papers in the despatch-box in the days following the inventory. He was, he said, very conscious of having been present at that inventory and of the codicil not being there then. His best understanding was that Josiah must have kept it separately and it had simply been overlooked, or added to the box on a later occasion. Josiah had been up at the mill three or four times in the spring, before the illness; the mill office had a typewriter, and it would have been nothing for him to sit down and draft something out. He had always transacted his correspondence at the mill rather than at the Hall.

"He always said the arrangement for Margaret and her issue wouldn't stand as he'd left it," Walter said, in a tone that was not argumentative, only explanatory. "That he'd made it in a soft mood and always meant to put it right. He said so more than once, and to more than one person." He looked briefly at his hands. "I would very much rather there were no solemn-form action about this. These are Margaret's children. I have no wish to be in dispute with Margaret's children."

Garside said that the firm must advise its clients and take instructions in the ordinary way, and that nothing further could be said at this stage. Walter accepted this with a brief nod — the nod of a man accustomed to solicitors' answers. He stood, and shook Garside's hand, and then Prescott's, and left without further remark.

Prescott wrote the attendance note in full: Walter's account of finding the codicil, his description of Josiah's correspondence habits at the mill, the timing of the despatch-box as Walter understood it, his stated wish to avoid proceedings. All of it dated and initialled and clipped to the file.

Then he went back to the letter-book and read the November second carbon a second time. The firm's notice letter had given Walter's solicitors the date of the first codicil in writing: the fifteenth of March nineteen-twenty-four. Nothing beyond it. No text; no terms; no word of the instrument except that it bore that date and materially affected the residue.

---

Edgar Fairhurst entered the caveat at the Probate Registry on the eighth of November. The notice arrived at Winckley Square three days later — a printed card, the date stamped in violet ink — and Prescott noted it in the file and clipped it to the caveat cover.

The file now held the will, the first codicil, the despatch-box inventory attendance note, the firm's notice letter carbon, Walter's attendance note, and the copy of the second codicil. A second codicil the firm had never been asked to draw, in an estate it had administered for forty years without a privately-made instrument of any kind.

He set the file on Mr. Booth's tray. Outside Winckley Square the light was already gone.
