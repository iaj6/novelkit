# Solemn Form

He had the deed-box under his arm by nine. He had been awake at half past three, had read through the authorship section of the brief by the bedside lamp, found no errors in it, and put it face-down on the desk. He did not sleep again until after five. The walk from the hotel to the Royal Courts of Justice was twenty minutes along the Strand, and the October morning was cold enough that he was glad of it — the room had been warm and small, and he had done everything in it that could be done.

The Royal Courts of Justice stood at the eastern end of the Strand — the particular Victorian Gothic of the State announcing its intentions in stone and pinnacles — and it was the third time Prescott had been inside it on Garside & Prescott business, the first two being a chambers meeting and a matter that had settled before argument and been no business of the court at all. The building had not diminished in the interval. He crossed the forecourt with the deed-box and found the corridor for the Probate, Divorce and Admiralty Division, which already held the morning's population of barristers in gowns, their solicitors at their elbows, each pair apart from the next with the composed patience of people who do this regularly and have learned that patience is part of the work. The smell of the place was distinctive — old stone, varnished wood, coal smoke from the court fires working behind closed doors — the smell of a building that has held the same proceedings for fifty years and taken the character of them into its walls.

Their counsel found him before ten. Keane was a tall man of about forty-five, considered in manner, who had acknowledged the brief in August with a letter of three sentences and who had since written twice with questions about the exclusion section. He had the unhurried confidence of a man who has read the brief and finds it sufficient. He shook Prescott's hand and asked whether the originals were in the deed-box.

Prescott confirmed they were.

They stood together in the corridor while it filled further. The courtroom doors had not yet opened; through the gap where a latch had not quite caught, Prescott could see the brass rail of the witness box and the bench above it, both empty.

---

Counsel for the defendant came across to them at ten minutes to ten. His name was Ardern, and Prescott knew from the action papers that he had been at the bar for twenty-five years. He was a shorter man, compact, who moved through the corridor with the ease of long practice.

The request was framed correctly. Whether the plaintiff's solicitor might permit the defendant a few minutes to examine the executed original of the first codicil, before the judge took his seat — purely for the purposes of instruction, Ardern said, and Prescott's side would retain custody throughout. It was said without the tone of a man asking a favour, and without the tone of a man who expected to be refused.

Keane looked at Prescott, briefly, because it was Prescott's deed-box.

Prescott set it on the bench beside him and opened the lid. The Eccleston papers were arranged as he had laid them in Preston the week before: the custody list on top, the originals beneath in exhibit order. He lifted the custody list, and beneath it, in a folded envelope marked in his own hand — *R2. First Codicil (executed original)* — was the instrument that had been in the strong-room since the fifteenth of March 1924.

He handed the envelope to Ardern without speaking.

---

Walter Eccleston was standing eight or ten feet away, beside a young man from Thurston & Marsh that Prescott did not recognise. He had come into the building while Prescott and Keane were talking; Prescott had noted his arrival at the edge of his attention without looking at him directly. He looked at him now. Walter was watching Ardern cross the corridor with the envelope.

Ardern held the envelope out. Walter took it. He opened it carefully — he was a careful man — and drew out the codicil. Rag paper, folded twice, typewritten, a single page. He unfolded it and began to read.

He read it the way a man reads a document he expects to confirm what he knows: quickly, his eyes moving across the type.

And then he slowed.

He went back a line, read it again; went back further; came to rest at a particular point in the instrument and stayed. He read it again from that point, deliberately, his head very slightly inclined. Then he read it a third time and was quite still.

He was fifty or thereabouts, broad-shouldered, a man whose presence had always been larger than his physical size. He was not large now. He held the instrument and stood without moving, and something in the set of his shoulders was different from a moment before.

---

Prescott watched and did not speak.

Walter had never been in a room with the executed text before this moment. He had been wrong since March 1924 and had not known it.

Prescott kept his face composed and his hands at his sides. There was nothing to say.

After some moments, Walter spoke briefly to Ardern — low, inaudible at the distance. Ardern put a hand once on Walter's arm, professional and brief, and withdrew with him down the corridor. Two men, twenty feet away, a conference too quiet to follow.

Keane stood beside Prescott and did not speak, which was correct.

A barrister passed between them on his way to another court, acknowledged Keane with a nod, and moved on.

Three minutes, or a little more.

When Ardern returned, his manner was composed in the way of a man who has instructions he does not enjoy executing but who will execute them precisely.

"My client is not in a position to proceed," he said. "I am instructed to apply for a withdrawal of the claim."

---

The matter came before the judge at a quarter past ten: the application to withdraw; Keane's assent on behalf of the plaintiff in solemn form; the brief exchange of counsel establishing the disposition of the instruments and the question of costs. The judge was a spare man of about sixty with a dry manner and a habit of precise speech who had clearly had the brief — his questions were exact — and who received the application without visible surprise.

The second codicil had not been proved; accordingly it was not to be admitted to probate. The instruments of 1919 and 1924 stood. Letters of administration with the will annexed would be sought in due course through the Principal Registry.

The handwriting evidence: two opinions, directly irreconcilable — one finding the signature genuine, one finding it not. The judge noted that the decision had not rested on either. He added one observation, speaking to the courtroom at large rather than to either set of counsel: that in cases of this kind the most reliable witnesses were frequently the most ordinary — a letter-book entry, a register note, a word in a recital — because ordinary documents do not accommodate themselves to what comes after. He said it in the same flat tone he had used throughout, and moved to the costs.

The costs were to be paid by the defendant. Garside & Prescott, which had carried the action at its own risk from the caveat onward, would recover them in full.

---

By eleven the corridor was nearly empty. The matter had gone quickly — as matters go quickly, Prescott knew, once the decision has been made before it reaches the court.

Keane gave him his hand and said that it had been well conducted. That was the right amount to say. Prescott thanked him, and they parted.

He opened the deed-box and replaced the first codicil, in its envelope, in its place in the exhibit order. He noted the custody on the list: *R2. Returned from use. RCJ, 6 Oct. 1927. A.P.* He closed the lid.

He would telephone Mr. Booth from the hotel before midday. He would write to Edgar Fairhurst this afternoon.

He picked up the deed-box and walked toward the entrance.
