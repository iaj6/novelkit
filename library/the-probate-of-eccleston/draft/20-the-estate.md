# The Estate

The afternoon post from the hotel went at half past four. He sat at the writing table with the window behind him and the Strand below, October rain finding the gutters steadily, and wrote to Edgar Fairhurst.

The letter was brief. The action had succeeded; the second codicil had failed of proof; letters of administration with the will annexed would be sought in due course through the Principal Registry. The estate would pass to the children under the first codicil of the fifteenth of March 1924. He would write again with more detail when he returned to Preston.

He sealed it, addressed it, and took it down to the desk.

---

The grant was made in the third week of November. Edgar Fairhurst, as guardian of both children, received letters of administration with the will annexed; the papers were in order and their counsel had attended at the Principal Probate Registry without incident. It was not probate. No executor had been made. These were the correct forms.

Prescott filed the grant in the matter, sent copies to Edgar Fairhurst, and noted the administration as now running. The estate was the administrator's province from here; what Garside & Prescott held was the record.

---

He visited Bank Parade on a Friday in late November, on his way back from a Ribchester appointment. The terrace was in the particular late-afternoon light of November in Preston — the shadow arriving early, the stone fronts taking it differently according to their aspect, some in full dark while the houses at the terrace's far end still held a thin brightness from the west.

Edgar Fairhurst opened the door himself. He had been composed at every stage of the thing, in the way of a man who has reckoned what composure costs and pays it anyway. He took Prescott's coat and called upstairs.

Nora came down. She was quieter than Prescott had expected — not exultant, not aggrieved, the way people are when something long-awaited arrives and proves to be not quite what was imagined. She thanked him and shook his hand and sat in the parlour with her father while Prescott went through the estate's position: the Hall to be put in order under the administrator's authority; the mill shares; the figures as they stood, accurate within the administrator's last monthly statement.

George appeared at the parlour door while Prescott was speaking. He came in without being asked and sat beside his sister without speaking.

When Prescott reached the mill — that it would need to be sold, that the mortgage could not be serviced from current income, that the proceeds after discharge of the mortgage would leave something, though not what the mill might have fetched in other times — George was quiet for a moment. Then he asked what happened to the men.

"The men pass with the sale," Prescott said. "They are the purchaser's concern."

He said it correctly. He was not certain it had been the right thing to say.

He stayed another twenty minutes, answered the remaining questions, and left before dark. The walk back to Winckley Square was a quarter-hour and the evening was cold and the square was nearly empty by the time he reached the office; Mr. Booth had already locked the front.

---

The mill was sold in January at a price that discharged the mortgage and left a residue. The administrator's figures had been accurate throughout. The estate was not a ruin; it was less than Walter's sale would have produced, in times when the mill had been mortgageable at a better rate, but the children were not poor. The Hall was theirs.

---

The two handwriting reports remained in the file as filed — one finding the signature genuine, one not — and that was where they would remain. The decision had not rested on either.

---

The prosecution followed. Walter Eccleston was charged in the new year, and the criminal matter went where it must in cases of that kind. He was not a monster. He went down anyway.

---

The costs were taxed and paid in December. Garside & Prescott, which had carried the action from the caveat at its own risk, recovered them in full. On a Thursday morning Mr. Booth brought the fee note across for signature — set it on the desk in Prescott's office, the blotter, a pen — and Prescott signed it and handed it back. Mr. Booth filed it without comment and returned to his desk.

The firm was solvent. It would continue.

It was Prescott's firm now, in the way that things come to be yours: not as a gift but as what remains when the person who built them is gone. He had known it since December, since the file and the action and the strong-room register had all come to him at once. He had not thought about it much in the interval. There had not been time.

---

The matter file for *In the Estate of Josiah Eccleston, deceased* — the caveat and its exhibits, the forty-six negative returns from the canvass, the document examiner's eleven-page report, the commission papers and the refusal, the Hall depositions, the correspondence from Thurston & Marsh, the brief and its supplement, the attendance notes back to the first visit to the Hall in October 1926, the fee note now noting costs recovered — came to the strong-room in the new year, once the costs receipt was filed. A little more than three inches of cotton-taped paper.

Prescott carried it down himself. He set it on the second shelf, in its place, and then opened the register to record the completion.

He entered it in the column beside Garside's last entries: *Eccleston estate. Action determined in solemn form, Probate, Divorce and Admiralty Division. Costs recovered. Matter closed.* He wrote the date.

Then he looked up the page.

Three pages back, in Garside's hand — the older man's lettering that had filled the register since 1906, steady and exact and not given to ornament — was the September 1926 entry: the date the deed-box had been fetched from the shelf for reading at the Hall; the first codicil, by its date and description; the instrument read entire; who was present; the date confirmed; returned the same day. In Garside's hand.

More precise than the occasion required. He could not have known it; he had made it anyway.

Prescott closed the register and returned it to its place on the shelf. He locked the strong-room and went upstairs.

The day's correspondence lay on his desk, unopened. The next file sat on top of it: a conveyancing matter, Booth's note clipped to the front. A Tuesday in January, fifteen months since Josiah Eccleston had died at eighty-one in the east bedroom at Eccleston Hall, in October, on a Tuesday.

Prescott sat down and opened the file.
