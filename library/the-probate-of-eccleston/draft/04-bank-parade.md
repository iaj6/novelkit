# Chapter Four — Bank Parade

The Avenham district began past the Corn Exchange, where Fishergate dropped and the legal quarter fell behind. Prescott walked it in under ten minutes on a Tuesday afternoon in late November, the pavements still wet from the morning and the light already coming in low. Bank Parade ran along the ridge above the river parks — a terrace of early Victorian stone and brick, two storeys above modest front gardens, the whole of the Ribble valley opening out somewhere below in the grey.

He found the house without difficulty. The brass plate was polished and the step was clean. From somewhere in the terrace came the sound of a piano — scales, patient and precise. He knocked.

Edgar Fairhurst opened the door himself. He was a man in his middle forties, thin-faced, composed in the careful way of someone who has had several days to prepare for a visit. He said he was glad Prescott had come and stood aside.

The hall had the unmistakable character of a schoolmaster's household: coats ranked on the row of hooks, an umbrella stand full, a stack of exercise books on the hall table with a Latin primer propped against them. Prescott took off his hat. From the landing above, a boy of about sixteen looked down; Edgar Fairhurst spoke his name quietly, and the boy went up.

In the parlour, a young woman stood when Prescott entered. She was nineteen. She stood the way a person stands who has decided not to sit until she understands what is happening.

"My daughter Nora," Edgar Fairhurst said.

They said how do you do to one another, all three, and they sat. The fire in the grate had been recently attended to. The bookshelves against the far wall ran floor to ceiling: school texts and household books mingled without system, the way books accumulate in a house where reading is not ceremonial.

He had explained legal actions to clients before — the shape of a contested probate, the risk, the time. He had done it in offices and in country houses and once in a farm kitchen in very bad weather. He had not done it before in a house that smelled of schoolroom coal and was clearly inhabited by people who had been getting on with things for a year and a half since something necessary to them was taken away.

He set out the situation plainly. A second codicil to Mr. Josiah Eccleston's will had been produced, some three weeks after the funeral, by Walter Eccleston through his solicitors Thurston and Marsh. A caveat had been entered on the advice of this firm. The action that would follow from the caveat was an action to prove the will and both codicils in solemn form, before the Probate Division of the High Court. The firm's position was that the second codicil was not genuine. He said this clearly, and then said: that position was not yet evidence, and building evidence took time. He would not pretend otherwise.

Edgar Fairhurst sat with his hands in his lap. He asked, in the orderly way of a man who has been composing his questions for some days: what did the firm believe the likelihood of success was.

Prescott said he would not put a fraction to it. The work was not done and the record was not yet sufficient. What he would say was that the firm had entered its name on the caveat with clear eyes and intended to carry the action to the hearing.

Fairhurst said: at the firm's own cost.

Prescott said: costs recoverable on success. He said it plainly, because the arrangement stated clearly at the outset was the firmest thing he could offer, and because Edgar Fairhurst was a careful man who would not be served by soft words about uncertain things.

Fairhurst looked at his hands for a moment. He said: I cannot fund it myself.

Prescott said he knew that.

A pause. Then: you have my instructions. Whatever the firm requires of us, we will provide.

It was brief. Prescott was used to instructions that took rather longer to arrive at the same destination; brevity of this kind was not simplicity.

Nora Fairhurst had not spoken. She spoke now.

She said: what was our mother to have had. Not abstractly. She wanted the particulars.

Prescott said: the residue of Mr. Josiah Eccleston's estate. That was to say, Eccleston Hall, the mill shares, and the balance of the money. Under the first codicil, the gift to Mrs. Fairhurst passed, on her death before Mr. Eccleston, to her issue — which was to say, to her children.

Nora said: and the second codicil proposes to take that back.

Prescott said: it proposes to revoke the first codicil. Yes.

She received this without comment.

She said: how long will it take.

He said: he did not know with certainty. More than a year, he thought.

She thanked him for saying so, without any sentiment beyond the words, and did not ask anything further.

He explained the administrator pendente lite: a neutral accountant, appointed by the court, neither side's man, who would manage the estate while the action was pending. Collect rents and dividends; pay the estate's debts; distribute nothing to anyone until the matter was resolved. His monthly figures on the estate's condition would be produced in the proceedings. Fairhurst accepted this without objection. There was no objection to be made; it was the mechanism the law required, and it was not an unjust one.

Then Fairhurst said: there is something that may be useful to you. He went to the writing table near the window and came back with a letter, one sheet, folded once, addressed in an old man's handwriting — the kind that has kept its form but slowed in the shaping of individual letters.

He said: it came to Margaret in March 1924. After she had visited Ribchester.

Prescott opened it.

---

*My dear Margaret, — I was very glad of Wednesday's meeting, and of seeing your children after so long. The matter we spoke of I have now attended to — settled is the word I mean to use, and I use it deliberately; I did not act in a soft mood that I expected to revise. I hope you will write to me from Bank Parade and tell me how George goes on at his lessons. — Your affectionate uncle, JOSIAH ECCLESTON.*

*15th March 1924.*

---

He read it once and then held it in his hands a moment. Then he said: might he take the original. It would go into the file as an exhibit; he would give Mr. Fairhurst a receipt.

Fairhurst said: take it.

Prescott wrote the receipt in his notebook, tore out the leaf, and set it on the writing table.

There was nothing further. He declined the offer of tea and thanked them both, and they returned to the hall. From upstairs came a brief sound — a chair, perhaps, or a door. The piano in the terrace had gone quiet.

He said good afternoon and Edgar Fairhurst said good afternoon and the door closed behind him.

---

The parks fell away below the ridge in terraces toward the river. Prescott went down through the upper park along the path by the balustrade, the Ribble grey below and a heron working the near bank. The light was already off the water. It was not yet four.

He was almost at the gate when he turned once. Along the terrace behind him, one upper window had its lamp lit.

He walked back to Winckley Square.

---

In the office he opened the file and wrote the attendance note. He recorded the date and the address and the instructions given. He recorded what documents had been produced: one letter, Josiah Eccleston to Margaret Fairhurst, the fifteenth of March 1924, original retained; receipt given. He numbered it Exhibit A in the matter and set it in the inside-cover pocket of the file.

The file had always had an address in it — Bank Parade, Preston — but it had been a legal address.

He put the file aside and went to draft the letter to Thurston and Marsh.
