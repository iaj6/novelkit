# Vale Mill

The motor took the road east out of Preston at half past eight on a Thursday morning in early March. The Ribble had been over its banks the previous week; the fields below the road still stood in water. Where the road fell toward the valley floor, the mud had crept across the lane, and the tyres moved with less assurance than Prescott preferred.

In September, Garside had driven and Prescott had been in the passenger seat with the Eccleston deed-box at his feet. An autumn morning, still warm; the family arranged in the Hall's drawing room when they arrived, and Garside's voice on the legal periods going exactly at the pace the instrument required. Prescott had sat with the box in his lap and thought about the quality of the light, and the draft from the windows, and nothing in particular. He had not been attending to the right thing.

He drove out of the valley at Longridge and back down into it again where the road turned toward Ribchester. The river showed through the trees as a grey-brown band, high for the season. Cold; the sort of day that refuses to lift.

---

Vale Mill stood on the hillside above the village on its own ground: stone-built, four storeys, a Victorian weaving shed of moderate scale. The engine-room chimney put out a thin plume of smoke, but the weaving shed was silent when Prescott came up through the gate. Late morning, between shifts; the looms not running; forty men in the village below waiting on the afternoon bell.

He left the motor on the road and went up through the yard. A mechanic crouched over something near the engine-room door and looked up without interest and looked back down. The counting house was through a plain wooden door at the ground level, glass-paned, with a card pinned to the frame: *Administrator — Estate of Josiah Eccleston, Decd.*

---

Mr. Hadfield was a precise, middle-sized man in a dark suit who had been working through the estate papers before Prescott arrived. He had the mill's accounts spread across the counting-house table — column books, receipt books, a sheaf of correspondence from the mortgage holder — and to one side of them, near the door, lay a printed mortgage instrument bearing the firm's name at its foot: *Garside & Prescott, Solicitors, Winckley Square, Preston.* At the bottom of the second page, Garside's signature was visible as the witnessing solicitor: small, exact, done for the record. Prescott had seen it ten thousand times at least. He looked back at the administrator.

"The figures for January," Hadfield said. He placed a single sheet across the table: the mill's income against its fixed charges for the month. The columns did not meet. "February is not better. I have interim figures — the month hasn't closed — but they follow the same direction."

Prescott looked at the income column. The mill was weaving at perhaps forty per cent of its former capacity; orders were there, in small parcels — work from a Blackburn merchant, some from a Bradford house — but not in the volumes that would cover the fixed charges. The mortgage interest was being met from the estate's capital reserves: capital in place of income, month by month.

"The mortgage is being met?"

"For the time being. I estimate the reserves will carry it for four more months at the present rate. Possibly five, if the March output holds."

Two hundred and eighteen days to the hearing. The reserves would last four months.

"The sale contract?"

"Still in place. The prospective purchaser has extended twice; his solicitors have indicated — not formally — that they will not extend again." Hadfield folded the income sheet. "The sale requires a grant of representation establishing the vendor's title. Until the action is resolved, that grant cannot issue."

Prescott knew this.

---

The connecting door between the counting house and the mill office stood ajar. While Hadfield went back toward the filing cabinets for a folder he had mislaid, Prescott stepped into the mill office and looked at the room.

A stone floor, like the counting house. Two metal filing cabinets against the far wall. A desk with the grain worn from its surface, a wooden swivel chair behind it, an inkstand without a pen. A window looking south over the yard. And on the desk, alongside the inkstand, a typewriter.

He had confirmed some weeks earlier that there was no machine at the Hall. Josiah had used the mill office for his correspondence — had made the twelve miles when he needed something typed — because there was nothing at the Hall on which a document could be set down in that form.

He did not touch it. He crossed to the desk and looked at the machine from a distance of eighteen inches: the carriage bearing the manufacturer's name on a small plate, the keys worn smooth on their faces, the type slugs visible in the slot. The plate read *Underwood Standard*. The lower-case *e* slug sat a fraction high; the full stop showed wear across its face. He took his pocket-book from his coat and wrote: *Mill office — Underwood Standard — e-slug high — full-stop worn — only machine in building — nothing at the Hall.*

Hadfield came back through the door. Prescott put the pocket-book away.

---

"I should like to know when Mr. Josiah Eccleston last visited the mill," Prescott said. "Whether there's a record of it."

Hadfield sent for the foreman.

Naylor was a wide, deliberate man who came in carrying the mill's daybook — a working log kept in the counting house, bound in green boards, entries in a single running hand. He set it on the table and found the page without being asked.

"The last time the old gentleman visited." He put his finger on the line.

*Mr. Eccleston visited, inspected the shed, attended the office; May 14, 1926.*

The entry sat between a 10 May note about a broken loom rod and a 16 May record of a delivery from the yarn merchant. The ink was continuous with the entries before and after it; the handwriting was the same throughout. A working notation made by a man with no reason to record anything beyond what occurred.

"He didn't visit after May?"

"He was ill," Naylor said. "He didn't travel much after summer. We had word he'd died in October, through Mr. Eccleston." He had the measured habit of a man who had specified, for fifteen years, which Mr. Eccleston he meant. "Mr. Walter Eccleston."

Prescott wrote in his pocket-book: *14 May 1926 — last confirmed visit — mill daybook, foreman's hand.*

The second codicil was dated the fourteenth of August 1926. Three months after the old man had last been in this building.

He did not write that second thought.

---

He was crossing back through the yard toward the gate when Walter Eccleston came out of the engine-room door on the far side.

Walter was heavier than Prescott had remembered from their meetings in Preston — a working man's build by now, not an office man's — in a flat cap and the working coat of a mill manager: the coat for the shed floor, not the office. He was speaking to a young mechanic as he crossed the yard, something brief and technical, and the mechanic nodded without looking up from his work. Walter did not look toward the counting house. He had not seen Prescott.

He moved through the yard with the ease of a man who understood every building in it — which level to walk, which door to take, where the ground would be slick near the engine room. Fifteen years of managing it; everything he had borrowed against was in these buildings, and his men were inside them. The mechanic said something back; Walter stopped and they both crouched over the same problem together. Then Walter stood and walked on, and the mechanic went back to his tools.

Prescott went out through the gate.

---

The road back to Preston ran west along the valley floor, the mill behind and above him on the hillside. He had the window open a fraction — enough to hear the valley — and above the road, faint through the wind noise, the looms had started their afternoon shift. A sustained, rolling sound: the weaving shed in motion, forty men. Prescott had the pocket-book on the seat beside him and did not open it. There was time for the office.

The road fell toward the Ribble crossing, the river still high, the light fading in the way it did in March before it had learned to last. The mill had gone behind a fold in the hillside. He drove west.
