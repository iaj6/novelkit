# Pitch

Arthur Prescott, thirty-eight, is the junior partner at Garside & Prescott, a Preston solicitors' firm
that has kept Josiah Eccleston's will and first codicil in its strong-room since the day they were
executed. When Josiah dies in October 1926 and Walter Eccleston's solicitors produce a second codicil —
dated August 1926, revoking the first — the document looks genuine enough. The paper is plausible, the
signature is good enough to divide the handwriting experts, and both attesting witnesses are beyond
examination: one dead of influenza, one sailed to Canada without an address. If the second codicil
stands, everything — the Hall, the mill shares, the money — goes to Walter. If it falls, the estate
goes to Margaret Fairhurst's two minor children, nineteen and sixteen, living with their schoolmaster
father on Bank Parade, Preston.

When old Mr. Garside dies at his desk that December, the action, the firm, and sixteen years of the
old man's ledger discipline pass to Prescott at once. The schoolmaster cannot fund a probate action.
Garside & Prescott carries it at its own risk, costs recoverable only on winning. Two calendars run
against Prescott: the hearing date, and his own accounts.

Prescott is a probate man, not a detective. He has the strong-room register in Garside's hand, every
instrument Josiah Eccleston executed in the last thirty years, and the operating faith that the file
does not forget. The second codicil is a forgery. The proof is not the signature. The proof is nine
words — or, more precisely, the difference between one.
