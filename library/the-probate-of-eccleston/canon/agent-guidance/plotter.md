# Plotter guidance — The Probate of Eccleston

## POV count and rotation

Single POV throughout: Arthur Prescott, third person limited, past tense. There is no rotation and
no exception. All 18–24 chapters belong to Prescott. Off-page events (Walter's finances, the mill's
condition between visits, the children's circumstances) reach the plot only through period-true
channels — correspondence, attendance notes, depositions, third-party testimony in the room. The
plotter must design the chapter sequence so that every door closure can be witnessed or received
by Prescott.

## Chapter-shape preferences

The dominant shapes are:

- **Single-scene document examination** — Prescott reads or receives a document; its implications
  are worked out within the chapter; the chapter's door is closed. Most chapters in Movement II.
- **Witness interview** — Prescott meets a witness (or reads a deposition); testimony fixes a
  fact. One or two chapters.
- **Correspondence chapter** — a letter received and its consequences managed. Efficient for
  calendar-gate beats and for introducing Walter through his solicitors.
- **Site visit** — the mill, Bank Parade, Eccleston Hall (once each, deliberately placed). These
  are the displacement chapters and the stake chapters.

Intercut chapters (Prescott managing two parallel developments in one chapter) are rare — use at
most twice, and only when the calendar is most constrained. Pure interiority chapters do not exist:
Prescott is always doing something, even when what he is doing is re-reading.

## The compounding mechanism — strict enforcement

This is the anti-sag spine of the book. Every chapter's outline row must name:

1. **Which door it closes** (select from the nine enumerated in `canon/continuity.md` item 26):
   - (a) the machine
   - (b) Josiah's presence
   - (c) the witnesses
   - (d) the profession (no solicitor anywhere took instructions for a revocation)
   - (e) the firm (no request for the codicil's text ever reached Garside & Prescott)
   - (f) the habit (every instrument of Josiah's adult life was drawn by the firm)
   - (g) the despatch-box (inventoried with no codicil; six days at the mill)
   - (h) the September reading (what the register records; what was read aloud; who was present)
   - (i) the date asymmetry (how Walter had the date but not the text; R5 as the source)

2. **The new record item** — the document produced, the deposition sworn, the custody fact fixed
   — that closes the door. The record item is the vehicle of the foreclosure, not a substitute for
   it. A chapter that produces evidence but forecloses nothing is not earning its place.

3. **The prior chapter's fact it tightens** — the compounding step. This chapter's closure must
   reference an established fact from an earlier chapter. The ladder must be visible.

4. **Days remaining to the hearing** — monotonically decreasing across the manuscript. Must not
   always appear at the same structural position in the chapter. Appears in every chapter. The
   refused commission is the one occasion the calendar moves against Prescott (days lost, not
   gained). At least one chapter should show the calendar costing Prescott in real time.

A door once closed must not be re-opened or re-walked in a later chapter. The record is permanent.

## The three movements

**Movement I — first quarter (4–6 chapters, ~10,000–14,000 words):**
The two codicils surface; the caveat is entered; Garside dies; the action and firm pass to Prescott.
Chapters here establish: the office and the strong-room culture; Josiah's death and the estate;
the first codicil (R2 must appear in full at least once, establishing the fair-play plant for the
reader — do this early, before Walter's first spoken misquotation); the firm's financial exposure;
Walter, glimpsed through the caveat meeting (one attendance note, his one spoken misquotation to
Prescott); Garside's death as the structural hinge. Doors closed in Movement I: (g) the despatch-
box (initial inventory); (h) the September reading (the strong-room register entry surfaced early).

**Movement II — middle half (8–12 chapters, ~22,000–28,000 words):**
The building of the record, foreclosure by foreclosure. The nine doors are distributed here (some
take two chapters for the full closure). Key beats:
- The negative canvass of the profession and the Gazette advertisement: (d)
- The firm-records check — no request for the codicil's text: (e)
- Josiah's correspondence and diary: (f) and (b)
- The mill visit: Prescott sees the typewriter (begins (a)); the mill office; short time. This
  chapter closes (b) (Josiah's last confirmed visit to the mill was May 1926).
- The typewriter identification report: closes (a) (machine in the mill office, not the Hall or
  any solicitors').
- The witnesses thread: (c) — the carter's death confirmed; the commission application for the
  gardener; the refusal (calendar-against beat, near the end of Movement II).
- R4 as financial exhibit: printed in full, unremarked, in the chapter covering Walter's mortgage
  and the sale contract. Its language ("and her issue") is not commented on.
- Walter to Nora and George at Bank Parade: third pinned misquotation; the Bank Parade chapter
  is one of two human-stake chapters (alongside the first Bank Parade visit with Edgar).
- The administrator pendente lite's first monthly figures: the mill bleeding in the ledger.
- The Garside displacement thread: Prescott keeps finding the file built more precisely than
  occasion required. This accumulates across three or four chapters without announcement.

**Movement III — final quarter (4–6 chapters, ~12,000–18,000 words):**
The discovery; the corroboration; the hearing; the aftermath.
- **Discovery chapter:** Late, alone, Prescott re-reads the propounded codicil against the strong-
  room original — file discipline, not intuition. He sees the ninth word. The chapter rests on K1
  (the R2/R3 comparison) plus R4 already in the record. Nothing material enters here that the
  reader has not seen.
- **Corroboration chapter:** the recital-variance closes (i) — the date was in Walter's hands in
  writing from R5; the text was not. Walter's misquotation is habitual (R4, the deposition of the
  bank manager or wife), written, and dated fourteen months before the codicil. The proof chain
  stated honestly: variance + closed doors = Walter as the only remaining candidate.
- **Hearing chapter:** Walter is handed the original first codicil; reads "or" for the first time;
  counsel takes instructions; action abandoned. Prescott's register is procedural, almost
  apologetic. The two handwriting experts remain split; this is confirmed at the hearing, not
  resolved.
- **Final chapter:** What the outcome costs and buys — the children, Walter, the mill, the firm.
  Criminal consequence begins and is stated plainly, not dramatized. Every opened account closed.

## Ending-mode distribution

For a 20-chapter manuscript (adjust proportionally for other lengths):
- 6 resolved-beats (Movement II door-closures)
- 4 coda (quiet displacement chapters: the office at night, the register, the mill visit end, the
  final chapter)
- 4 turn (Garside's death; refused commission; discovery; hearing)
- 3 mid-action (correspondence received at a bad moment; a proceeding broken off)
- 3 declarative-close (plain statement of what has been established)

No run of more than two chapters in the same ending mode. The coda endings cluster around the human-
stake chapters (Bank Parade, mill visit, Garside displacement). The turn endings mark the four
structural pivots.

## Chapter target words

- Standard chapter: 2,000–3,000 words
- Document-heavy chapters (two documents quoted in full): 1,500–2,000 words
- Major set pieces (discovery, hearing): up to 3,500–4,000 words
- Final chapter (aftermath): 1,500–2,500 words
- Total target: **45,000–55,000 words**; ceiling 70,000; the shorter book is the better book if
  every door is closed and every thread is settled

## Fair-play architecture constraints

**The three Walter misquotation occasions (pinned, non-negotiable):**
1. R4 printed in full as a financial exhibit — in a Movement II chapter on Walter's mortgage and
   sale contract; unremarked by the narration
2. Walter to Prescott, at the early caveat meeting in Movement I — mid-sentence, not the topic of
   the sentence
3. Walter, kindly, to Nora and George at Bank Parade — in a Movement II human-stake chapter

No additional occasions. The plotter must ensure these three are spread across the manuscript (not
consecutive chapters) and that each is doing other work in its scene.

**The reader's fair-play access:**
R2 must appear in full before Walter's first spoken misquotation. The reader must have seen "or her
issue" before they hear "and her issue" from Walter's mouth. Place R2's full first printing in the
early chapters of Movement I.

**Nothing new after three-quarters:** No document, witness, or fact that materially advances the
proof may be introduced after the three-quarter mark. The discovery re-reads what already exists.
The plotter must ensure all nine doors have at least begun to close by the end of Movement II.

## Genre-specific structural conventions

This is Golden Age fair-play detection in a literary register, not a thriller. The structural
conventions:

- **Patient accumulation** over dramatic revelation — the book's tension is procedural, not
  physical; the reader follows the record's growth rather than a chase
- **Calendar pressure** as the structural counterweight to the record's growth — the hearing date is
  fixed and approaching; it must feel like a genuine constraint, not a backdrop
- **The clean ending is the contract** — fully resolved; the forgery proven on the page; Walter's
  authorship established; the estate disposed of; criminal consequence stated. The editor and reader
  passes must not re-open it.
- **One licensed irresolution:** the handwriting experts split and remain split. This is not a
  defect; it is part of the book's argument about the limits of expert evidence. Do not resolve it
  in the outline.
