# World

## Period and place

Lancashire, England, October 1926 through the autumn of 1927. Two principal locations: **Preston**,
county town and legal centre of Lancashire; and **Ribchester**, a weaving village twelve miles east on
the Ribble valley.

## Preston

The firm of Garside & Prescott occupies a town house on **Winckley Square** — Preston's Georgian legal
quarter, fifty yards from Fishergate, the main commercial street. Three or four storeys of brick, the
square's central garden behind iron railings, the foot-traffic of solicitors and barristers on the
pavement at quarter-to-ten each morning. The firm's strong-room is in the basement: fireproof, with a
register in Garside's hand going back twenty years. The deed-box for the Eccleston family — black
painted steel, "ECCLESTON" in white on the lid — sits on the second shelf.

The Principal Probate Registry is in London; contentious probate matters are argued before the
Probate, Divorce and Admiralty Division of the High Court. Documents travel by post. Counsel appears
in London; Prescott manages evidence and correspondence from Preston.

**Bank Parade** is a terrace of Grade II early-Victorian town houses in the Avenham district —
c.1830–1840, stone and brick, running above the River Ribble between the public parks. Middle-class
and quiet: a respectable address for a schoolmaster. A ten-minute walk from Winckley Square. Edgar
Fairhurst, husband of the late Margaret, lives here with Nora (nineteen) and George (sixteen).

## Ribchester and Vale Mill

Ribchester sits on the south bank of the Ribble, twelve miles from Preston — a small weaving village
(fewer than two thousand souls) dependent on its mills. **Vale Mill** stands above the village on the
hillside, a stone weaving mill working short time in October 1926: perhaps forty men where it once ran
eighty. The mill office occupies a stone-floored room at the ground level, with a table, two filing
cabinets, and a typewriter. **Vale Mill House** — Walter Eccleston's residence — stands thirty yards
from the mill gate, solid Victorian stone, a kitchen garden behind.

**Eccleston Hall** is half a mile up the valley from the mill: moderate in size, not a great estate,
with grounds sufficient for a man who is not quite a squire. Josiah managed both the Hall and the mill
for sixty years and died in the Hall's east bedroom in October 1926, aged eighty-one. There is no
typewriter at the Hall; Josiah used the mill office for correspondence.

## Physical culture of documents

Wills and codicils are engrossed on good rag paper, typed on an office machine, executed in black ink.
The strong-room register records every deposit and withdrawal in a bound ledger — a column for the
date, one for the document described, one for the clerk's signature. Outgoing correspondence is
carbon-copied into the letter-book. A deed box inventoried after a death shows exactly what was in it,
item by item, because the solicitor present writes the list as the box is opened.

By the 1920s the decline of handwritten engrossment in favour of typed instruments is complete for
most provincial firms. Each typewriter's face has a manufacturer's signature; each individual machine,
through wear, has its own. A trained document examiner in 1926 can place a document to its maker and,
given sufficient comparison material, to its machine. What he cannot establish is who was at the keys.

## Economic texture

The Lancashire cotton industry is in its long decline. Mills recapitalized in the 1919 boom — floated
at valuations the trade can no longer sustain — now carry mortgage debt against assets nobody will
buy. Short time is the industry's normal state: the looms run three or four days a week, wages are
partial, the bank correspondence accumulates on the mill manager's desk beside the orders that did not
arrive. In October 1926, a mill sale conditioned on completion of the estate is the only thing between
Walter Eccleston and his creditors. The Bank of England's Lancashire Cotton Corporation, which will
try to rationalize the industry, does not exist until 1929. There is nowhere to turn.

## Sensory register

October rain on Winckley Square cobbles. The smell of the strong-room: clean paper, a faint mineral
from the iron shelves, the slight damp of flagstone floors. A coal fire in Prescott's office that runs
from October to March. The mill working short time has a particular sound: the looms do not run the
full day, and the silence between the morning and afternoon shifts is longer than it should be.
Preston in December, the light gone by four. The Ribble liable to flooding in winter.
