# Themes

## The record against memory

The book's whole engine is the divergence between text and memory — and the principle that memory,
however sincere, is not evidence. Josiah told Walter what he had done; Walter carried those words for
two years, wrote them to his bank, said them to his wife, told his dead cousin's children what their
mother was to have had. He was not lying about what he remembered. What he remembered was wrong by
one word, because he had never read the executed text, and the spoken sentence Josiah gave him was
not the same as the instrument Garside drew.

The same divergence runs through the handwriting experts: two honest men, looking at the same
signature, arriving at opposite conclusions. The book does not sneer at them. The limit of honest
memory and honest perception is not a villain's alibi — it is the condition under which all of them
operate. The file does not forget because the file is not human.

## Procedure as justice

Prescott is not a detective. He has no authority to arrest, no dramatic confrontation to stage, no
genius for intuitive leaps. He can build a record. A record, if it is thorough enough, constrains
what is possible — forecloses the innocent explanations, one by one, until what remains must be what
happened. The compounding mechanism is this theme enacted structurally: the calendar shrinks, the
record grows, and the growing record is justice advancing at the pace justice advances when it is
done properly rather than narratively. The book trusts that this is sufficient — and asks the reader
to trust it.

## The cost of expectation

Walter's crime is not greed in the simple sense. He was owed the mill. He had carried it for fifteen
years; he had been told it was his; he had borrowed against it in good faith; the debt had compounded
while Josiah's sentimentality ran its course. The first codicil was, in Walter's reading, a temporary
aberration — Josiah would put it right; Josiah said so; and then Josiah died without doing so. The
forgery is the act of a man who had waited as long as he could wait and decided to close the account
himself. The book will not let him be simply wrong; it insists on the logic of his position. What it
refuses to permit is the conclusion that the logic made him right. The quiet corruption the book
traces — from the spring of 1924 to the autumn of 1926 — is the compound interest on believing
oneself owed something that belongs to someone else.

## Grief converted to procedure

Prescott cannot grieve for Garside directly; it is not the register of the man or the book. What he
can do is find the file better than it needed to be. Find the strong-room register more precise than
the occasion required. Find, at the end, that Garside's habit of exactitude — a professional habit,
unremarkable for forty years — is what convicts the forger. The old man is everywhere in the record,
and the record is what wins.

Prescott's grief is therefore useful; it is the fuel of the method. The discovery is not a detective's
triumph — it is a clerk's reflex, a check run late at night that turns out to matter. The procedure
Garside taught him is what Prescott enacts in Garside's place. That this is also cold is true. The
book leaves both propositions on the table.
