# Chapter Five — The Hinge

The court had risen early. Prescott came back through Winckley Square at ten minutes past eleven, briefcase in hand, and found the outer door unlocked.

Mr. Booth was standing in the passage.

In sixteen years Prescott had never seen Mr. Booth standing in the passage. The managing clerk occupied either his desk in the front room or the corridor between the deed-room and the letterpress.

He said: "Mr. Garside, sir. About half past nine. He was at his desk when it happened — the notes are open."

They were. Garside had been annotating the margin of a client letter: the pen lay on the page, still uncapped, the ink dried in the nib before anyone had thought to close it. The annotation was three words and not finished. Dr. Ainsworth came at ten o'clock and confirmed what was evident before he arrived.

The arrangements took the rest of the morning. The doctor's certificate; a brief attendance from the coroner's office, who found nothing requiring them further; a message to Garside's chambers contact in Manchester; a telegram to Garside's sister in Harrogate, who was his only surviving family. Mr. Booth handled most of it with the quiet efficiency of twenty years' managing. Prescott signed one letter at Garside's desk that could not wait and returned to his own. The chair had not yet been vacated; but it would have to be used.

He was thirty-eight. He had worked beside this man for sixteen years.

By the afternoon the office was working again, because it had to. There were three matters due before the week's end.

---

The clients were written to, each file noted with the date and the name of the partner now handling it. Two long-running matters were referred to larger firms better placed to carry them. The other matters remained.

Mr. Booth's habits did not change. He put the post on Prescott's desk each morning and collected the outgoing letters at noon and at four, and continued to treat the telephone as an instrument of occasional and controlled utility. The firm was Prescott and Booth now. Neither of them used those words.

The Eccleston file sat on the corner of Prescott's desk through those first days: the caveat entered, the administrator pendente lite still to be applied for, the action assigned to the Probate Division and waiting for a date. Prescott attended to more urgent matters first. On the fourth evening, when the office was quiet and the street outside had gone dark, he put everything else aside and opened the file from the beginning.

---

He went through the will, the first codicil, the covering correspondence, the register index sheet, the attendance notes, the letter-book carbon, Josiah's letter to Margaret Fairhurst, the working note Prescott had written in November recording forty years of instruments and the silence of the correspondence thereafter. He read each item and set it face-down to the right of the blotter. The coal in the grate had burned low; he put three pieces on before sitting down again with the strong-room register.

He had consulted the register in October — glanced at the September entry to confirm what he and Garside had both been present to witness. He read the page now in full.

The register was a tall narrow ledger, twenty years of entries in two hands: Garside's copper-plate filling the greater part of it, with Prescott's narrower script appearing in the same columns for the matters he had handled in the latter years. The overall character of the book was Garside's.

He found the September entry.

---

*19 September 1926. Document: First Codicil, J. Eccleston dec., dated 15 March 1924. Purpose: Read to assembled family at testator's request; testator's health declining; testator wished there to be no surprises. Persons present: H. Garside; A. Prescott; W. Eccleston; Mr. E. Beckett (steward); one other of the household. Instrument read entire, including date. Time out: 9.45 a.m. Time returned: 3.20 p.m. Document in good order on return. H.G.*

---

He did not move for some time.

The entry was consistent with every other entry in the book: the same instrument named in full, the same purpose given, the same persons noted, the same times. *Instrument read entire, including date.* That phrase was not in any requirement of law or practice. It was Garside's.

He turned back through the pages. The same character for twenty years. A box of deeds fetched for a completion in 1918. The will read to Josiah at the Hall in 1919. The mortgage papers in 1921. Every entry at the same level — not the level a register strictly required, but the level of a man who did not distinguish between what required a record and what deserved one.

He closed the register and set it back on its shelf. He sat with the open file for a few minutes longer. The coal was nearly spent.

---

The letter from the Principal Probate Registry had come that morning; he had set it aside for the afternoon and not reached it. He opened it now.

The action had been set down for hearing in the Probate Division. The date fixed: the sixth of October 1927.

He counted forward. He was in December; it was the twentieth. He counted to October.

Two hundred and ninety days.

He banked the fire, locked the strong-room, and put on his coat. He walked home through Winckley Square in the cold of December. Two hundred and ninety days.
