# Chapter 1 — The Strong-Room

The deed box was on the table when Prescott arrived: black painted steel, "ECCLESTON" lettered in white on the lid, the label in Garside's copper-plate facing up across the room. Garside was at the far end of the table with his hat still on, which meant he had not been in from the strong-room long.

"Dead yesterday evening," Garside said. "The Hall sent a telegram."

Josiah Eccleston had been a client of the firm since before Prescott came into articles. Cotton spinner, magistrate, owner of the Hall at Ribchester and the Vale Mill above the village — a man whose every instrument from will to conveyance had come through this office in the forty years since Garside first took his instructions. Prescott had known him first as a name in the ledger, and later, from the will itself onwards, as a man who shook hands as though a contract were meant by it. He had been eighty-one years old. He had died, as far as anyone knew without disorder, on a Tuesday evening in October, at the Hall.

The coal fire was going. Outside on the square the October light was doing what it did.

Garside set his hat down and opened the deed box.

The will was first: folded twice on cream paper, "WILL — J. ECCLESTON — 3 JUNE 1919" on the outside. Garside opened it, turned to the fourth clause, and read:

> I GIVE DEVISE AND BEQUEATH all the residue of my estate both real and personal whatsoever and wheresoever unto my nephew Walter Eccleston of Vale Mill House Ribchester absolutely and I appoint the said Walter Eccleston sole executor of this my will.

Prescott noted the date and the name and set his pen down.

Garside folded the will and reached into the box. The codicil was underneath: same cream paper, "CODICIL — J. ECCLESTON — 15 MARCH 1924" on the outside fold, shorter by half. He opened it.

"After the reconciliation," he said, more to himself than to Prescott. He read:

> I REVOKE clause 4 of my said will and IN PLACE THEREOF I GIVE DEVISE AND BEQUEATH all the residue of my estate both real and personal whatsoever and wheresoever unto my niece Margaret Fairhurst of Bank Parade Preston or her issue absolutely AND IN ALL OTHER RESPECTS I confirm my said will.

The coal shifted behind the grate. Prescott wrote down the date — fifteen March nineteen-twenty-four — and the name and address, and waited.

"Clause four revoked entire," Garside said. "Gift and executorship both — he intended a complete revision. There is, at present, no executor." He set the codicil alongside the will. "Section thirty-three of the Wills Act does nothing for a niece against lapse. She is collateral, not a descendant. He used the substitutional form accordingly."

Margaret Fairhurst. Prescott had known her name from the codicil's making two years before, and had noted the memorandum in the file when she died: May 1925, a year and a half before Josiah. Her issue were her two children — Nora, nineteen, and George, sixteen — living with their father at the Bank Parade address named in the instrument. Edgar Fairhurst. A schoolmaster.

"Both children are minors," Prescott said. "Majority is twenty-one."

"Letters of administration with the will annexed," Garside said. "To the father as guardian, if the instruments stand."

Walter Eccleston had managed the mill for fifteen years on the expectation of this estate. Prescott knew the mill was mortgaged — the kind of information that accumulated in a solicitor's knowledge without being entered in any single place.

He took up the strong-room register from beside the box.

The register was a cloth-bound ledger: date column on the left, document described in the centre, clerk's signature at the right margin. Garside had maintained it in his own hand since 1906; Prescott had added entries as occasion required, in his own narrower hand, the two scripts sharing the same columns for two decades. He opened to the current year and ran down the September entries.

September the nineteenth. The entry was Garside's:

*19 September 1926. Document: First Codicil, J. Eccleston dec., dated 15 March 1924. Purpose: Read to assembled family at testator's request; testator's health declining; testator wished there to be no surprises. Persons present: H. Garside; A. Prescott; W. Eccleston; Mr. E. Beckett (steward); one other of the household. Instrument read entire, including date. Time out: 9.45 a.m. Time returned: 3.20 p.m. Document in good order on return. H.G.*

Prescott had been at that reading. He had carried the deed-box into the drawing room at the Hall and stood there while Garside read the codicil from first word to last — date and all — to Josiah, who was in his chair and had been failing since August. Walter had been there, and the two women from the household, and Josiah's man beside the door. Josiah had wanted no surprises.

Walter Eccleston had stood beside the fireplace with his arms at his sides, listening.

Prescott turned to the working correspondence at the back of the file. The papers from the previous week were loosely arranged: the telegram from the Hall, a draft letter of condolence, and behind them his own attendance note from the despatch-box inventory.

He had made the note at the Hall table on the afternoon of the inventory, while Walter stood by. The box had been opened on the dining-room table and Prescott had listed each item as it was removed: three insurance policies, title deeds to the Hall, unsigned drafts of two rental agreements, a letter from the bank dated 1923, three letters tied in blue ribbon, an unsigned copy of the will.

No codicil.

He read through the list a second time and found the same. He clipped the note to the front of the file.

Garside restored the will and the codicil to the deed box and carried it back to the strong-room himself — he always carried the Eccleston box himself — and Prescott heard the iron door of the vault close below, and the latch engage. A minute later Garside came back up without the box and stood in the doorway.

"Two instruments," Prescott said. "The will and the first codicil. The estate belongs to the Fairhurst children if the instruments stand. There is no executor, and there was no second codicil in the despatch-box." He looked at his notes. "I should write to Thurston and Marsh today, about proving the will in proper form. And we must write to Bank Parade."

"Yes," Garside said. He sat down and took up his pen.

The file was in order: the will of the third of June nineteen-nineteen, the codicil of the fifteenth of March nineteen-twenty-four, the strong-room register exact about both. What Walter Eccleston would make of those terms was a question the record had not yet been required to answer.
