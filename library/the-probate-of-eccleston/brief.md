## Premise

Josiah Eccleston — cotton spinner, magistrate, owner of Eccleston Hall and the Vale Mill above
Ribchester in Lancashire — dies in October 1926, at eighty-one, leaving one will and, it appears, two
codicils.

The will, made in 1919, leaves the residue of his estate — the Hall, the mill shares, the money — to
his nephew **Walter**, who has managed the mill for fifteen years and expects to inherit it. The
first codicil, made in March 1924 after Josiah's late reconciliation with his estranged niece
**Margaret Fairhurst**, takes both the residue and the executorship away from Walter and gives the
residue to Margaret *or her issue*. Margaret died in May 1925. Her issue are two children, nineteen
and sixteen, living with their father — a Preston schoolmaster — at Bank Parade. Under the 1924
codicil, the estate is theirs.

Three weeks after the funeral, Walter's solicitors produce a second codicil, dated August 1926, in
which Josiah revokes the first codicil and confirms his will. If it is genuine, everything goes back
to Walter. It looks genuine. The paper is plausible, the signature is so good that the handwriting
men will end the book divided about it, and the two attesting witnesses cannot be cross-examined —
one is dead, and one has gone to Canada.

**Arthur Prescott**, thirty-eight, is the junior partner at Garside & Prescott of Preston, the firm
that drew the will and the first codicil and has kept both in its strong-room since the day they were
executed. Prescott is a probate man, not a detective: a man whose working faith — learned from old
Mr. Garside across sixteen years — is that the file does not forget. When the second codicil is
propounded, the children's father enters a caveat; when Garside dies at his desk that December, the
action, the firm, and the old man's strong-room discipline all pass to Prescott at once. The
schoolmaster cannot fund a probate action. The firm carries it at its own risk, costs recoverable
only on winning, and the firm is small. Two calendars run against Prescott all book: the hearing
date, and his own ledger.

The book's answer, fixed and to be delivered in full: **the second codicil is a forgery, and Walter
made it.** The proof is not the signature. The proof is text. The second codicil, like any careful
codicil, recites the instrument it revokes — and its recital quotes the 1924 codicil as giving the
residue to Margaret Fairhurst *"and her issue."* The executed codicil in the strong-room says *"or
her issue."*

The variance proves less than a detective would like and exactly enough for a solicitor: it proves
the recital was not taken from the executed text, nor from anything held at Garside & Prescott. What
convicts Walter is the slow closing of every other door — no solicitor anywhere took Josiah's
instructions; no request for the codicil's text ever reached the firm; the machine that typed it
stood in the mill office Josiah had last entered in May; and the misquotation itself is *Walter's*,
in his own hand, fourteen months before the codicil's date, in a letter to his bank pledging an
expectancy he did not have. Walter has said "and her issue" since 1924 — it is how Josiah himself
described the codicil to him, once, in words, and Walter's memory kept the sense and lost the letter.
He was never in a room with the executed text in his life. He forged around a document he had never
read, and he transcribed his own misquotation into it. The record convicts him because the record is
exact and memory is not.

Motive is period-real and unhidden: the Lancashire cotton trade is in its long slump, the Vale Mill
is mortgaged, Walter has been carrying its debts on the expectation of the residue, and the sale
contract he has signed — completion conditioned on a grant of representation establishing his title —
is the only thing between him and his creditors. He is not a monster; the book should let him be a
man who persuaded himself he was correcting an old man's late sentimentality rather than committing a
felony. He still goes down.

## Length and shape

- **No more than 26 chapters or 70,000 words — and if the record is built and every opened account
  settled at 45–55,000, the shorter book is the better book.** Length is a ceiling, not a target.
  Never drop a briefed thread or beat to come in short.
- Three movements, in proportion (not fixed chapter numbers): the first quarter — the two codicils
  surface, the caveat is entered, Garside dies; the middle half — the building of the record,
  foreclosure by foreclosure, while both calendars tighten; the final quarter — the discovery of the
  recital variance, the corroboration, the hearing, and the settling of every account the book has
  opened.
- Single POV throughout: third person limited, past tense, Arthur Prescott. No other POV chapters.
  Off-page events reach the book only through period-true channels — correspondence, attendance
  notes, depositions, opposing solicitors' letters, what witnesses say in the room — and verbatim
  document inserts may stand as set-off passages within a chapter; they are records, not POV
  breaches. Within the single POV, four braided threads: the action itself; the firm's own ledger
  and survival; Walter's finances closing in, seen only through documents and third parties; and the
  children, who must remain people rather than a cause.
- The ending is CLEAN, and the fully-resolved ending is the contract of the book, not a defect —
  editor and reader passes must not re-complicate it. The forgery is proven on the page; Walter's
  authorship is established; the action collapses; the estate passes under the 1924 codicil (a grant
  of administration with the will annexed — see the executor point below); the criminal consequence
  begins and is stated plainly rather than dramatized. The last chapter shows what the outcome costs
  and buys — the children, Walter, the mill, the firm. No withheld answer, no ambiguity about the
  central question, no final-page unsettling. The one licensed irresolution: the handwriting experts
  are never reconciled.

## Compounding mechanism (optional — the anti-sag engine)

**The file only grows, and the calendar only shrinks.** Two ladders run under every chapter:

1. **Every chapter closes a door.** Each chapter must close or materially narrow one named innocent
   explanation of the second codicil — the new record item (document produced, deposition sworn,
   custody fact fixed) is the *vehicle* of that foreclosure, never a substitute for it. A fact once
   sworn cannot be unsworn; a foreclosure once made must never be re-walked. Each chapter's plan
   names which door it closes and which prior chapter's fact it tightens. A date pinned or a
   deposition taken that closes nothing is not a chapter. The doors, enumerated (the plotter
   distributes them; each is one or more chapters):
   - the machine: the second codicil was typed on the mill-office machine — not at the Hall (which
     has none), not at any solicitor's office;
   - Josiah's presence: his last visit to the mill office was May 1926; after that he did not leave
     the Hall unaccompanied — so the codicil was typed where he wasn't;
   - the witnesses: what the carter's genuine signatures on earlier papers show; the hunt for the
     gardener in Canada, and the refusal of the commission to examine him (the calendar-against-
     Prescott beat);
   - the profession: no solicitor in the county took instructions for any revocation — the negative
     canvass and the Gazette advertisement;
   - the firm: no request for the codicil's text, or so much as its date, ever reached Garside &
     Prescott from anyone;
   - the habit: every instrument of Josiah's adult life was drawn by the firm; his diary and
     correspondence are silent on any change of mind;
   - the despatch-box: inventoried a week after the death with no codicil in it; six days at the
     mill in Walter's keeping before the codicil is produced;
   - the September reading: the strong-room register shows the codicil fetched and returned the same
     day; who was present; what precisely was read aloud — including the date;
   - the date asymmetry: where Walter could have had "the fifteenth day of March 1924" without ever
     having the text (he had it in writing — see R5).
2. **The hearing date gates an irreversible act.** The action to prove the second codicil in solemn
   form has a fixed date, and Walter's mill sale completes on the grant. If Prescott's side is not
   ready, the estate is distributed and cannot be clawed back whole. The days remaining must be
   named in every chapter — fed from the record, not improvised, so the countdown is monotonic — but
   vary where in the chapter the calendar lands; it must not close every chapter like a metronome.
   At least once the calendar moves against Prescott (the refused commission above).

## Voice

Dry, precise, procedural, and quietly warm — a book that loves documents the way its narrator does.
The prose register is 1920s English professional class: exact about instruments, dates, and money;
restrained about feeling; capable of landing weight in a plain sentence. Wit is permitted in the
Sayers vein — observational, never cute — and it lives in Prescott's narrated noticing only: no
banter partner, no Wimsey-adjacent wit character, no repartee. Interiority is Prescott noticing and
deciding, not ruminating. When a document speaks, the book quotes it exactly and the prose gets out
of the way. For the quiet chapters, feeling displaces onto things, not rumination: the office and
its managing clerk, the strong-room register in Garside's hand, Preston streets in weather, one
visit to the mill working short time, one visit to Bank Parade. The dead senior partner is the
book's standing displacement object — Prescott keeps finding the file is better than it needed to
be, and every such discovery is grief converted to procedure.

## Audience and register

Adult. Golden-age fair-play detection played straight in a literary register — Sayers's *Unnatural
Death* and Crofts's patience, not pastiche. The reader is invited to solve it and given everything:
every fact necessary to the solution is on the page before Prescott assembles it. The two forms of
the clause — *or her issue* in the executed codicil, *and her issue* in Walter's mouth, in his bank
letter, and in the second codicil's recital — are each printed verbatim, in plain view, before the
discovery, without the narration ever underlining the variance. Fair play is the contract; the
withheld-answer reflex is off.

**Quotation ceiling:** printing a canonical text in full is an event, not a texture — R1, R2, and R3
each appear in full at most two or three times before the discovery and once at it; thereafter they
are referred to by name or by fragment. The book must never re-quote a document as filler.

## The documents (canonical verbatim records — fixed to the letter)

The architect registers all five below as verbatim records in the world store, exactly as printed,
before any chapter is drafted, under these record ids: `the-will-clause-4`, `first-codicil`,
`second-codicil`, `walter-bank-letter`, `firm-notice-letter`. Their texts may not be altered,
paraphrased when quoted as documents, or "improved." Entity discipline: the documents live in the
store under entities `the-will`, `first-codicil`, `second-codicil` (plus the letters); no agent may
ever assert Walter's spoken version of the clause as a *fact about* any of these documents — his
spoken words belong to him, not to the record.

**R1 — `the-will-clause-4` (executed 3 June 1919; typed; in the strong-room of Garside & Prescott):**

> I GIVE DEVISE AND BEQUEATH all the residue of my estate both real and personal whatsoever and
> wheresoever unto my nephew Walter Eccleston of Vale Mill House Ribchester absolutely and I appoint
> the said Walter Eccleston sole executor of this my will.

**R2 — `first-codicil` (executed 15 March 1924; typed; in the strong-room of Garside & Prescott):**

> I REVOKE clause 4 of my said will and IN PLACE THEREOF I GIVE DEVISE AND BEQUEATH all the residue
> of my estate both real and personal whatsoever and wheresoever unto my niece Margaret Fairhurst of
> Bank Parade Preston or her issue absolutely AND IN ALL OTHER RESPECTS I confirm my said will.

**R3 — `second-codicil` (dated 14 August 1926; typed; produced by Walter's solicitors three weeks
after the funeral):**

> WHEREAS by a codicil to my said will dated the fifteenth day of March 1924 I gave the residue of my
> estate unto my niece Margaret Fairhurst and her issue absolutely NOW I HEREBY REVOKE the said
> codicil in its entirety and I confirm my said will dated the third day of June 1919 in all respects
> as if the said codicil had not been made.

**R4 — `walter-bank-letter` (Walter Eccleston to his bank manager; 11 June 1925; handwritten; enters
the book as an exhibit in the financial-pressure thread, its wording unremarked on until the
discovery):**

> Dear Mr. Holt, — You ask what further security I can offer beyond the mortgage. My uncle has made
> a codicil, as I told you, in favour of my late cousin Mrs. Fairhurst and her issue, but I am
> assured the arrangement will not stand; he made it in a soft mood and has spoken since of putting
> it right. My expectations are otherwise what they have always been. — Yours faithfully, WALTER
> ECCLESTON

**R5 — `firm-notice-letter` (Garside & Prescott to Walter's solicitors; 2 November 1926; typed on
the firm's paper; the letter that gave Walter the codicil's date in writing without ever giving him
its text):**

> Dear Sirs, — We act in the estate of the late Mr. Josiah Eccleston of Eccleston Hall. We hold his
> will dated the 3rd June 1919 and a codicil thereto dated the 15th March 1924 which materially
> affects the disposition of the residue. We shall be glad to receive your client's proposals for
> proving the will in proper form. — Yours faithfully, GARSIDE & PRESCOTT

The variance — R2's *"or her issue"* against R3's recital and R4's *"and her issue"* — is the whole
engine. Walter's spoken misquotations in dialogue are the ONE deliberate exception to record
fidelity: in speech, from memory, Walter always says *"and her issue."* That is characterization,
not record drift, and the book depends on the distinction staying razor-clean.

## The case, pinned (operative — the architect builds canon from these facts, not around them)

- **Origin of the misquote.** In March 1924 Josiah told Walter what he had done, in his own words —
  "I've left it to Margaret and her issue after her; you'll not want for the mill if you earn it" —
  and that spoken sentence, not any document, is the root of Walter's version. Walter has repeated
  "and her issue" since 1924: to his bank manager (R4, in writing, June 1925), to his wife, and once
  to Prescott. He heard the executed text read aloud exactly once — September 1926, at the Hall,
  when Josiah, failing, had Garside fetch the codicil from the strong-room and read it to the
  assembled family so there would be no surprises. The strong-room register records the fetch and
  same-day return in Garside's hand. Garside read the instrument entire, including its date.
  Prescott was present, carrying the deed-box. Walter has never in his life had the text before his
  eyes.
- **No stray copies — canonical and absolute.** No copy, draft, or carbon of the first codicil ever
  existed outside Garside & Prescott: the executed original in the strong-room, the letter-book
  carbon that has never left the office (it becomes a corroborating exhibit), nothing else. Josiah
  kept no copy at the Hall; drafters must not invent one. This is load-bearing: it is why a genuine
  privately-made second codicil could not have recited the text correctly *and* why nobody innocent
  could have recited it wrongly with the date exactly right.
- **Walter's account of the second codicil (his cover story, fixed):** that Josiah must have made it
  privately in August 1926 — typed it himself or had it typed on one of his days at the mill office
  — and laid it in the despatch-box at the Hall, where Walter found it while going through the
  insurance papers after the funeral. The box had been inventoried, with Prescott present, a week
  after the death — no codicil in it then; it then stood six days at the mill in Walter's keeping.
  Walter forged the codicil in those six days, in late October 1926 — after R5 had given him the
  1924 codicil's date in writing — and dated it 14 August.
- **The attesting witnesses, fixed.** Two names appear on the second codicil, both real men who had
  genuinely witnessed papers for Josiah in years past, chosen because neither could ever be asked:
  a carter, who died of the influenza on 28 September 1926, and a jobbing gardener, who took an
  assisted passage to Canada in mid-October 1926 leaving no address. Their genuine signatures on
  earlier estate papers exist for comparison, and the copies on the codicil are good. The defense's
  case on due execution is therefore unbreakable by testimony and unprovable by it: honest-looking
  attestation that nobody living can speak to. The hunt for the gardener, and the refused commission
  to examine him abroad, is the calendar-against-Prescott beat. The witnesses never testify; they do
  not need to be broken; when authorship is shown, their silence becomes part of the design.
- **The proof chain, stated honestly.** The recital variance proves only that the recital was not
  taken from the executed text or from the firm. The foreclosure ladder (Compounding mechanism,
  above) eliminates the innocent drafter — no solicitor, no request to the firm, no habit of private
  drafting, no possibility Josiah typed it where and when it was typed. What remains must explain
  BOTH halves of the asymmetry: wrong on the one word memory would smooth, exact on the date memory
  would blur. Walter is the only candidate on both counts — his misquotation is habitual, written,
  and dated fourteen months before the codicil he "found"; and the date was in his hands in writing
  (R5) from the second of November. The discovery chapter must rest the predating-proof on R4, the
  written artifact, with depositions corroborating habit — never carrying the date alone on memory,
  which is the very faculty the book distrusts.
- **The executor point (intentional, load-bearing — not a drafting error).** The first codicil
  revokes clause 4 *entire* — gift and executorship both. Josiah meant it: the reconciliation took
  from Walter the office as well as the money, and Garside drew it so. Consequently, if the second
  codicil fails there is no executor at all: the grant is letters of administration with the will
  annexed, to the children's father as guardian (both children are minors; majority is twenty-one).
  During the action the estate sits under an administrator pendente lite — a neutral Preston
  accountant — which is how the reader watches the mill bleed in monthly figures nobody can act on.
  Walter's sale contract is conditioned on "a grant of representation establishing the vendor's
  title," which the failure of the second codicil makes impossible. Drafters must not write Walter
  acting as executor, and must not write "probate granted" to the children's side.
- **The discovery, staged.** Late, alone, on the eve of the hearing's final preparation, Prescott
  re-reads the propounded codicil against the strong-room original — file discipline, not intuition
  — and sees the ninth word. The chapter turns on exactly this: K1 plus the R2/R3 comparison, then
  R4 pulled from the exhibits already in the record. Nothing material enters the book after the
  three-quarter mark that the reader has not already seen; the discovery re-reads what exists.
- **The exposure, staged.** At the hearing (or in its immediate face, counsel to counsel), Walter is
  handed the strong-room original of the first codicil and reads the word "or" for the first time in
  his life. That is the hurt: he learns he was undone by never having read the thing he forged
  around. No summation of triumph from Prescott — his register at the exposure is procedural and
  almost apologetic. Walter's counsel takes instructions; the action is abandoned; the rest is
  consequence, delivered plainly.

## Structure (operative)

- **Records first.** The architect registers R1–R5 (ids above) in the world store, texts exactly as
  printed, before any chapter is drafted. Drafters read the record before every re-quotation of any
  document text. The recital variance between R2 and R3 is a fact of the texts themselves — it must
  never be flattened by a well-meaning consistency fix.
- **Binding continuity facts (the architect must write these, numbered, into canon/continuity.md,
  which every audit, editor, and repair pass reads):**
  1. The registered texts of `first-codicil` ("or her issue") and `second-codicil` (recital: "and
     her issue") are BOTH correct as printed. The one-word variance is the book's engine, by design.
     Any finding that they disagree is by-design, and no pass may ever reconcile, "correct," or
     repair either text toward the other.
  2. Walter Eccleston, quoting the codicil in DIALOGUE, always says "and her issue." This is
     deliberate characterization — the plot device itself — and never record drift. No continuity,
     audit, or repair pass may "correct" spoken dialogue toward the registered records. Only
     narrator-framed document quotations must match the record.
  3. Repair passes may never alter words inside the quotation of any canonical document text.
  4. Verbatim fidelity is achieved by prevention (read_record before re-quoting), not detection —
     no pass should expect an audit to catch a drifted in-prose quote, and none should introduce one.
- **Lean-into pattern (architect writes into canon/style.md):** repeated verbatim printing of the
  will and codicil clauses is fair-play planting, not padding or a tic — subject to the quotation
  ceiling above.
- **The seeded knowledge ledger (fixed, not delegated).** Proposition slug, shared by all knowers:
  `first-codicil-operative-words` — "the operative words of the first codicil are 'or her issue'."
  - K1: Walter has never read the executed first codicil (basis: strong-room custody its whole
    life; the single September 1926 hearing-aloud; the no-copies rule).
  - K2: Walter `wrong_believes` the operative words are "and her issue," from Josiah's own spoken
    account of March 1924 — and does not know he is wrong. Stance flips to `knows` only in the
    exposure scene.
  - K3: `@reader` `knows` the true words no later than chapter 3, when R2 is printed entire. From
    then on every Walter misquotation lands as dramatic irony for the reader who is paying
    attention, and as nothing at all for the reader who is not. The narration NEVER flags it before
    the discovery.
  - Prescott: `believes` the second codicil is a forgery from the caveat onward; `knows` — and knows
    he can prove it — only at the discovery, at roughly the three-quarter mark.
  - The architect seeds these in canon (and the ledger, epistemic tools being enabled); drafters
    record the stance beats as they land on the page.
- **Fair-play plants — occasions, not a rhythm.** Walter's misquotation appears on the page at most
  four times before the discovery, never in consecutive chapters, never as the topic of the
  sentence it occurs in, and the narration never repeats or paraphrases his version back. The
  pinned occasions, each doing other work in its scene: (1) R4 quoted entire, once, as a financial
  exhibit in a middle-movement chapter about the mortgage — its wording unremarked; (2) Walter to
  Prescott, in an early attendance about the funeral arrangements and the caveat, mid-sentence;
  (3) the cruel one, found on re-read: Walter, kindly, to Margaret's children, telling them what
  their mother was to have had. Do not add further recitations to reinforce the motif.
- **The handwriting deadlock is a feature.** The signature experts split honestly and stay split;
  the book does not rescue itself with forensics. Text convicts; ink and typewriter evidence only
  narrows provenance (the mill-office machine, the May visit) and never closes it alone.
- **Chapter-map discipline.** Each chapter's outline row names: the door it closes (from the
  enumerated ladder), the new record item that closes it, the prior chapter it tightens, and the
  days remaining to the hearing.

## Constraints (optional)

- No murder. Nobody dies on the page or suspiciously off it; Josiah dies of being eighty-one, the
  carter of the influenza, Garside of his heart at his desk. The crime is paper. The stakes are
  livelihoods, a house, two children's futures, and one man's idea of what he was owed — the book
  must make those feel sufficient, and they are.
- No detective. Prescott is a solicitor doing a solicitor's work; no policeman drives the plot, and
  no character exists to explain things to. Procedure carries the tension a chase would carry in a
  lesser book.
- No last-minute evidence. Nothing material enters the book after the three-quarter mark that the
  reader has not already seen; the discovery re-reads what exists.
- Walter is never POV, but he is a person: competent at the mill, liked by his men, genuinely fond
  of Josiah once. His exposure should hurt — per the staging pinned above.
- Period accuracy is load-bearing but never lectured: legal and historical material may surface
  only at the moment it bears on a decision Prescott is making or a document being read, never as a
  standing paragraph of explanation. The 1925 property legislation resolves to at most one line in
  the whole book.
- Names are plain 1920s Lancashire English throughout (the register pin): no invented-soft or
  literary-liquid names. Names inside the canonical texts (Josiah Eccleston, Walter Eccleston,
  Margaret Fairhurst) are fixed and may not be renamed.

## Research scope (optional — including this section enables the researcher phase)

- English contentious probate practice circa 1926: caveats, probate in solemn form, the Probate
  Division, proof of due execution (Wills Act 1837 s.9), commissions to examine witnesses abroad
  and when they were refused, letters of administration with the will annexed, administrators
  pendente lite, guardianship where legatees are minors, typical timelines — enough to keep
  procedure honest. (Note: the 1925 property legislation came into force January 1926; the will and
  first codicil predate it, the dispute postdates it — verify what that changes and what it
  doesn't, and keep it to a line.)
- Wills and construction: substitutional gifts ("or her issue" against "and her issue"), lapse
  where a legatee predeceases, why the distinction mattered for a niece's gift in 1926 — the
  book's legal spine must be defensible to a reader who knows the law of the period.
- The Lancashire cotton industry's 1920s slump: mill finance, mortgages, short time, what a mill
  sale looked like in 1926; Preston and the Ribble valley as places — solicitors' offices, the
  physical culture of documents (strong-rooms and their registers, engrossment, typewriters in a
  1920s provincial firm and their typeface individuality, carbon copies and letter-books, despatch
  boxes).
- Document examination as practiced circa 1926: what typewriter and paper evidence could and could
  not prove; the era's handwriting experts and their real, documented fallibility.
