# Reader Act 2 Assessment
## Chapters: 08-the-gazette, 09-vale-mill, 10-the-machine, 11-the-sale-contract, 12-the-sale-contract, 12-the-witnesses, 13-the-fairhursts

---

### Overview

Act 2 is, by and large, the compounding mechanism working as the brief intends. Each chapter advances the record; the legal vocabulary is used as furniture, not display; and the prose stays in register without faltering. The act's high point is **09-the-gazette** through **09-vale-mill** — a one-two sequence where the negative canvass closes a door cleanly, and then the mill visit delivers the book's best single chapter so far. The act's central problem is structural and requires attention before synthesis: there are **two draft versions of the sale-contract chapter**, both in the story arc, both containing R4 in full.

---

### What's Working

**09-vale-mill is the act's standout chapter.** The opening memory — Garside driving, Prescott in the passenger seat with the deed-box at his feet, thinking "about the quality of the light, and the draft from the windows, and nothing in particular. He had not been attending to the right thing" — is exactly what the brief calls for in the displacement thread. The sentence does not explain itself; it doesn't need to. The mill visit carries all four threads Prescott is weaving: the Hadfield figures advancing the firm's exposure (Thread 1), the mortgage instrument in Garside's hand on the counting-house table (Thread 2), Walter observed crossing the yard with the ease of a man who had earned his understanding of every building in it (Thread 4), and Josiah's last dated visit — "May 14, 1926" — pulled from the foreman's daybook without ceremony. The final note of the chapter — "He did not write that second thought" — is the register working perfectly. The literary-fade ending (Prescott driving west, the looms audible, the mill behind a fold in the hillside) is one of the act's best sentences.

**10-the-machine handles its technical material without losing the voice.** Croft's eleven-page report could easily become a dry inventory; instead the act of reading it is rendered in Prescott's register throughout. "What it said was what he had known in the mill-office doorway, looking at the machine from a distance of eighteen inches with his pocket-book in his hand; but knowing was not demonstrating, and demonstrating was the eleven pages." That sentence earns its place. The limits of Croft's evidence — "The examination of typewriting specimens does not establish the identity of the operator or the date of production" — are correctly given in set-off rather than paraphrased, and the prose does not explain why those limits are actually useful to Prescott. He understands it; the reader is trusted to follow.

**08-the-gazette is patient procedural work that doesn't drag.** Booth's two-column list, the forty-six firms, the careful phrasing of the notice, the weeks-long wait, the final summary memo placed at the front of the file — all of it moves at exactly the pace a negative canvass should move. "The Gazette produced nothing" as a section heading carries the dry wit the brief licenses, and the closing paragraph — the cover story requiring a solicitor who had vanished from the professional record without a letter-book carbon or a bill rendered — is a clean, satisfying door-closing that earns its last sentence: "The door was locked."

**13-the-fairhursts lands all three of its required beats.** Thread 3 (the children as people) is renewed at the case's midpoint through George's question — "What happens to the mill?" and "The men lose their jobs" — which is the best Thread 3 beat in the book so far: a sixteen-year-old asking the question his father didn't, and settling for the answer in silence. The third fair-play misquotation is planted correctly via Nora's report of Walter's visit: "grandfather had made a codicil giving everything to Mother and her issue, which meant us." The narration doesn't flag the "and"; the chapter doesn't remark on the word; it moves on to the signed letter. And Nora's portrait of her mother — "She was not a person who liked to be the reason for anything" — followed by Prescott's "That is not unusual, which was true, and insufficient," is the act's most precise moment of the displacement register working at its best.

**The Garside thread accumulates correctly through the act.** The instrument index in Garside's hand in 12-the-witnesses ("red boards worn soft at the corners, in Garside's hand from its first entry in 1906"). The mortgage instrument at the counting-house table in 09-vale-mill, "Garside's signature visible as the witnessing solicitor: small, exact, done for the record. Prescott had seen it ten thousand times at least." These are exactly the kind of specific professional discoveries the brief requires — more precise than the occasion required, accumulating without announcement.

### The Structural Problem: Duplicate Sale-Contract Chapter

The act contains two drafts of the same chapter. **11-the-sale-contract.md** and **12-the-sale-contract.md** cover identical ground: the £8,500 mortgage in Garside's drafting, the conditional sale contract and its two extension memoranda, Walter's nine letters to Holt, R4 (the June 1925 bank letter) quoted verbatim, and Hadfield's March account at a loss of approximately £300, with three months of reserves remaining. Both chapters end with 185 days to the hearing. Both appear in the story-arc digest with near-identical one-line summaries. The longer version ("12-the-sale-contract") is more thoroughly rendered — the mortgage reading is fuller, the banking correspondence more developed — but they describe the same professional act on the same day.

**This is a structural failure that must be resolved before synthesis.** It creates two consequences. First: R4 appears verbatim twice in the manuscript — once in the "11" version, once in the "12" version. The fair-play brief pins the second plant of the misquotation as R4 quoted once as a financial exhibit, unremarked. Two full quotations of R4 do not technically over-signal the variance (the narration remarks on neither), but the duplicate makes the intent ambiguous and could shade toward over-planting for a careful reader. Second: the chapter numbering is now broken — "12-the-sale-contract.md" and "12-the-witnesses.md" share the same number, and the story arc has logged both "12" chapters as well as a duplicate entry for "12-the-witnesses."

**The likely resolution:** one version of the sale-contract chapter should be canonical (almost certainly the longer "12" version, which is more fully developed), and the other removed. If the "11" number is vacated, the chapter ordering through the act needs to be re-sequenced. This is a human call, not a repair-agent call. No agent should attempt to reconcile the two.

### Minor Concerns

**The sale-contract chapter does not strictly close one of the nine enumerated doors.** The brief requires each chapter to close or materially narrow a named innocent explanation of the second codicil. The sale-contract chapter documents motive and formally installs R4 in the brief — essential work, and not orbiting — but the nine doors are: machine, Josiah's presence, witnesses, profession, firm, habit, despatch-box, September reading, date asymmetry. Motive isn't on the list. The chapter's foreclosure contribution is indirect: it narrows the despatch-box door by establishing what Walter needed the codicil to do and when he needed it, but it doesn't explicitly close it. This is a softer concern than the duplicate-chapter problem; the chapter earns its place through Thread 4 and the fair-play plant. But on strict compounding-mechanism audit, the brief's requirement is not fully met for this chapter in either version.

**One very minor voice-register note in 09-vale-mill.** The sentence "Fifteen years of managing it; everything he had borrowed against was in these buildings, and his men were inside them" is Prescott observing Walter — it's technically through Prescott's external noticing, which is permitted, but "his men" leans slightly toward inhabiting Walter's perspective rather than observing it. The effect is close enough to the line that it registers, though the passage does not tip into POV breach.

### Thread Development in This Act

All six threads advance on schedule. The witness trap (Thread 5) opens its irreversibility in 12-the-witnesses — Croft's attestation reports are "consistent but unbreakable," the commission is submitted with no address in hand — positioning the refused commission (Thread 5's permanent close) correctly for Act 3. The Garside displacement thread accumulates through three separate acts of professional discovery across the act's seven chapters, precisely as the brief requires. Walter's logic (Thread 4) is the act's most humanizing thread, receiving its three best moments: the mill visit (ch.09), the financial desperation laid bare (ch.11/12), and the kindly visit with the wrong word (ch.13). The six-thread weave is intact.

### In-Act Pacing

The act covers approximately February through May 1927. Chapter lengths are appropriate to their tasks; no chapter feels padded or rushed. The calendar countdown is named in every chapter and decreases monotonically — except for the duplicate problem, where both sale-contract chapters mark "185 days," leaving the calendar static between them. Once the duplicate is resolved, the calendar is clean. The act ends at 155 days, with the firm's record substantially built and the only remaining paths being the Hall depositions, the date asymmetry chapter, the commission refusal, and the discovery. The pacing into Act 3 is correctly set.

---

### Summary

The act is working. The prose is in register, the threads are advancing, and the compounding mechanism produces genuine accumulation — the reader at 13-the-fairhursts has more on the page than at 08-the-gazette, and they know it. The duplicate sale-contract chapter is the act's one genuine problem and requires a human decision on canonical version before the book can be considered structurally sound. Everything else is either working or minor enough to leave alone.
