# The Sale Contract

The sale contract ran to forty-three pages. Prescott had been through the first twenty-one before he reached the clause that controlled everything else.

*Completion of this Agreement is in all respects conditional upon the vendor producing a grant of representation establishing his title to the said estate and hereditaments.*

Twelve lines. Standard post-1925 form; the purchaser's solicitors had drafted it without any particular expectation of difficulty. Two memoranda of extension were attached behind it — the first dated January 1927, the second dated the fifteenth of March — each granting a further eight weeks on the standard form. Clipped behind the second memorandum was a note from Thurston & Marsh to the purchaser's solicitors, advising that a third extension was not expected.

When the action concluded, the contract concluded with it.

---

He turned to the mortgage papers.

The mortgage had been prepared by the firm — Garside's drafting, March 1921, his hand in the instrument number at the head of the page and in the attestation clause set below the covenants as Garside had always set it — and Prescott read it with the economy of a man who knew the architecture before he reached the operative parts. Eight thousand five hundred pounds secured on the mill's plant and machinery and the business's goodwill; a Blackburn bank on one side, Josiah Eccleston on the other; four and a half per cent and the quarter-days for the interest.

Hadfield had confirmed in February that the last two quarter-days' interest had been paid from the estate's capital. The principal was untouched and would remain untouched unless the sale proceeded. Prescott did not write the remainder of that calculation.

---

The banking correspondence had arrived in Hadfield's second parcel, copies obtained through discovery in the action: nine letters from Walter Eccleston to his bank manager, Mr. Holt, running from the spring of 1924 to January 1927, and five replies from Holt, whose responses grew shorter as the correspondence ran on.

The record across all nine letters was consistent. A mill mortgaged, an inheritance expected, a codicil complicating matters — but not permanently, Walter had been at pains to explain, because the codicil was a soft mood and his uncle had spoken of putting it right. The bank should be patient. The estate would come through. In the letters from 1926 and early 1927, Walter had given up explaining and simply affirmed: the estate would come through, the grant would follow, the bank should wait.

Among the nine letters was one dated the eleventh of June 1925, written after Holt had asked for further security. Prescott set the other eight aside and placed this one on the desk.

---

*Dear Mr. Holt, — You ask what further security I can offer beyond the mortgage. My uncle has made a codicil, as I told you, in favour of my late cousin Mrs. Fairhurst and her issue, but I am assured the arrangement will not stand; he made it in a soft mood and has spoken since of putting it right. My expectations are otherwise what they have always been. — Yours faithfully, WALTER ECCLESTON*

---

Prescott filed the letter with the mortgage and the sale contract. He opened Hadfield's monthly account, which had come with the second parcel.

The mill had lost a little over three hundred pounds against projected income in March. The wage bill was being met; the mortgage interest was not — Hadfield had paid the March quarter-day from the estate's capital reserves, following the same course as the two previous quarter-days. His estimate of the remaining reserves was not revised from his February communication: at the current rate, the estate's liquidity would be exhausted in approximately three months.

---

Prescott assembled the financial exhibits in order — the mortgage instrument, the sale contract with the two extension memoranda, all nine of the Holt letters, Hadfield's account — and clipped them into the brief.

One hundred and eighty-five days.

He had written the figure in the margin of his working notes that morning. The exhibits went behind it. Motive was clear and, now, documented. And then Josiah had died in October without putting it right, and within six days of the despatch-box being returned to the mill, a second codicil had appeared that put it right for him.

It explained the forgery. It proved nothing of the kind. For proof there was a typed document and a recital, and the recital quoted a text that had never left the strong-room at Winckley Square. That argument had not yet been made into the brief; it was the most work still remaining.

He stacked the exhibits and set the brief to one side. The witnesses folder lay open on the desk where Booth had left it that morning. There was the question of the commission — whether the Probate Division would grant one, and how long the application would take — and no settled answer to it yet.

He drew the folder toward him and turned to the first page.
