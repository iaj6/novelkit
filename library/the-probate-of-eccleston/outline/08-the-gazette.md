# Chapter 8 — The Gazette

**One-line summary:** Prescott advertises in the London Gazette and canvasses all solicitors in the county; the responses come in over weeks; every one is negative — no solicitor took any testamentary instructions from Josiah Eccleston after 1924.

---

## POV and Tense
Arthur Prescott — third person limited, past tense.

## Setting / Time
Garside & Prescott, Winckley Square, Preston. Late January through February 1927. The canvass takes several weeks; the chapter covers its arc through a montage of correspondence.

## Chapter Shape
**Correspondence chapter — time-skip or montage.** The advertisement is placed; the letters go out; the replies accumulate over weeks. Prescott's handling of each negative is rendered briefly; the cumulative effect is the closure. One substantial scene at the end when the last relevant reply arrives and Prescott completes the record.

## Ending Mode
**Resolved-beat.** The negative canvass is complete; the finding stated in a plain note for the file. The chapter ends cleanly: a professional task accomplished; a door locked.

---

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

**Prescott's goal:** Establish affirmatively that no solicitor anywhere in the county was instructed by Josiah Eccleston to draft any testamentary instrument after the execution of the first codicil in 1924.

**Obstacle:** The canvass is inherently open-ended; Prescott cannot know in advance whether a response will come in that complicates everything. Each reply reduces uncertainty; only the set of all replies makes the negative conclusive. The obstacle is time: the canvass must be conducted and completed before the evidence is assembled, and the calendar ticks.

**Beats:**

1. **The Gazette advertisement:** Brief scene — Prescott and Mr. Booth composing the advertisement for the London Gazette. Its substance: inviting any solicitor who acted for the late Josiah Eccleston of Eccleston Hall, Ribchester, in connection with any testamentary instrument or codicil after March 1924, to communicate with Garside & Prescott. The advertisement is placed; the letter-book records it.

2. **The circular letters:** Prescott writes — or directs Mr. Booth to write — to every firm of solicitors known to practise in the county. Preston, Lancaster, Blackburn, Burnley, the smaller towns. The letters ask the same thing. Mr. Booth handles the list; Prescott signs.

3. **Montage of replies:** Over several weeks, the replies come in — brief renderings of each. Some firms have no file for Josiah Eccleston at all. A few have acted for him on specific commercial matters (one conveyance in 1919 on the other party's side; one matter the circumstances of which make it plainly irrelevant). None has any testamentary instruction from Josiah after 1924. The replies accumulate. Prescott checks each against the list; the list shrinks.

4. **The Gazette advertisement produces nothing:** Two months after publication, no response has come through the Gazette. The silence is itself an exhibit.

5. **The final tally:** A late-February morning. The last expected reply arrives — a small Blackburn firm confirming no Eccleston file. Prescott checks the list: complete. Every firm has been canvassed. Every reply is negative. He notes the date.

6. **The calendar note:** Prescott glances at the hearing calendar: 235 days. He is on time, for now. The canvass consumed six weeks; it could not have been shorter.

7. **The record item:** Prescott writes a consolidated note for the file: "Canvass of all solicitors' firms in Lancashire county and advertisement in the London Gazette have produced no evidence that any solicitor was instructed to draw any testamentary instrument for the late Josiah Eccleston after the execution of the codicil of 15 March 1924." Signed, dated. Filed.

8. **Brief reflection on the profession:** The chapter touches lightly on the nature of the profession canvass — solicitors who keep records as Garside kept them, the collective archival density of a county's legal offices. There is no loose end in these replies that could become a problem later. The profession did not do this.

---

## Outcome / Turn
Door (d) is closed. No solicitor anywhere took instructions for a revocation. If the second codicil was drafted by a professional, it was done invisibly and outside the county — which would itself be aberrant for a man of Josiah's habits. The profession canvass supports the habit evidence from Ch 3 and Ch 7.

## New Question or Pressure Raised
Every professional route is closed. Every firm has been asked; none of them drew it. The mill-office typewriter has been observed but not formally tested yet (Ch 9 is next). If it wasn't drawn by a solicitor, and Josiah's habit was never to do things privately — where does that leave the cover story?

---

## What Compounds

- **Door (d) — the profession:** Closes. Affirmative negative: every solicitor in the county has been canvassed; the Gazette advertisement has been placed; no solicitor took testamentary instructions from Josiah after March 1924. The cover story's claim that Josiah "had it typed" privately is consistent with nothing in the professional record.

- **Prior chapter advanced:** Ch 7 — the habit evidence (every instrument through the firm, no private drafting) is supported from the external side: the profession confirms it saw nothing.

- **Days to hearing:** 235 days. Stated mid-chapter, before the final tally.

---

## Continuity Notes

- The London Gazette advertisement: placed; dated in the letter-book; no response came in.
- Canvass of Lancashire solicitors: complete; every firm covered; all responses negative.
- This finding — no professional involvement — is a permanent record item. It must not be revisited in later chapters.
- Mr. Booth's role: substantial in this chapter's logistics; he manages the correspondence list; his institutional knowledge of the county's firms is useful.
- The chapter should NOT introduce any new complication or hint at a positive response. The silence is the result.
- 235 days to hearing: noted matter-of-factly.
