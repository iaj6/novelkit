# Chapter 10 — The Machine

**One-line summary:** The document examiner's report arrives: the second codicil was typed on the mill-office typewriter; the Hall has no machine; no solicitor's office machine matches; the paper is consistent with mill-office stock.

---

## POV and Tense
Arthur Prescott — third person limited, past tense.

## Setting / Time
Garside & Prescott, Winckley Square, Preston. March 1927. A morning; the report received by post; Prescott reads it at his desk.

## Chapter Shape
**Single scene — document examination.** The examiner's report received and read; its implications worked through; the finding recorded. Prescott in his office; Mr. Booth briefly present at the opening. One location; near real-time.

## Ending Mode
**Resolved-beat.** The chapter closes with the machine identified as a hard fact. The door closes with the precision of a legal finding: source established, alternative sources eliminated.

---

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

**Prescott's goal:** Obtain a clear finding from the document examiner about the typewriter that produced the second codicil, and understand what that finding can and cannot prove.

**Obstacle:** Typewriter identification can prove the machine; it cannot prove who sat at the keys. This is the limit the examiner will state, and Prescott accepts it. The finding is evidence of source, not of authorship — authorship must come from other evidence. Also: Thurston & Marsh's own expert may contest the identification, and the handwriting experts are not yet in play.

**Beats:**

1. **The report arrives:** Mr. Booth brings in the morning post; the examiner's report is among it. The examiner is a trained document examiner from Manchester — practising since 1912, experienced in typewriter identification. Prescott opens the report at the desk.

2. **The examination:** The examiner's methodology: he has compared the typewriting of the second codicil with comparison specimens from the mill-office typewriter and specimens from the typewriters at various solicitors' offices (obtained through the canvass or by separate arrangement) and from the Hall (where, as the report confirms, no typewriter exists). His method: typeface design (make and model), then individual machine characteristics (worn keys, misaligned letters, specific defects in particular characters).

3. **The finding — make and model:** The second codicil was typed on a Remington No. 12 (or equivalent machine of the period and make) — identified from typeface design. The mill-office machine is the same make and model. No other machine examined is the same make and model.

4. **The finding — individual machine:** The examiner identifies four specific defects in the type of the second codicil that match corresponding defects in specimens from the mill-office typewriter: a worn serif on the letter "d," a slight upward displacement of the letter "r," and two other specific misalignments. These defects are present in the comparison specimens; they are not present in specimens from any other machine. His conclusion: the second codicil was typed on the mill-office machine.

5. **The limits stated:** The examiner notes, precisely and without prompting, what his identification does not prove: he cannot establish who operated the machine, or when. He can say the machine is the source; he cannot say the occasion or the operator.

6. **The paper:** A brief supplementary note on the paper: the rag-content paper used for the second codicil is consistent with the stock kept in the mill office (a supply of plain writing paper kept for correspondence); it is not the heavier, better-quality paper used by Garside & Prescott for engrossments. This is consistent with, but does not prove, mill-office origin.

7. **Prescott's analysis:** The examiner's finding is good. The machine is identified; the Hall is eliminated (no typewriter); the solicitors' offices are eliminated (different machines or no match); the mill office is the source. Combined with the cover story: Walter says Josiah typed it at the mill or had it typed there. Josiah's last confirmed visit was May 14 (Ch 9). The document examiner's report proves the machine; Ch 15 will prove Josiah wasn't there.

8. **Close:** Prescott writes the finding into the working note: "The second codicil was typed on the mill-office typewriter, identified by individual machine characteristics. No other machine examined produces a match." Filed. The door shuts. 200 days remaining — noted in the margin of the file page.

---

## Outcome / Turn
Door (a) is closed. The second codicil was typed on the mill-office machine. This is now a forensic fact. Combined with door (b) (Josiah's last visit May 1926), when that door closes, the cover story will be physically impossible: the codicil was typed on a machine in a room Josiah had not entered since May.

## New Question or Pressure Raised
The machine is identified. But the examiner cannot say who sat at the keys. Who, in August or October 1926, had access to the mill-office typewriter and a reason to use it? Walter Eccleston worked there every day. Josiah was not there.

---

## What Compounds

- **Door (a) — the machine:** Closes. The second codicil's typewriting is matched to the mill-office typewriter by individual machine characteristics: specific worn serifs and letter displacements identified in both. No other machine matches. The Hall is confirmed to have no typewriter.

- **Prior chapter advanced:** Ch 9 — the mill-office typewriter was observed and noted; the document examiner has now formally matched it; Ch 7 — Josiah's diary noted no mill activity in August; this is now a forensic confirmation of location that must be explained.

- **Days to hearing:** 200 days. Stated at the close of the working note.

---

## Continuity Notes

- The document examiner: a professional from Manchester; his report is a formal exhibit.
- The second codicil was typed on the mill-office typewriter: this is established as a forensic finding, not an inference.
- Specific individual defects identified: worn serif on "d"; upward displacement of "r"; two other defects. These are the same defects in both the codicil and the comparison specimens from the mill office.
- The Hall has no typewriter: confirmed by the examiner (no machine found there to compare; household confirmed it by deposition if needed in Ch 15).
- The paper is consistent with mill-office stock; this is corroborating but not conclusive alone.
- The examiner's limit: he can prove the machine, not the operator.
- The handwriting analysis (on Josiah's signature on the second codicil) has not yet been addressed in the book. Two handwriting experts will be retained later; they will split. This chapter concerns typewriting only.
- 200 days to hearing: stated at the close of the chapter.
