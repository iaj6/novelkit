# Chapter 7 — The File

**One-line summary:** Prescott completes the forty-year survey of Josiah Eccleston's instruments with the firm and reviews Josiah's private diary and later correspondence: every instrument is here, and nothing — not a word — suggests Josiah ever changed his mind about the 1924 codicil.

---

## POV and Tense
Arthur Prescott — third person limited, past tense.

## Setting / Time
Garside & Prescott, Winckley Square, Preston. Late January 1927. A long working day extending into the evening; the fire banked; the last of the daylight gone early.

## Chapter Shape
**Document or epistolary — interiority-light.** Prescott works through the rest of Josiah's papers: the physical diary from the Hall (obtained through the administrator), correspondence with family and business associates, the firm's full instrument register. One location; methodical.

## Ending Mode
**Coda.** The chapter closes on Prescott returning the last of the papers to the file — Josiah's handwriting, Garside's notes, the register's final entries — and recording his finding. Quiet; the lamp; the strong-room door closed. The Garside displacement thread has its heaviest moment here.

---

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

**Prescott's goal:** Establish, from Josiah's private diary and correspondence, whether any change of mind about the first codicil is recorded anywhere, and confirm that no other instrument affecting the estate was made anywhere.

**Obstacle:** The diary and correspondence are personal; reading them is an intrusion that Prescott performs with professional precision rather than curiosity. The evidence he is looking for — absence — is not visually dramatic. He is also aware that a man's diary not containing a statement is not conclusive. But it is a record.

**Beats:**

1. **The diary arrived:** Through the administrator, Josiah's pocket diary from 1926 has been produced — a small leather diary, his handwriting in it. Prescott opens it without ceremony.

2. **The diary's entries:** Josiah, at eighty-one and in declining health, kept a sparse diary. Appointments at the mill (reduced in frequency from January 1926 onward; the last mill entry is May 1926). A few household notes. A doctor's visit. A letter from a magistrates' clerk. Nothing about the codicil; nothing about Walter; nothing about Margaret's children; nothing about any testamentary intention. His last entry is in August — the date of the forged second codicil — a note about the weather and a visit from his steward. The entry is mundane. It is the entry of a man who made no testamentary instrument that day.

3. **Josiah's 1926 correspondence (from the Hall):** Letters arriving and departing from the Hall from January to October 1926. None to any solicitor other than Garside & Prescott; none concerning any change to the will or codicil; none that could be read as reflecting any change of mind. A brief, warm letter to Nora Fairhurst in spring 1926 — Josiah's grandniece, in effect — the tone of which confirms the reconciliation as settled and genuine, not temporary.

4. **The firm's instrument register, final section:** Prescott returns to the instrument register — the comprehensive list of every instrument made on Josiah's behalf since 1884. He reads the last entry: the first codicil, dated 15 March 1924. No later entry. No 1926 codicil. The register ends with the 1924 instrument.

5. **Door (f) clinched:** The combination of the diary, the correspondence, and the instrument register closes the door completely: Josiah's private record is as silent as his legal record. There is no evidence, private or public, that he ever changed his mind after March 1924 or that he ever directed any such change through any channel.

6. **Garside displacement (major instance):** At the bottom of the file, Prescott finds the covering letter Garside wrote to Josiah when the first codicil was engrossed and ready for execution — March 1924. A brief, careful letter: the instrument is enclosed, properly prepared, and awaits his signature in the presence of two witnesses; Garside will attend if Mr. Eccleston prefers. The letter is three sentences. It is more careful than three sentences need to be: every detail correct, the formalities noted, the witnesses' role explained even though Josiah knew it already. Garside wrote it for the record, not for instruction. The file is Garside. Prescott notes this and returns the letter to its place.

7. **Close:** Prescott writes the finding into the working note: "No private record or correspondence contains any reference to any intention to revoke or alter the first codicil after its execution in March 1924." He dates it; closes the file; goes to the strong-room to replace the register. The strong-room smells of clean paper and iron shelves. He locks it and goes home.

---

## Outcome / Turn
Door (f) is closed. The forty-year record is complete; the diary and correspondence are silent. Josiah Eccleston did not privately record any change of mind, and everything he made legally for forty years went through this firm. There is no innocent route by which the second codicil could have been made with Josiah's knowledge and Garside & Prescott's ignorance.

## New Question or Pressure Raised
Every instrument through the firm; no change of mind; no private action. The mechanism by which the second codicil was made is becoming clearer. But who is left — if Josiah didn't make it, and no solicitor made it, and the firm never gave out the text?

---

## What Compounds

- **Door (f) — the habit:** Closes. The full survey is complete: every instrument, the diary, the correspondence. Nothing in Josiah's private record supports the cover story. The habit of using the firm is Josiah's habit; the silence of his diary is the silence of a man who made no August codicil.

- **Prior chapters advanced:** Ch 3 — the habit survey begun in Movement I is now complete; Ch 5 — the register's final entry (the 1924 codicil) confirms what Garside's hand recorded; Ch 6 — the letter-book's negative confirmed from the other direction (no private correspondence reflects a request for the text either).

- **Days to hearing:** 250 days. Stated early in the chapter.

## Continuity Notes

- Josiah's diary, 1926: sparse; no mention of any testamentary act in August 1926; last mill entry in May 1926; entries continue through August but are mundane.
- The last entry in the firm's instrument register: the first codicil, 15 March 1924. No further instrument.
- Josiah's correspondence with Nora Fairhurst (spring 1926): warm; confirms the reconciliation as settled and genuine.
- Garside's covering letter with the first codicil (March 1924): now in Prescott's file as a corroborating exhibit.
- Garside displacement: central instance — the letter that was more careful than it needed to be; the file that was built for exactly this use.
- The forty-year survey is now complete. This door is closed; it must not be re-walked in later chapters.
