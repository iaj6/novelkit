# Chapter 17 — The Ninth Word

**One-line summary:** Late and alone, doing a final file-discipline check before the brief goes to London, Prescott reads the propounded second codicil against the strong-room original of the first — and sees, in the ninth word of the recital, the difference between "and" and "or."

---

## POV and Tense
Arthur Prescott — third person limited, past tense.

## Setting / Time
Garside & Prescott, Winckley Square, Preston. A July evening, perhaps 9 or 10 p.m. The office empty except for Prescott; the strong-room open; both documents on the table.

## Chapter Shape
**Bottle — interiority-light, document-anchored.** One location; one act; near real-time. The chapter is almost entirely Prescott reading two documents. What happens is small and enormous.

## Ending Mode
**Turn.** The discovery is the structural pivot of the entire novel. Everything that follows is consequence; everything before it was preparation. The chapter ends with the variance seen and R4 pulled from the file — the three pieces in Prescott's hands, and nowhere left to go but forward.

---

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

**Prescott's goal:** Complete the final file-discipline check before dispatching the brief to London counsel — compare the propounded copy of the second codicil against the firm's own records to confirm that every citation in the brief is exact.

**Obstacle:** He is tired; the office is late; the check is routine. He does not know what he is about to find. The obstacle is the ordinariness of the act: this is a clerk's check, not a detective's eureka moment.

**Beats:**

1. **The setting:** Late evening; the office empty. Mr. Booth has gone home. The brief for London is nearly complete. Prescott, before he sends it, does what Garside always did and what he has done himself since: he reads everything against the original, once, from the start.

2. **The strong-room:** He goes down to the strong-room and retrieves the Eccleston deed-box. He opens it and takes out the first codicil — the executed original, in the strong-room since March 1924. He brings it upstairs.

3. **The propounded copy:** He has the second codicil — the propounded instrument produced by Thurston & Marsh — from the action files. He places both documents on the desk.

4. **The comparison begins:** He reads the second codicil's recital to compare it with the first codicil's text. He is checking for citation accuracy in the brief. He reads the recital: "WHEREAS by a codicil to my said will dated the fifteenth day of March 1924 I gave the residue of my estate unto my niece Margaret Fairhurst and her issue absolutely—"

5. **The first codicil:** He reads the first codicil's operative clause: "...unto my niece Margaret Fairhurst of Bank Parade Preston or her issue absolutely..."

6. **The ninth word:** He stops. He goes back to the recital. He counts: "Margaret Fairhurst" — one, two — "and" — the ninth word. He looks at the first codicil: "or." He looks again at the recital: "and." He sits still for a moment. The ninth word of the recital is wrong.

7. **What Prescott understands:** He does not say it aloud; there is no one to say it to. He understands what the difference means, and what it proves, and what it does not prove, in the space of perhaps twenty seconds. The recital quotes the first codicil — any careful codicil quotes the instrument it revokes — and it quotes it wrong. Not on the date (the date is exact). On the operative word. The one word that would survive in a man's memory incorrectly if he had only ever heard the clause described, not read it. The one word that Walter Eccleston has been getting wrong since 1924.

8. **R4 pulled from the file:** Prescott goes to the exhibit file and finds R4 — Walter's bank letter of 11 June 1925 (filed in Ch 11 as a financial exhibit). He reads it: "in favour of my late cousin Mrs. Fairhurst and her issue." He notes: fourteen months before the second codicil's date, in Walter's own hand, the same wrong word. The document is not new; it has been in the file for months. He is reading it as if for the first time.

9. **The proof chain assembled (privately):** The recital says "and." The executed text says "or." Walter's bank letter — in his hand, fourteen months before the codicil's date — says "and." No copy of the first codicil ever left the firm; the text was never transmitted to anyone. Walter was present at the September reading but never had the text before his eyes. Walter has said "and" since Josiah told him in March 1924, in Josiah's own spoken words. The recital was not taken from the document; it was taken from a memory that was wrong in exactly the way Walter's memory was wrong, on exactly the word that a spoken account would blur.

10. **Not triumph:** Prescott puts both documents down and the bank letter with them. He does not feel triumph. He feels the weight of what the record has produced — slowly, exactly, without drama. He notes the time. He notes the date. He writes a single sentence in the working file: "The recital of the second codicil cites the first codicil as giving the residue 'unto my niece Margaret Fairhurst and her issue absolutely.' The executed instrument (R2) says 'or her issue.' The variance is between R2 and R3's recital."

11. **Close:** He replaces both documents. He sits at the desk for a while; the office around him; the strong-room register, Garside's hand, on the shelf below. Then he takes out a fresh sheet of paper and begins to write the addition to the brief.

---

## Outcome / Turn
The variance is found. The ninth word of the recital says "and" where the executed first codicil says "or." R4 says "and" in Walter's hand, fourteen months earlier. The discovery is not a guess; it is a file check. Nothing new has entered the record; the three pieces (R2, R3, R4) were all already there.

## New Question or Pressure Raised
The variance is found. Now it must be proved — not just that the recital is wrong, but that Walter is the only candidate who could have made it wrong in exactly this way, with the date exactly right. The brief must be rewritten to lead with the variance. 88 days.

## What Compounds

- **Door (i) — the date asymmetry:** Closes. The direct comparison of R2 and R3 reveals the variance; R4 (from Ch 11) ties the misquotation to Walter's habitual version. The asymmetry is now complete: the date (right, from R5) and the word (wrong, from Walter's memory). Walter is the only candidate who satisfies both conditions.

- **Prior chapters advanced:** Ch 1 — R2 was introduced entire in the first chapter; Ch 2 — R3 was introduced entire in the second chapter; Ch 11 — R4 entered the file as a financial exhibit; the discovery is a re-reading of what all three chapters have already put before the reader.

- **Days to hearing:** 88 days. The brief must be complete. The discovery has come nearly too late.
