# Chapter 5 — The Hinge

**One-line summary:** Henry Garside dies at his desk on a December morning; Prescott inherits the action, the firm, and the old man's files; re-reading the strong-room register entire, Prescott establishes definitively what was read at the September hearing, who was present, and what the record says.

---

## POV and Tense
Arthur Prescott — third person limited, past tense.

## Setting / Time
Garside & Prescott, Winckley Square, Preston. December 1926. The day of Garside's death and the days immediately following: the office in winter, the strong-room at night, the file as inheritance.

## Chapter Shape
**Multi-scene — interiority-light, document-anchored.** Two linked scenes: Garside's death (brief, handled with restraint); and Prescott's extended re-reading of the Eccleston file and the strong-room register by himself. The second scene is the weight of the chapter.

## Ending Mode
**Turn.** The structural hinge of the novel: Garside is gone; the action, the firm, and the register — Garside's twenty years of entries — are Prescott's now. What Garside built becomes what Prescott must use. The chapter ends with the first calendar number.

---

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

**Prescott's goal:** In the days after Garside's death, Prescott must understand the full state of the Eccleston file and decide what the action requires of him now that he is carrying it alone.

**Obstacle:** He is now the firm. There is no one above him to advise. The file is his; the risk is his; and the thing he is looking for — proof that the second codicil is wrong — is not yet there.

**Beats:**

1. **Garside's death:** Brief and plain. Garside dies at his desk on a December morning — his heart. Mr. Booth (the managing clerk) is the one who finds him. Prescott arrives mid-morning, having been in court. The chapter enters the scene already past the moment: the office has been quiet for twenty minutes before Prescott knows. He makes the necessary arrangements; he does not dramatize what he feels. He is thirty-eight; he has worked beside this man for sixteen years.

2. **The days that follow:** The firm continues because Mr. Booth continues. The clients are written to; the other matters are disposed of or referred. Prescott is the firm now — partner, probate man, and managing clerk in all but name. The weight of this is carried in what he does next, not in what he says about it.

3. **The file at night:** Several days after Garside's death, Prescott is alone in the office late. He pulls the Eccleston file entire and reads it from the beginning — not because there is anything new in it, but because this is what Garside would have done: a thorough read before proceeding, to know exactly what one has. The coal fire burning low.

4. **The strong-room register examined in full:** Prescott opens the strong-room register — he has looked at the September entry before (Ch 1), but now he reads the page entire. Garside's hand: the entry for 15 September 1926. Document: "First Codicil, J. Eccleston dec., dated 15 March 1924." Purpose: "Read to assembled family at testator's request; testator's health declining; testator wished there to be no surprises." Persons present: Garside, Prescott, Walter Eccleston, Mr. Elias Beckett (Josiah's steward). Time out: 9:45 a.m. Time returned: 3:20 p.m. Document in good order. Signed: H. Garside.

5. **What the register proves:** Prescott sits with this for a moment. The entry is complete — more complete than the occasion required. Garside recorded the reason for the reading. He recorded who was present. He noted the document was read entire. Walter was there. Prescott was there. Both men heard "or her issue" read aloud. The register is exact; it has been exact since 1903; twenty years of Garside's discipline are visible in its pages.

6. **Garside displacement (central instance):** Looking at the register — twenty years of entries in the same hand, the same precision — Prescott understands that Garside knew the thing might come to this. He built the record as if it would. He may not have known about any forgery; but he recorded every visit and every return with the care of a man who understood that the record was the only thing that could not be crossed. Prescott does not name any of this. He restores the register to its shelf.

7. **The hearing date:** A letter from the Principal Probate Registry arrives or is expected: the action has been assigned a hearing date. The date is, say, approximately 290 days hence. This is the first time the calendar is stated as a number. Prescott notes it.

8. **Close:** Prescott banks the fire and locks the strong-room. The office is quiet. He has the file, the register, the hearing date, and no proof yet. He goes home.

---

## Outcome / Turn
Garside is dead. Prescott is the firm. The strong-room register is definitively established: the September reading is fact; what was read, who was present, and the date are all in Garside's hand. The clock has started.

## New Question or Pressure Raised
Prescott has a hearing date and no proof. The record of the September reading is now locked in the register — Walter was present when the instrument was read entire. But what does that prove about the codicil's authorship? How does a document tell you who made it?

---

## What Compounds

- **Door (h) — the September reading:** Closes. The strong-room register is read entire for the first time. What it records is now a fixed fact: the first codicil was fetched on 15 September 1926, read entire to the assembled family (including Walter) at Josiah's request, and returned the same day. The reason for the reading is noted in Garside's hand. The date of the instrument was included in what Garside read. Nothing about this entry is uncertain; the register cannot be altered.

- **Prior chapters advanced:** Ch 1 — the September register entry first surfaced; this chapter establishes its full content; Ch 3 — the habit of precise record-keeping confirmed as Garside's consistent practice (twenty years of the register behind this entry).

- **Days to hearing:** **290 days** — first stated. The clock has started.

## Continuity Notes

- Henry Garside: dies December 1926, at his desk, his heart. Age approximately 74.
- Strong-room register, 15 September 1926 entry: document — first codicil of J. Eccleston dec.; purpose — read to assembled family at testator's request; persons present — Garside, Prescott, Walter Eccleston, [one household witness]; time out — 9:45 a.m.; time returned — 3:20 p.m.; document in good order; signed H. Garside.
- This entry is in Garside's hand; it cannot be altered; it is a register entry made contemporaneously.
- Walter Eccleston was present at the September reading and heard the first codicil read entire, including its date and its operative words.
- Prescott was present at the September reading, carrying the deed-box.
- The hearing date is approximately 290 days from this point.
- Mr. Booth, the managing clerk: has run the office since 1911; continues after Garside's death; Prescott and Booth are now the firm.
- Garside displacement thread: major instance here — reading the register in Garside's hand; the precision of the entries; understanding that the record was built for this purpose.
- Do NOT have Prescott think abstractly or at length about his grief for Garside. The grief surfaces entirely through what he notices in the work Garside left.
