# Chapter 20 — The Estate

**One-line summary:** The estate is settled, the criminal consequence stated plainly; the children receive their grant; the mill is sold at a loss; the firm recovers its costs; Garside's file is closed — and was, all along, the thing that won.

---

## POV and Tense
Arthur Prescott — third person limited, past tense.

## Setting / Time
Garside & Prescott, Winckley Square, Preston; Bank Parade; and briefly Prescott's own office in the weeks and months after the hearing. Autumn 1927.

## Chapter Shape
**Time-skip or montage — coda-weighted.** The chapter covers several weeks or months in accelerated time, settling each open account in turn. The pace is quiet; there is no urgency now. The final scene is Prescott alone in the office.

## Ending Mode
**Coda.** The book closes as it opened — in the office, with the file. The last image is Garside's: the register in the strong-room, the hand that built the record that won the case.

---

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

**Prescott's goal:** Settle every account the book has opened: the estate's disposition; the children; Walter; the mill; the firm; and the standing fact of what Garside built.

**Obstacle:** None that can be overcome — only the weight of consequence to be stated and accepted. The chapter's obstacle is the cost of winning: Walter is ruined; the mill will bleed; the children receive what the law gives them but not what their mother would have wanted for them in these circumstances. The book does not withhold the cost.

**Beats:**

1. **Edgar Fairhurst notified:** Prescott writes to Edgar from his London hotel room the afternoon of the hearing. The letter is brief: the action has succeeded; the second codicil has failed; letters of administration will be applied for in due course. The estate will pass to the children under the first codicil. He will write again with more detail when he is back in Preston.

2. **Letters of administration with the will annexed:** In the weeks following, the grant is made. Edgar Fairhurst receives letters of administration with the will annexed as guardian of Nora and George, both being minors. Not probate; not Walter as executor. The grant is made in the correct form. The estate is now administrable.

3. **Bank Parade — the last visit:** Prescott visits Bank Parade one more time. Edgar is composed; Nora is quieter than Prescott expected — not exultant, not aggrieved, the way people are when something long-awaited arrives and proves to be not quite what was imagined. George asks what happens to the mill. Prescott tells him.

4. **The mill:** The estate includes the mortgaged mill. The administrator's figures have been accurate: the mortgage cannot be serviced from income; a forced sale is necessary. The mill will go — at a price well below the 1919 valuation; the proceeds, after the mortgage is discharged, will leave less than Walter's sale price would have yielded. The children are not poor; the estate includes the Hall, a modest sum, and the mill's diminished residue. They are not what Walter was, which is to say: they are not ruined.

5. **Walter:** The criminal consequence is stated plainly. Walter faces prosecution for the forgery — not presented with legal detail, but as a fact: charges will follow, as they must. He is not on the page in this chapter; what happens to him is stated in a sentence, perhaps two. He was not a monster. He was a man who persuaded himself he was correcting an error and committed a felony instead. Both propositions remain true.

6. **The firm's accounts:** Costs are recovered. Garside & Prescott will continue. The recovery does not make the firm wealthy; it makes it solvent. Prescott signs the fee note with the same restraint he brings to everything else. The firm, after this, is his alone.

7. **The office — Garside's file:** The Eccleston matter file is closed — a final note for the record, Prescott's hand, dated. He returns the file to the strong-room. He is putting away a file that took longer than it should, cost more than it budgeted, and won on a word. He puts it on the shelf beside the register — Garside's hand, twenty years of entries.

8. **Garside displacement — final instance:** Prescott opens the register to make the final notation — "matter completed; grant of administration issued; [date]." He writes in the column below Garside's last entries. Then he reads back up the page: the September 1926 entry, in Garside's hand, that started all of it. The instrument read entire. The date. Who was present. Signed. More precise than the occasion required. He made the record for exactly this. He could not have known it; he made it anyway, because that was his habit.

9. **Close — the plain sentence:** Prescott closes the register and returns it to its place. He locks the strong-room. He goes upstairs. It is a Tuesday in October, a year after Josiah Eccleston died at eighty-one. The case is done. He picks up the next file on his desk.
