# Chapter 18 — Counsel's Brief

**One-line summary:** Prescott assembles and dispatches the complete hearing brief to London: the nine closed doors; the recital variance at the centre; the handwriting evidence framed as subsidiary; the proof chain stated honestly.

---

## POV and Tense
Arthur Prescott — third person limited, past tense.

## Setting / Time
Garside & Prescott, Winckley Square, Preston; and brief correspondence with Thurston & Marsh. Late July to mid-August 1927. The final weeks before the hearing.

## Chapter Shape
**Correspondence chapter — intercut.** Prescott assembles the brief; a letter from Thurston & Marsh arrives (they sense the shape of what's coming); the brief is dispatched. Two threads briefly intercut — the brief and the opposing side's muted response.

## Ending Mode
**Mid-action-cut.** The brief is sent; the hearing is approaching; the outcome is not yet determined. The chapter ends with the brief in the post to London and 60 days left — the machine running, no turning back.

---

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

**Prescott's goal:** Complete the brief so that London counsel has everything necessary to argue the case at the hearing: every door closed, every exhibit named, the recital variance as the spine, the handwriting split honestly framed.

**Obstacle:** The brief must be complete and legally honest. The proof chain is genuine but circumstantial in the old sense — each element supports the others; none is individually conclusive except the variance itself, which proves the recital was not taken from the document. The brief must state what each element proves and what it does not.

**Beats:**

1. **The brief begins — the variance as the spine:** Prescott drafts the argument section of the brief. He leads with the recital variance: the second codicil quotes the first codicil as giving the residue "and her issue"; the executed first codicil says "or her issue." A recital that misquotes the instrument it recites was not taken from the instrument. The question is: where was it taken from?

2. **The nine doors as the elimination argument:** The brief then proceeds through the nine doors in order. No machine at the Hall or any solicitor's office matches; the mill-office machine does (door (a)). Josiah was at the Hall from June 1926; the machine was at the mill he could not visit (door (b)). The witnesses are beyond examination; their selection is consistent with deliberate choice of unavailable witnesses (door (c)). No solicitor took instructions (door (d)). No request for the text reached the firm (door (e)). Josiah's habit was the firm; forty years without exception (door (f)). The despatch-box contained no codicil until it stood at the mill (door (g)). The September reading is exact in the register (door (h)). The date was transmitted to Walter by R5; the text was not transmitted to anyone (door (i)).

3. **The authorship argument:** The recital was composed from a source that had the date right (R5) and the word wrong (Walter's habitual misquotation). Walter Eccleston has said "and her issue" since March 1924: in R4 (June 1925, in writing, fourteen months before the codicil's date), and in spoken form (attendance Ch 2; Nora's report Ch 13). He heard the instrument read aloud once (September 1926) but had never had the text before his eyes. He forged around a document he had never read, and transcribed his own misquotation into it.

4. **The handwriting evidence:** The brief handles the handwriting evidence with care. Two experts have been retained — one per side; they have examined Josiah's signature on the second codicil. Their reports, as counsel will know, split. They do not agree; neither can resolve the other. The brief states this plainly: the handwriting evidence is inconclusive; the case does not rest on it; the text convicts.

5. **Thurston & Marsh's letter:** A letter arrives from Thurston & Marsh — carefully worded, inquiring about the state of Prescott's evidence. The professional courtesy of the letter is itself a signal: they know something is coming. Prescott replies with equal courtesy and no disclosure beyond what is required. He and Mr. Booth read the letter once and file it. 60 days.

6. **The brief dispatched:** The brief — exhibits numbered, argument complete, counsel instructed — goes to London by first post. The deed box is checked; the originals of R1, R2, and R3 are accounted for and will travel to London for the hearing. Prescott signs the letter of instruction to counsel.

7. **Close:** The brief is in the post. The office is quiet. Mr. Booth has checked the registered mail receipt. 60 days to the hearing. Prescott takes out the next correspondence and goes back to work.

---

## Outcome / Turn
The brief is dispatched. The proof chain is complete, honestly stated, and built around the variance as the spine. The handwriting evidence is subsidiary and the brief says so. 60 days remain.

## New Question or Pressure Raised
The brief is in counsel's hands. Thurston & Marsh know something is approaching. Walter does not yet know what the record says about his own words. At the hearing — or in the immediate face of it — he will be shown the word he has never seen.

---

## What Compounds

- **N/A — corroboration only.** All nine doors are closed. This chapter assembles and dispatches the brief. No new door is closed; what compounds is the coherence of the assembled case: the nine doors + the variance = a proof that has no innocent explanation remaining.

- **Prior chapters advanced:** All. The brief synthesises every door-closure chapter; it is the moment where the full record is stated as an argument rather than accumulated as individual findings.

- **Days to hearing:** 60 days. Noted at the point of dispatch.

- The handwriting experts split: both have reported; they disagree; the brief states this plainly and does not rely on handwriting.

- The originals of R1, R2, and R3 will travel to London for the hearing.
