# Chapter 16 — The Date

**One-line summary:** Preparing the hearing brief, Prescott re-examines R5 as an exhibit: the firm's November 2 letter gave Walter the first codicil's date in writing — and nothing else; the letter-book carbon corroborates; the asymmetry is now a formal record item.

---

## POV and Tense
Arthur Prescott — third person limited, past tense.

## Setting / Time
Garside & Prescott, Winckley Square, Preston. July 1927. A working week in final preparation; the hearing brief being assembled document by document.

## Chapter Shape
**Single scene — document examination.** Prescott assembles the brief; R5 is examined as an exhibit in the context of the date question. One location; methodical. The chapter is about the architecture of what Walter could and could not have known.

## Ending Mode
**Declarative-close.** The chapter closes on a plain statement of the asymmetry as a record fact: Walter had the date; he did not have the text; R5 explains why. The prose states it cleanly and stops.

---

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

**Prescott's goal:** Establish precisely, as an exhibit, how Walter could have known the correct date of the first codicil to put in the second codicil's recital — and confirm that no route exists by which he could have obtained the correct text.

**Obstacle:** The date looks like it could simply be known by recollection — Walter was present at the September reading. But the precise date ("the fifteenth day of March 1924") in the recital, while it matches R5 exactly, could also have been remembered from hearing Garside read the instrument. The argument from the date alone is not as clean as the argument from the word. Prescott must set up the asymmetry precisely: both halves of it.

**Beats:**

1. **The hearing brief assembled:** Prescott is working through the exhibits in sequence, preparing the brief for counsel. He reaches R5 — the firm's notice letter of 2 November 1926. He examines it as an exhibit, formally.

2. **R5 re-examined:** [R5 referenced by name and key phrase — not quoted in full again; the date ("the 15th March 1924") is extracted as the material element.] Prescott traces the documentary chain: R5 was sent to Thurston & Marsh on 2 November 1926. Walter's solicitors received it; Walter would have seen it. The letter contains the date of the first codicil precisely as it appears in the second codicil's recital: "the fifteenth day of March 1924."

3. **The letter-book carbon as corroboration:** The letter-book carbon of R5 confirms the transmission — dated, addressed, filed. The date was in writing to Thurston & Marsh from 2 November 1926. Before that date — including in the six days the despatch-box stood at the mill — Walter did not have the date in writing. R5 gave it to him.

4. **The timeline of the forgery:** The despatch-box stood at the mill from approximately mid-October 1926 (when Hargreaves sailed to Canada — one of the two newly-unavailable witnesses) to when Walter produced the codicil. R5 was sent on 2 November. The forgery must have been made between 2 November 1926 (when Walter received the date in writing) and the day he produced it to his solicitors. The forger dated the instrument 14 August to predate his access to the box.

5. **The asymmetry stated:** Prescott writes the working note: "The recital of the second codicil is exact as to date ('the fifteenth day of March 1924') and wrong as to the operative words ('and her issue' for 'or her issue'). The date was in Walter Eccleston's hands in writing from 2 November 1926, by transmission of R5. The text of the first codicil was never transmitted in writing from this firm or from any other source, as established by the letter-book record (Ch 6 finding) and the profession canvass (Ch 8 finding). The recital reproduces the date that was available in writing; it does not reproduce the text that was not."

6. **What this proves:** Prescott sets down what this establishes and what it does not. It establishes that the source of the date in the recital was R5 and not the executed text. Combined with the executed text's different word — and Walter's habitual misquotation (documented in R4, Ch 11, and in Ch 13's attendance note) — it establishes that the recital was composed from memory and from R5, not from the executed document. This is not, by itself, conclusive of authorship. But it is conclusive that the recital was not composed by someone who had access to the executed text.

7. **Close:** 90 days to the hearing. Prescott files the working note. The asymmetry is now in the record. The discovery — seeing the variance itself — has not yet been made. He has been working around it for months. He has not yet looked at the two texts side by side.

---

## Outcome / Turn
The date asymmetry is a formal record item: Walter had the date from R5; he did not have the text; the recital exhibits both halves of this. The argument is assembled. The discovery remains to be made.

## New Question or Pressure Raised
The asymmetry is established: date right, text wrong. But Prescott has been assembling this argument about the date without yet making the one comparison that would show the variance directly — R3's recital against R2's text. He has been around the codicils in circles. He has not yet read one against the other.

---

## What Compounds

- **Door (i) — the date asymmetry:** Advances to its penultimate step. The documentary source of the date (R5) is established as a record item; the absence of any text source is confirmed; the working note states the asymmetry's two halves. What remains is the direct comparison of R2 and R3 — which will come in the next chapter.

- **Prior chapters advanced:** Ch 6 — the letter-book finding (no text transmitted) is now cited in the working note; Ch 11 — R4's written misquotation ("and her issue," June 1925) is now part of the asymmetry's logic; Ch 13 — the spoken misquotation reported by Nora further documents the habitual version.

- **Days to hearing:** 90 days. Noted at the close.
