# Chapter 3 — The Habit

**One-line summary:** Prescott and Garside survey forty years of Josiah Eccleston's legal instruments — every one drawn by this firm — as the opening step in understanding whether a privately-made codicil could have existed at all.

---

## POV and Tense
Arthur Prescott — third person limited, past tense.

## Setting / Time
Garside & Prescott, Winckley Square, Preston. Mid-November 1926. A working morning in the office; the Eccleston files pulled from the cabinets. Cold November light through the tall windows.

## Chapter Shape
**Document or epistolary — interiority-light.** Prescott works through the file; Garside is present intermittently. The chapter is a review of documents (conveyances, previous wills, correspondence, the 1919 will, the 1924 codicil) with procedural reflection between. One location; near real-time.

## Ending Mode
**Coda.** The chapter closes quietly: Garside re-shelves the last file, and Prescott records a finding — forty years of Josiah's instruments, all here, not one of them made elsewhere. The file is too complete to suggest a habit of private drafting.

---

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

**Prescott's goal:** Establish whether Josiah Eccleston had any history of private testamentary drafting — any instrument made outside the firm — and whether his correspondence or diary contains any indication that he had changed his mind about the 1924 codicil.

**Obstacle:** The documents are extensive (forty years) and orderly. The task is patient. There is also a quieter obstacle: Prescott is reading Garside's work, and it is better than the occasion required.

**Beats:**

1. **Opening:** Prescott pulls the Eccleston index from the cabinet: a bound list in Garside's hand, every matter and instrument, from Josiah's first conveyance in 1884. The list runs to three pages. Prescott begins at the beginning.

2. **The conveyances and deeds:** The early entries — purchase of the mill, the mortgage of 1921, the Hall's title deeds. All through the firm. A note in Garside's hand on the mortgage instrument: "prepared on client's instructions, 17 March 1921. Terms agreed with Blackburn bank. [signature.]" The precision is characteristic.

3. **The previous wills:** Two earlier wills before 1919, both drawn by Garside & Prescott, both superseded. The 1919 will (R1) replaced the second of these; it is in the strong-room, as Prescott knows. There is no instrument anywhere in this file that was made privately, before a stranger solicitor, or in any way outside the firm.

4. **Josiah's correspondence in the file:** The letter-book copies of Garside's outgoing letters to Josiah, and Josiah's own letters to the firm: a man who communicated through his solicitor for every material transaction. His voice in the letters — direct, laconic, practical. None of the letters in this file, from 1924 to his last correspondence in mid-1926, contain any reference to any desire to revoke or alter the first codicil.

5. **Garside's note from March 1924:** In the file: Garside's attendance note from the day the first codicil was executed. Josiah's instructions noted; the reconciliation with Margaret Fairhurst recorded in one understated sentence ("Client has renewed relations with his niece Mrs. Fairhurst and wishes to provide for her issue in the event of her predecease"). The precision of "or her issue" noted as deliberate — not an oversight, but the professionally correct form for a collateral relative. Garside knew what he was doing.

6. **Garside displacement (first substantial instance):** Prescott reaches a letter from 1924 in which Josiah thanks Garside for attending to the codicil in terms that reveal the two men's long acquaintance. Garside has written it into the file with more care than a brief acknowledgment required. The file is a portrait of a meticulous man, and Prescott is reading it in the knowledge that this meticulous man will be dead before Christmas. He does not name this; he notes that the file is well kept, and returns to the task.

7. **Close:** Garside, passing through, asks what Prescott has found. Prescott says: nothing in forty years outside the firm; no private drafting; no hint of a change of mind in any letter or note. Garside says — and this is done in passing, without drama — that Josiah was not a man who did things unofficially. He moves on. The finding is now in the working note on the file.

---

## Outcome / Turn
The habit is established: Josiah Eccleston used this firm for every legal instrument of his adult life. There is nothing — not a scrap — to suggest he had ever made anything privately, and no correspondence supports the second codicil's cover story.

## New Question or Pressure Raised
If Josiah had no habit of private drafting, and his correspondence is silent on any change of mind, who is the second codicil's author?

---

## What Compounds

- **Door (f) — the habit:** Opens. The Eccleston file spans four decades of instruments, every one drawn by Garside & Prescott. No private drafting is evidenced anywhere. Josiah's own letters are silent on any change of mind after 1924. The habit of the man runs against the cover story.

- **Prior chapters advanced:** Ch 1 — the strong-room's completeness is confirmed from the archive side; Ch 2 — Walter's cover story (Josiah typed it himself at the mill) sits against a forty-year record of never having done such a thing.
