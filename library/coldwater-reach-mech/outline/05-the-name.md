# Chapter 5 — The Name

**One-line summary**: Marcus works at the document fragment in his room, determines the dead man's name matches someone he knew from Quebec, and writes — but does not send — a letter to a contact in Montreal.

**POV**: Dr. Marcus Klein
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: Marcus's room at the Crow house; the school (briefly). Late November–early December 1943.
**Chapter shape**: Interiority-heavy (Marcus at the desk, with his documents, working through the logic of what he knows; the school appears only as a frame for coming home)
**Ending mode**: Literary-fade
**Target words**: ~2,400

---

## Displacement Object

**The document fragment plus Marcus's unsent letter to Montreal** — he drafts a letter to his contact Simon Ehrlich, burns the first draft (too direct), writes a second draft disguised as an academic inquiry with a casual personal mention. He does not send it.

**Fact introduced**: The dead man's name, as far as Marcus can determine from the fragment, matches Josef Lutz — a specific person Marcus knew in the Quebec internment at Sherbrooke, a German-Jewish refugee who was released in late 1942/early 1943 and was attempting to travel to New York. Marcus has heard this name before; this is not a stranger.

**Named fact changed/recontextualized**: The dead man is no longer "unknown" in the way Rockland understands unknown. He has a name Marcus is almost certain of. He had a history, a trajectory, a purpose. He was not random; his presence on this coast was the result of a long journey.

**Object rhyme**: Marcus's unsent letter ↔ Hannah re-reading Karl's note (ch6): both characters are returning to their held objects, turning them over, deciding what the object means. Neither acts; both sit with it.

---

## Goal

Determine whether the name on the fragment belongs to the Josef Lutz he knew — and decide what to do with that determination.

## Obstacle

There is no safe way to investigate this. A German-speaking teacher writing to a man in Montreal about a Jewish refugee in the context of an unidentified body on the Maine coast is a letter no censor should find. Every step toward clarity is a step toward exposure.

## Action Beats

1. **The working week.** Marcus has had the fragment for ten days. He has attended school — taught his Latin and German sections, graded papers, been polite in the faculty room, walked home on Prospect Street. He has looked at the fragment each evening and closed the drawer.

2. **A Saturday morning.** Henry has gone to the school to fix the furnace register — Saturday maintenance. Lillian is out (the Congregational Ladies' Auxiliary, or an errand). Marcus is alone in the house. He has the whole morning.

3. **The systematic reading.** He takes the fragment out and, for the first time, works on it properly — scholar's attention. He has a magnifying glass (a small German-made lens he's carried since Vienna). He looks at every legible mark. Under the magnification, more emerges: not new words, but more certainty about the ones he could read. "J. Lutz" is unambiguous. The partial word he thought was *ankommen* (to arrive) is confirmed. There is a date fragment — October? A year? He cannot say. There is something that might be a city name — he reads it as "Qu—" (Quebec). And there is a phrase near the bottom, badly damaged: "...nicht mehr warten..." — wait no longer, or can wait no longer.

4. **The recognition.** He knows Josef Lutz. He has told himself he doesn't know; now he stops telling himself that. At Sherbrooke, in 1942, Josef Lutz was in the bunk two rows from Marcus's. Viennese Jew; trained as an archivist; spoke five languages. He was released in the spring (or fall) of 1942 under the sponsorship of a refugee committee. He told Marcus he had a cousin in New York, a man in the garment trade, who would take him in. He needed only to get there. Marcus shook his hand when he left the camp. He told himself — because it was what a person did to survive — that Josef had gotten there.

5. **The first letter draft.** Marcus takes a sheet of paper. He addresses it to Simon Ehrlich, Montreal, a man he knows from the camp who did reach Canada legally and who is now in the refugee aid community. He begins to write a direct inquiry: does Ehrlich know what became of Josef Lutz after his release? Is he in New York? Has he been in contact? He writes three paragraphs, clear and urgent, and then reads them back.

6. **The burning.** He reads the letter and understands it as a censor might: a German-speaking internee-refugee, now teaching in a Maine coastal town, writing to a man in Montreal asking about a missing German Jew in the context of an unidentified body found locally. He takes the letter to the kitchen and burns it in the stove.

7. **The second draft.** He writes again — this time in the form of an academic inquiry. He is writing to ask Ehrlich about access to German-language texts for the school's advanced Latin students (a plausible purpose — Ehrlich knows the community). In the second paragraph, almost as an afterthought, a personal aside: he has been thinking about mutual acquaintances from the Sherbrooke period and wonders if Ehrlich has had any word from Josef Lutz, who he believes was to go to New York; he has lost track and would welcome news. Casual. Unurgent. The kind of inquiry a man makes when he is thinking of old friends.

8. **He doesn't send it.** He reads the second version. It is better. It is not safe, exactly — but it is possible. He puts it in the drawer with the fragment. He does not address it. He does not walk to the post office.

9. **Evening.** Henry comes home from the school. Lillian starts supper. Marcus sets the table as he always does. Under the lamp, after the meal, he opens a student's Latin exercise to grade. The drawer is closed.

---

## Outcome

Marcus has confirmed, to his own satisfaction, that the name on the fragment belongs to Josef Lutz — the man he knew from Quebec. He has a letter that could begin to verify this, and he hasn't sent it. The fragment and the unsent letter are together in the drawer.

## New Question or Pressure Raised

What will it take to send the letter? And what will Marcus discover when he does?

## Continuity Notes

- Simon Ehrlich: fictional contact in Montreal — a German-Jewish refugee, formerly at Sherbrooke or another Quebec internment camp, now in Montreal's refugee aid community. He knows Josef Lutz; he will confirm the identification in chapter 8.
- Josef Lutz: Viennese Jew, trained as an archivist, fluent in German, French, likely others. Released from Sherbrooke (sponsored by a Quaker or refugee committee — similar to how Marcus arrived in Coldwater Reach). Had a cousin in New York in the garment trade.
- The fragment detail "...nicht mehr warten..." (can wait no longer) suggests Josef wrote this note recently, in urgency — he was trying to move before winter closed the routes. This is a small piece of his state of mind at the end.
- Marcus's magnifying glass: a small German-made lens, carried since Vienna. This is a personal object that characterizes him — it's a scholar's tool, worn and familiar, one of the things that survived all the crossings.
- The burning of the first draft: Marcus does this in the kitchen stove, cleanly, watching it. He is practiced at this kind of caution. He does not look guilty; he looks methodical.

## Craft Notes for the Drafter

- **The chapter is Marcus alone with his expertise.** His scholarly method — systematic, precise, building from observed evidence to conclusion — is what makes this chapter move. The interiority should feel like a mind working a problem, not a man feeling his feelings.
- **Literary-fade ending**: Marcus at the lamp, grading the Latin exercise, the drawer closed. The chapter ends on the routine act — the student's paper, the red pen — not on the drawer. The drawer is present in its absence.
- **Do not let Marcus name Josef Lutz's death explicitly.** He thinks around it — "the alternative was unthinkable, and Marcus had enough unthinkable things already." The fact of Josef's death should be something the reader has assembled, not something the prose states.
- **The burning of the first letter is not dramatic.** Marcus walks to the kitchen and burns it in the stove as a practical act. He is not tormented; he is careful. His register is the register of someone who has learned that efficiency is the only luxury available.
- **The Sherbrooke memory.** The chapter's one passage of genuine interiority — Marcus remembering Josef at Sherbrooke — should be brief and specific. One or two images: the bunk rows, Josef's habit of watching doors, the handshake at the gate. Do not explain what the camp was like; render one true detail.
