# Chapter 3 — Allgäu

**One-line summary**: On the Pfeiffer farm in November, Hannah and Karl exchange their first real words and he presses a folded note into her hand before the guard circles back.

**POV**: Hannah Pfeiffer
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: Pfeiffer dairy farm — barn corridor, yard, farmhouse evening. A Thursday in mid-November 1943, one of the last full workdays for the POW crew before the winter reduction.
**Chapter shape**: Single scene (sustained in the barn corridor and yard; farmhouse at the end)
**Ending mode**: Literary-fade
**Target words**: ~2,500

---

## Displacement Object

**Karl's note** — a small piece of paper folded four times, pressed into Hannah's palm during a moment when Carvell Oakes has his back turned. The note is in German, written in a careful hand. She reads it that evening in her room. The note contains Karl's brief self-account and a detail about a man on the road in the rain that Hannah does not yet understand.

**Fact introduced**: Karl Reinmar is from the Allgäu region near Marktoberdorf — approximately fifty kilometers from the Pfeiffer home village of Kempten. His note mentions he saw a man walking on the coastal road in the rain in mid-October, carrying something, moving toward the water. The detail is unremarked; Karl offered it as a small observation. Hannah files it.

**Named fact changed/recontextualized**: The possibility that Karl witnessed Josef Lutz in his final hours is planted. The reader will not be sure yet what this detail means; Hannah is not sure either.

**Object rhyme**: Hannah's received and kept note ↔ Eira's left-blank log entry — both are records that should go somewhere and do not. Three chapters in, all three threads' displacement objects have been introduced.

---

## Goal

Get through the workday without anything noticed — stay useful in the barn, keep the ordinary rhythm. She is aware of Karl in the way she is aware of weather coming.

## Obstacle

When she hears Karl speak to another POW in the Allgäu dialect — the German of her father's kitchen — she answers before she can stop herself. The word is already out.

## Action Beats

1. **The farm morning.** The POW crew arrives by Army truck at seven. Four men in PW armbands; Carvell Oakes descends from the truck cab stiffly. Hannah is carrying milk pails from the morning milking; she has been up since five. Werner is in the upper field. Marta is in the kitchen with the radio on. The crew disperses to their assigned work: fence-mending, manure hauling, the water pump repairs that have waited all autumn.

2. **The dialect.** Hannah is in the barn corridor, setting down the second pail, when she hears Karl speaking to another POW — a man named Brauer, older and silent. Karl says something in German that makes her stop moving. Not the words: the sound. The Allgäu vowel-shaping. The specific softening of consonants that is her father's German, the German of the kitchen when her parents think she isn't listening. She hasn't heard it from outside the house in years. She says something — one word, also in German — before she thinks.

3. **Karl stops. Looks at her.** Not with alarm — with a kind of recalibration. She is not just the farmer's daughter; she speaks. He answers her in the same dialect. They look at each other. Oakes is twenty feet away at the barn's far end, examining the water pump with someone's help. He is not watching.

4. **The exchange.** Brief, careful. Karl is from near Marktoberdorf. Hannah: her parents are from near Kempten — *Meine Eltern kamen 1922.* He absorbs this — the idea of a person from there ending up *here*, a farm girl in Maine speaking Allgäu German. He was conscripted at eighteen; he doesn't volunteer more than that. He asks if her father is — he pauses — still from there. She says: *Er ist immer noch von dort* (He is still from there). A pause. They both understand what that means in 1943.

5. **The detail about the road.** Then — not dramatically, just as a thing he mentions — Karl says he saw something odd in October, on the transport from Bangor. They drove the coastal road late at night, rain coming down, and he saw a man walking along the verge, moving fast, toward the direction of the water. *Older man. Light on his feet. Carrying something.* Karl mentions it the way you mention a strange bird you saw: a small thing, worth noting, already half-forgotten. He says: *Er schien einen langen Weg vor sich zu haben.* (He seemed to have a long way ahead of him.) Hannah notes it. Doesn't yet know why.

6. **The note.** Oakes begins to move back toward the barn. Karl, quickly, takes a folded piece of paper from inside his coat pocket — he had it ready; he must have prepared it before today — and presses it into Hannah's hand as she lifts the second milk pail. "Don't read it here," he says in English — quiet, precise — and moves away, back to the fence wire he was carrying.

7. **The rest of the day.** Hannah carries the pails. She works. She does not read the note. She can feel it in her apron pocket through the afternoon — the weight of it, almost nothing, but present.

8. **Evening: her room.** The farmhouse after supper. Werner at the radio; Marta at the dishes. Hannah goes upstairs. Under the lamp she unfolds the note. It is in German, written carefully in a small deliberate hand. Karl's name. His town. His conscription story in three sentences. A line about the farm — he says the cattle remind him of home; it is not a compliment so much as a fact. And at the end, the sentence about the man on the road: *Den Mann auf dem Weg habe ich noch in Erinnerung. Er schien einen langen Weg vor sich zu haben.* She reads it twice. She doesn't know what to do with it. She folds it and holds it in both hands.

---

## Outcome

Hannah has received the note; she keeps it. The line about the man on the road is recorded inside her, though she doesn't yet know what it points to. The braid's third displacement object is established.

## New Question or Pressure Raised

What was the man on the road? And what does Hannah do with a note in German from a German POW in her apron pocket?

## Continuity Notes

- Karl is from near Marktoberdorf; Hannah's family is from near Kempten — approximately 50km apart in the Allgäu region of Bavaria. Both characters would know the specific sound of that German.
- The other POW present: Brauer — older, silent, not a named character. The other two POWs are elsewhere on the farm (field work).
- Carvell Oakes: arthritic, not attentive. He circles back to the barn roughly every 20–25 minutes. Hannah has observed this.
- Werner is in the upper field during this exchange. He is not a witness to the note or the conversation.
- The note's physical description: small, folded four times, written in pencil on paper from a workbook or similar — the kind of paper available in a camp. Small handwriting, careful.
- The note's content in German (to be reproduced accurately in the draft): Karl's name, his town, his conscription (approximately three sentences), a line about the cattle and home, the sentence about the man on the road.
- **The man on the road detail**: mid-October; the coastal road; nighttime; rain; an older man, slight, carrying something, heading toward the water. Karl's transport was from Bangor — the coastal road near Wilder's landing (or a similar landmark). This maps to Josef Lutz's likely path on the night of October 2.

## Craft Notes for the Drafter

- **Open on Hannah at work, not on Karl.** The first paragraph should be the weight of the milk pails, the cold of the barn, the smell of manure and hay. The POW crew is present but background. Karl comes into focus as a sound — the dialect — not as a face.
- **German phrases without translation.** Per the voice guide: Hannah's German surfaces as the natural grain of bilingual thought. The exchange with Karl should include German that the reader can feel the meaning of from context. *Meine Eltern kamen 1922* needs no gloss. *Er schien einen langen Weg vor sich zu haben* (He seemed to have a long way ahead of him) carries its meaning in its weight.
- **The note-pressing should be fast and physical.** Not romantic — logistical. Karl had it ready; the gesture is rehearsed. That preparation is its own disclosure.
- **The evening reading is quiet.** Hannah alone, the lamp, the folded paper. No dramatic response to the man-on-the-road detail — she doesn't understand it yet. She folds the note and holds it. The chapter ends there — the farmhouse lamp, the note in her hands, the sound of the radio below.
- **Literary-fade ending**: The chapter ends on image and atmosphere, not action or revelation. The unresolved detail (the man on the road) is present in the reader's mind without being announced.
