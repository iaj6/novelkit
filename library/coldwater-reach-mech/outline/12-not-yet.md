# Chapter 12 — Not Yet

**One-line summary**: When Werner searches Hannah's room looking for his tobacco, he misses the note by inches — and that evening Hannah takes it out, holds it to the lamp, and puts it back.

**POV**: Hannah Pfeiffer
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: Pfeiffer farmhouse — Hannah's room; the kitchen; the barn at distance. A humid June afternoon, 1944.
**Chapter shape**: Interiority-heavy (the chapter's action is a single internal decision; the external event is brief and catalytic)
**Ending mode**: Literary-fade
**Target words**: ~2,300

---

## Displacement Object

**Karl's note** — taken from the lining at evening; held to the lamp flame; withdrawn before it touches fire; returned to the lining. The physical act of holding the note over the flame and pulling it back is the chapter's central action. After this, the note's status has changed: it is no longer something Hannah is holding by default. It is something she has explicitly chosen to keep.

**Fact introduced**: Werner searched Hannah's room (looking for tobacco — not for the note, and he didn't find it). The near-discovery changes the note's risk level. Hannah is now aware that keeping the note is an active choice with a cost. She makes it consciously.

**Named fact changed/recontextualized**: The note has become a commitment. Before this chapter: Hannah kept the note by not not-keeping it. After this chapter: she has held it to the flame and pulled it back. The keeping is explicit, named (to herself), and irreversible in its meaning. She is no longer a person who hasn't destroyed a note; she is a person who chose to keep one.

**Object rhyme**: Hannah's conscious keeping ↔ Eira's conscious non-writing (ch10: she fills out the form, checks "none," sends it — a deliberate act of concealment) ↔ Marcus's non-return of the fragment: all three characters have now named their withholding to themselves, even if not to others. The pattern of explicit choice—the moment when a passive omission becomes an active decision—runs through all three threads by this point in the novel.

---

## Goal

Keep the note safe. Keep the day ordinary. Keep Werner from knowing she knows anything.

## Obstacle

Werner is in her room for two minutes in the afternoon, looking for tobacco he thinks he left in his old coat pocket — the coat is in Hannah's closet, hung there last spring. He opens the closet, rummages in the coat, doesn't find it. He leaves. He does not search the room. But the school bag was in the corner of the closet, and for a moment his hand was near it.

## Action Beats

1. **The June afternoon.** Hot for Maine in June; the upper pasture haze. The POW crew is in the far field — Karl visible at distance, indistinguishable from the others. Carvell Oakes sits on the fence, hat over his face. Werner is somewhere in the barn; Marta is putting up rhubarb preserves in the kitchen.

2. **Werner comes upstairs.** Hannah is in her room, writing a letter to her cousin in Vermont (she does this occasionally — a real letter, ordinary life). She hears her father on the stairs — his tread is distinct. Her door is open. He looks in, says he's looking for the old barn coat, he thinks he left tobacco in the pocket. She says it's in the closet. He comes in, opens the closet.

3. **His hand near the school bag.** He finds the coat; he checks the pockets — no tobacco. He steps back and the school bag is on the floor of the closet beside his boot. He doesn't look at it. He says *"Danke, Hanna"* — her German name, which he uses without thinking — and goes back downstairs.

4. **Hannah doesn't move.** She stays at the desk, pen in hand, for a full minute after his footsteps fade. Then she sets the pen down and closes the closet door. Her letter to her cousin remains on the desk, half-finished, and she cannot go back to it.

5. **The rest of the afternoon.** She finishes the farm chores. The crew leaves at four. Karl does not look toward the house. The evening comes. Werner and Marta eat dinner; Hannah eats with them; no one says anything unusual.

6. **After supper.** Marta is in the sitting room with her mending; Werner has gone to bed early — he has been tired lately. Hannah goes upstairs. She takes the school bag down from the hook.

7. **The note comes out.** She opens the lining seam — she has done this enough times now that her fingers know it. She takes the note out, unfolds it. Reads it. Karl's side: his words, from November, the careful small handwriting. Her side: her transcription of his testimony, from March. The man on the road. Moving toward the water. *Sah sich um. Lief weiter.*

8. **The lamp.** She gets up and stands at the lamp on the table — the oil lamp, lit for the evening. She holds the corner of the note near the flame. Not touching — a centimeter away. The paper yellows at the edge. She holds it there.

9. **She pulls it back.** She folds it. She goes back to the school bag and puts the note into the lining and presses the seam back. She does not say to herself *I can't do it* or *I won't do it.* She does it — she keeps it — and then she goes to bed.

10. **The room at night.** The school bag on the hook. The lamp blown out. Through the window, the last bit of June light. The far field is dark. She can hear Marta's mending rhythm from downstairs — the faint click of the needle case.

---

## Outcome

Hannah has explicitly chosen to keep the note. The choice is now a fact about her, not just about the note. She is the keeper; she has named this, even without naming it aloud.

## New Question or Pressure Raised

She has chosen to keep it — but what does she intend to do with it? And how long can she keep it without someone finding it? The pressures of Werner's proximity and the note's content will converge in ch15.

---

## Continuity Notes

- Werner calls Hannah *"Hanna"* (the German form without the H at the end) — this is his private name for her, the name of the family and the language of the house. Hannah uses the English form of her name in public. The two forms signal the public/private divide throughout.
- The tobacco: Werner smokes a pipe; rationing has made this difficult. Marta disapproves of the smoking; Werner keeps the tobacco in various coat pockets to avoid the conversation.
- Karl in the far field at distance: Hannah can see the POW crew working from her room window. The distinction between "Karl at a distance, indistinguishable from others" and Karl in the barn corridor where they can speak is the geography of the thread — she has one register for the distance view and another for proximity.
- The letter to her cousin: a real domestic act that grounds the chapter. The cousin is in Vermont; the letter is ordinary. It remains unfinished on the desk.
- The near-discovery: Werner's hand near the school bag is not dramatized as a close call in the thriller sense. He is not searching for the note; he doesn't know it exists. The near-discovery is a structural fact that forces Hannah to confront the note's existence with new clarity.

## Craft Notes for the Drafter

- **The chapter's center is the lamp scene.** Everything else — Werner in the room, the afternoon, the supper — is a frame for the moment Hannah holds the note to the flame. The frame should be rendered efficiently; the lamp scene should be slow. Make the paper yellowing at the edge physical — the smell, the slight warmth, the decision in the fingers.
- **Literary-fade ending**: The last images: the school bag on the hook, the blown lamp, the June light through the window, the mending rhythm from downstairs. The chapter ends on atmosphere and quiet — no resolution, no announcement, just the house at night and the note inside its lining.
- **Hannah does not explain the decision.** She doesn't tell herself why she keeps it. She just pulls it back. The interiority is feeling and sensation, not analysis. If a sentence begins to explain the decision — if it reaches toward "because she thought" or "she understood now that" — cut it before it arrives.
- **The interiority-heavy shape.** The chapter's action is one internal choice. Everything else is texture. The reader's attention should be on Hannah's relationship to the object, not on plot events. This is the chapter where the reader fully understands Hannah's displacement object and what keeping it costs her.
- **Werner's German name for Hannah.** *"Danke, Hanna"* — simple, no dramatization. But the German of it should register: this is the language of the house, the private language, the one he uses without thinking. It is the language the note is written in. The two things are in the same register.
