# Chapter 14 — One Year

**One-line summary**: On the first anniversary of the body's discovery, Marcus walks to the mailbox on Prospect Street and sends a letter to Josef Lutz's sister in Zürich — one minimal truth, dispatched, irreversible.

**POV**: Dr. Marcus Klein
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: Coldwater Reach High School (fourth period); Crow house; Prospect Street. October 3, 1944.
**Chapter shape**: Multi-scene (school; home; the mailbox walk; back to the Crow house)
**Ending mode**: Coda
**Target words**: ~1,800 (compression chapter — the anniversary deserves quiet proportions)

---

## Displacement Object

**The letter to Josef Lutz's sister** — written over the previous weeks, addressed to a name Marcus tracked down through Ehrlich's network (a last-known address in Zürich; he does not know if she is still there). The letter says: her brother reached Maine; he was not forgotten; Marcus is sorry. It is signed with a partial initial and the phrase "ein Freund aus Sherbrooke" (a friend from Sherbrooke). He puts it in the mailbox on Prospect Street.

**Fact introduced**: Marcus has made his first act of outward disclosure — Josef's sister may now know that Josef reached Maine, and that someone here knew him. This is irreversible: the letter is in the mail. Whether it reaches her; whether it reaches a censor first; whether she is still in Zürich or has been displaced again — Marcus does not know.

**Named fact changed/recontextualized**: The secret has moved outward for the first time. Until now, Marcus's knowledge has stayed inside the Crow house (shared with Henry, ch11, but not documented or transmitted further). The letter is the first act of transmission. The withholding is not total anymore.

**Object rhyme**: Marcus's sent letter ↔ Eira's mailed inquiry form (ch10) — both are minimal truths dispatched, both are irreversible, both say just enough. The rhyme is structural: Eira's form said "none" (an official non-disclosure); Marcus's letter says "he reached Maine" (a private disclosure). Two different directions, same minimal shape.

---

## Goal

Get through the teaching day. Go home. Send the letter.

## Obstacle

The letter has been written for three weeks. It has been in the drawer with the documents. Sending it is a risk: if it is read by censors in the right (wrong) way, it places a German-speaking teacher in Coldwater Reach in connection with an unidentified drowned man. The risk is real. He is going to send it anyway.

## Action Beats

1. **October 3, 1944.** Marcus wakes up knowing the date. He has been aware of it for a week — the way a person is aware of a fixed point on the calendar. He gets dressed, eats the breakfast Lillian has made, walks to school.

2. **Fourth-period Latin.** His seniors — the small senior Latin class, five students. They are working through Vergil's *Georgics*, the farming poems, which feel apt for this coast. A student — a girl named Phyllis Bowen — is struggling with the dative of purpose construction. Marcus explains patiently. He is thinking about the letter. He explains it twice.

3. **After school.** He walks home. Prospect Street in October: the maples are going orange. Lillian is not home yet. Henry is at the school still, late in the day. Marcus goes upstairs.

4. **The letter.** He takes it from the drawer — it is on top of the fragment and the Montreal reply, where he put it three weeks ago after he addressed it. He reads it one last time. *"Your brother Josef was known to me from an earlier time. I have reason to believe he reached the coast of Maine in October of last year. I cannot tell you more than this without risking harm to others. He was not forgotten. He died far from where he was born, and I am sorry."* He has signed it: *"Ein Freund aus Sherbrooke."*

5. **He puts on his coat.** He takes the letter, goes downstairs, and walks to the mailbox on Prospect Street — the one at the corner, near the school. October afternoon, colder than it was a week ago. The maples have all gone orange. Two boys from the school are kicking something in the road ahead of him; they don't look at him.

6. **The mailbox.** He puts the letter in. The slot makes its sound. He stands for a moment. The letter is in there now, and it will go out with the evening pickup and then it will travel wherever it travels, and he cannot take it back.

7. **The walk home.** He walks back to the Crow house. The light is on in the kitchen — Lillian is home. He can smell something cooking. He goes in.

8. **The evening.** The Crow table; the food; Henry home by six. Marcus eats. He tells Henry about the student's difficulty with the dative of purpose. Henry mentions something about the furnace — it is always the furnace. Lillian laughs at something. The evening is ordinary.

9. **Before bed.** Marcus goes upstairs. He opens the drawer — the fragment, the Montreal letter. The slot where the letter to the sister was is empty now. He closes the drawer. He goes to bed.

---

## Outcome

The letter has been sent. The secret has moved outward, minimally, irreversibly. Marcus has done the one thing he can do for Josef Lutz — he has told someone who should know that Josef was not forgotten and did not disappear without a witness.

## New Question or Pressure Raised

Will the letter reach her? What happens if it is intercepted? And: has Marcus done enough — or is there more he could do that he is not doing?

---

## Continuity Notes

- Josef Lutz's sister: at a last-known Zürich address. Marcus obtained this through Ehrlich's network, apparently — the refugee community kept records of family members' last known locations. Zürich would have been a possible way-station or refuge for Jewish families with some means of travel. Whether she is still there in 1944 is unknown.
- The letter's text: given in this outline as Marcus's direct quotation. The drafter should use this text or very close to it — it should be simple, spare, and personal. Not eloquent; not literary. The letter of a man who has chosen his words very carefully.
- *"Ein Freund aus Sherbrooke"*: "A friend from Sherbrooke." This phrase identifies Marcus to anyone who was in the Quebec camp system and knew Josef — which is exactly the audience the letter is intended for, if it arrives at the right person.
- The Latin class: the *Georgics* of Vergil — the farming poems. This is historically plausible senior Latin material. The dative of purpose construction (*dativus finalis*) is a real grammatical challenge in Latin. No knowledge of Latin required on the reader's part; the teaching moment is brief.
- Phyllis Bowen: a student, female, one of five in the senior Latin class. She does not recur unless the drafter finds a use for her.

## Craft Notes for the Drafter

- **Coda ending**: The chapter is brief, quiet, and retrospective in feel — it marks a year; it records a single action. The coda shape means the chapter's main question (the sending of the letter) is resolved relatively early, and the rest of the chapter is the aftermath — the walk home, the dinner, the drawer. The chapter breathes slowly in the final scene.
- **Compression chapter (target ~1,800 words).** The shortness is deliberate — it gives the anniversary appropriate dignity without expanding it into more than it is. A year, a letter, an evening. That is all.
- **Marcus's interiority at the mailbox.** One or two sentences of inner life at the moment the slot closes. Not a long reflection — he is not a man who lets himself have long reflections at mailboxes. Something like: the slot's sound, and the letter now in a space he cannot reach, and that being the right shape for it.
- **The empty slot in the drawer.** The chapter's final image is not the mailbox but the drawer — the empty space where the letter was. This is Marcus's version of Eira's log blank: a space that marks what has been sent away.
- **October light.** The maples going orange; the light colder. This is one year exactly from the body's arrival. The season rhymes; the drafter should let it without underlining it.
