# Chapter 8 — Montreal

**One-line summary**: Marcus receives Ehrlich's reply from Montreal confirming that the dead man is Josef Lutz — a stateless refugee, not a sailor, a man who was trying to reach New York — and puts the letter in the drawer with the fragment.

**POV**: Dr. Marcus Klein
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: Crow house; the school (briefly, in morning). A Saturday in early March 1944.
**Chapter shape**: Multi-scene (the school day's end; arriving home; reading the letter; dinner)
**Ending mode**: Elliptical
**Target words**: ~2,400

---

## Displacement Object

**The fragment plus the Montreal letter** — two documents now together in the bottom desk drawer. The Montreal reply has confirmed what Marcus suspected; it has also added detail he did not expect (Josef's intended destination, the cousin in New York, the date of his departure from Quebec). Two documents, both held, neither filed.

**Fact introduced**: Josef Lutz — confirmed by Ehrlich. A stateless German-Jewish refugee. Released from Sherbrooke internment in late 1942. Traveled through Maine illegally, without papers, in autumn 1943. Was heading to an address in New York (a cousin in the garment trade). Was last known to Ehrlich's network as "somewhere in the Maritimes/Maine region" by September 1943. He did not arrive in New York. Ehrlich does not know why.

**Named fact changed/recontextualized**: The dead man on the breakwater is now confirmed as Josef Lutz by two independent sources — the fragment (Marcus's scholarship) and Ehrlich's community knowledge. The identity is established in Marcus's possession. Rockland has no idea.

**Object rhyme**: Marcus's two held documents ↔ Eira's two-layer log (the written entry and the crossed-out attempted entry below it, ch7): both characters now carry doubled records — something written and something attempted, something confirmed and something withheld. The pattern of layered documentation runs across threads.

---

## Goal

Marcus sent the letter to Ehrlich three weeks ago (between ch5 and this chapter — the sending happens off the page, between chapters, as Marcus crossed the threshold quietly). He is waiting for a reply, and today he gets one.

## Obstacle

The reply tells him more than he expected. It is not just a name confirmed; it is Josef's whole trajectory — the cousin's address in New York; the date he left Quebec; the fact that Ehrlich's network tried to track him and found nothing after September 1943. Marcus now holds Josef Lutz's final months in his hands. This is more than a document; it is a life.

## Action Beats

1. **The Saturday letter.** Marcus comes home from the school — he goes in on Saturday mornings to prepare the week's materials. Lillian is in the kitchen. On the hallway table, with the other mail, is an envelope with a Montreal postmark. Lillian has not opened it; she has not commented. He takes it upstairs.

2. **Reading the letter.** Ehrlich has written carefully — careful in the way of people who know their mail is read. The letter is framed as a response to Marcus's "academic inquiry" — a few sentences about German-language resources in Montreal (the fiction Marcus constructed). Then a paragraph, almost casual: yes, he remembers Josef Lutz from Sherbrooke; he was released in the autumn of 1942 (Ehrlich is specific about the date: November 1942). He had been trying to reach his cousin in New York for a long time. He left Montreal in early September 1943, heading south; Ehrlich had helped him with some of the practical details (he does not specify what, in writing). They expected word from him by November; they received none. Ehrlich writes: *"I had hoped he had simply lost touch. If you have heard anything, I would be grateful to know."*

3. **What Marcus understands.** He reads the letter twice. He understands everything it does not say: Josef traveled through Maine in September-October 1943. He was heading for New York along the coast — the only route available to him without papers. He arrived somewhere near this coast in October. And then there is the body on the breakwater, and the fragment with his name on it, and Ehrlich waiting for word. Marcus has the word. It is not the word anyone wants.

4. **He puts the letter in the drawer.** He takes the Montreal letter and puts it in the bottom desk drawer, under the fragment. They sit together — the original and the confirmation. He looks at them for a moment. He closes the drawer.

5. **The Sherbrooke memory.** (A brief passage — specific, not extended.) Marcus thinks about Josef at Sherbrooke: not the camp in general, but one specific thing — the way Josef hoarded books, borrowed every German-language text he could get in the camp, read them the way a man reads when he doesn't know if he'll have books next week. An archivist's instinct. A man who treated knowledge as the one portable thing. He got on a boat or a road and he tried to get somewhere and he didn't make it, and the thing he was carrying when Karl saw him on the coastal road was probably a bundle of papers and perhaps one or two books, the way an archivist always has one or two books. Marcus doesn't cry. He closes the drawer.

6. **Dinner.** Marcus goes downstairs. Lillian has made something with potatoes — it is always potatoes now, the last year's stored root. Henry is already at the table. The radio is in the next room, playing something from the evening news. Marcus sits down. He serves himself. He eats.

7. **After dinner.** The table is cleared. Henry reads the paper. Lillian does something at the kitchen counter. Marcus grades a stack of student Latin exercises at the corner of the table. He marks errors carefully. The drawer upstairs is closed.

---

## Outcome

Marcus now holds confirmed knowledge of the dead man's identity in documentary form. He has two documents, neither returned, neither filed. He knows Josef Lutz; he knows Josef is dead; he knows Josef's sister or family may be somewhere not knowing. He has done nothing with this knowledge.

## New Question or Pressure Raised

Ehrlich is waiting for word. Somewhere — in Zürich? in what remains of Europe? — Josef Lutz has a sister, a cousin, someone who should know. Does Marcus tell them? And if he does, how?

## Continuity Notes

- Ehrlich's letter is in the mail — it went through the domestic post, not V-Mail (V-Mail is for overseas military correspondence). It was postmarked Montreal; it arrived in the regular mail. Lillian saw the postmark.
- Josef Lutz's departure from Montreal: early September 1943. He would have had approximately six weeks of travel through Maine before the October gale. He spent time in transit, perhaps at way stations, perhaps sheltered by networks. Werner Pfeiffer was the final shelter (established later in ch17).
- Josef's cousin in New York: in the garment trade, in Manhattan. The cousin's name is not established in this chapter; Marcus doesn't know it. Ehrlich mentioned it obliquely.
- Marcus sent the Montreal letter between chapters 5 and 8, off the page. The drafter can acknowledge this briefly if necessary — "three weeks ago, he had finally addressed and mailed the letter to Ehrlich" — but it should be treated as a quiet act, not dramatized.
- Lillian's noticing the Montreal postmark: she doesn't comment. This is characteristic — she knows things she doesn't ask about.

---

## Craft Notes for the Drafter

- **The letter is the chapter's load.** Ehrlich's letter should be rendered — not the whole text, but the key paragraphs in Ehrlich's voice (slightly formal, careful, coded in its phrasing). The reader should feel they are reading Marcus reading it.
- **Elliptical ending**: The chapter ends not on revelation or resolution but on the ordinary action of grading Latin exercises. The ellipsis is not announced — the drawer upstairs is present in its absence, as the note in the lining is present in Hannah's chapters. The meal, the paper, the exercises are the chapter's last image.
- **The Sherbrooke memory is brief and specific.** One concrete detail — the hoarded books, the archivist's habit. No extended reverie. Marcus does not mourn in paragraphs; he mourns in the closing of the drawer.
- **Marcus's register**: the taxonomic interiority. He understands Josef's situation as a set of logical steps — the date, the route, the absence of word. He thinks it through like a map. The feeling is under the map. The drafter should let the map do the work and let the feeling stay under it.
- **Do not have Marcus plan what to do next.** The elliptical ending requires that no action is committed to by the chapter's close. He grades Latin. He doesn't decide to write to Josef's family. That comes later, off the page, and then in ch14.
