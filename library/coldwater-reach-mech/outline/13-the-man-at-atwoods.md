# Chapter 13 — The Man at Atwood's

**One-line summary**: At Atwood's general store on a Saturday errand, Eira looks at Werner Pfeiffer's back and shoulders and recognizes the figure she saw on the dock — and that evening writes an entry that does not name him.

**POV**: Eira Bowman
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: Atwood's general store; the main street; the harbormaster's shack. A Saturday in August 1944.
**Chapter shape**: Single scene (Atwood's, the walk back, the shack)
**Ending mode**: Turn
**Target words**: ~3,200 (high-pressure chapter — the major second-half recontextualization)

---

## Displacement Object

**The harbor log** — the entry Eira writes that evening, after seeing Werner Pfeiffer. The entry records the ordinary day: tide heights, wind direction, vessel movements. It does not record Werner Pfeiffer's name. It does not record that she has identified the second figure on the dock. This is the most consequential thing the log will ever not contain.

**Fact introduced / MAJOR RECONTEXTUALIZATION**: Eira recognizes Werner Pfeiffer as the second figure she saw on the dock on the night of October 2, 1943. She is as certain of this as she will ever be — certainty built on posture, proportion, the specific way a man holds his weight, not on a face. She will not pursue this; she will not confront Werner; she will not write his name. The chapter is the moment she knows.

**Named fact changed/recontextualized**: Everything the reader has built about the body's final hours now has Werner Pfeiffer in it. Werner was on the dock with Josef Lutz. Werner knew Josef (this the reader will learn in ch17); Werner guided Josef to the harbor to find a boat. Something happened after they arrived at the dock. This braids Eira's thread to Hannah's thread: the man whose farm Hannah is on, whose daughter is hiding a note that partially identifies Josef, was with Josef when Josef died.

**Object rhyme**: Eira's un-named log entry ↔ Karl's oral testimony that Hannah holds in the note — Werner is named in neither document. In the note: Karl saw a man walking, didn't know who sheltered him. In the log: Eira saw Werner, won't write his name. Both characters are protecting the same person without knowing the other is doing the same thing.

---

## Goal

Pick up the August ration books at Atwood's, get the kerosene order logged, return to work.

## Obstacle

Werner Pfeiffer is at the counter ahead of her.

## Action Beats

1. **The Saturday morning.** August in Coldwater Reach — the harbor is full, the summer lobstermen are at peak. Eira has been in the shack since six. Around ten she walks up the main street to Atwood's with the ration book and the kerosene order form.

2. **Atwood's: the queue.** Four or five people ahead of her at the counter. She takes her place. She is looking at the list of what she needs — kerosene, a spool of rope-repair twine — and not paying attention to the people in front of her.

3. **Werner at the counter.** She becomes aware of the man at the counter — not by seeing his face, which is toward the counter, but by the shape of him. Back. Shoulders. The particular distribution of weight — right shoulder slightly lower than the left; the way he stands in work boots with his feet at a small angle outward. She has seen this posture before. She is not certain, immediately.

4. **She places him.** It happens in the time it takes Werner to finish his transaction and pocket his ration book — perhaps thirty seconds. She is looking at his back, at his hands taking the book from the counter, at the turn of his neck. October, the dock, the dark, the figure at approximately twenty feet. She held the lantern in the shack doorway for a moment before going to bed; she saw two people on the dock, one of whom she did not recognize, one of whom she took for local. The local: this proportion, this posture.

5. **Werner turns.** He turns to leave and encounters Eira waiting in the queue. He looks at her — a flat, community-level acknowledgment. They know each other the way people know each other in a town of four hundred: by occupation, by location, by the faces in line at Atwood's. He nods, the way you nod at the harbormaster. She nods.

6. **He passes her and leaves.** The door. The sound of his boots on the step outside. Gone.

7. **Eira at the counter.** She buys the kerosene order and the twine. She thanks the man behind the counter — Atwood himself, or his son, whoever is there. She does not register what he says to her. She goes back out to the main street.

8. **The walk back.** She walks the main street toward the breakwater access. She does not hurry. She thinks — not in the sense of deliberating but in the sense of letting the knowledge settle into its shape. Werner Pfeiffer. The dairy farm three miles inland. She knows where he is from — the word went around the town early in the war, that the Pfeiffer farm had German ancestry, that they were watching for it. She never thought much about it. She thinks about it now.

9. **What she does and does not know.** She knows the figure on the dock was Werner Pfeiffer. She does not know why he was there. She does not know whether Josef died by accident or whether Werner had any agency in the death. She does not know whether Werner tried to save him. She knows only that Werner was there, and that Josef is dead, and that Werner has said nothing, and that she has said nothing, and that these two silences have been standing next to each other for ten months.

10. **She does not go to the constable.** She thinks about this — the way she thinks about anything procedural, as a checklist of what is required and what is available. She has no evidence beyond her own observation. She has an observation she didn't record. She is not certain she could pick Werner Pfeiffer out of a photograph. She is certain in her body, in her standing knowledge of shapes on docks in the dark. That is not a thing she can put in a report.

11. **The shack: the evening entry.** She sits at the desk. She writes the evening entry: August __, 1944. Wind WSW, 10 knots. Tide height. Two vessels in; one out. She writes it as she always writes it — complete, exact. She does not write Werner Pfeiffer's name. She closes the log.

---

## Outcome / Turn

The turn is the moment in Atwood's — the recognition. What had been a blank with a shape becomes a specific person. The log's blank now has a name inside it, even though the log will never hold that name.

The reader, post-ch9, knows that Karl saw Josef walking to the harbor; now the reader learns that Werner Pfeiffer was on the dock to receive him. The braid's two threads are now aligned — Hannah holds Karl's testimony; Eira holds Werner's identity — and neither of them knows what the other holds.

## New Question or Pressure Raised

Is Werner Pfeiffer dangerous? Does Eira think so? The answer (from what she observes and what she chooses not to do) is: no. Or: not in any way that would be helped by Eira writing his name in a legal record. This question — about what silence protects and what it enables — is the novel's deepest thematic pressure.

## Continuity Notes

- Werner Pfeiffer: mid-fifties, dairy farmer, Bavarian immigrant. His physical description: right shoulder slightly lower than the left; work-boot stance, feet angled out. These are specific enough to be recognizable but not photographic.
- Eira held a lantern in the shack doorway on the night of October 2 — briefly — before going inside. This is the observational basis for her recognition. She saw two figures on the dock at roughly twenty feet, in lantern light and gale darkness. She saw posture and proportion, not a face.
- Eira is as certain as she will be — which is not forensic certainty but working certainty, the kind she uses in the harbor to identify a vessel by its running light pattern or to gauge a boat's draft by its waterline. She is not guessing.
- Werner and Eira do not speak. They nod. This is a complete exchange for two people who know each other by community rather than by acquaintance.
- The chapter should not have Eira follow Werner or make any approach. Her decision not to act is built on genuine uncertainty (she cannot prove it) and on a judgment she does not name aloud.

## Craft Notes for the Drafter

- **High-pressure chapter (3,200 words).** The length comes from the slowness required: the recognition builds across thirty seconds of observation at Atwood's, and then the chapter has to carry the weight of what Eira knows all the way to the evening log entry. The walk back to the shack and the entry-writing give the recognition room to settle in.
- **Turn ending**: The turn is the recognition at Atwood's. But the chapter does not end there — it carries through to the evening entry, so the reader feels both the turn and its immediate cost: the log entry that doesn't say Werner Pfeiffer. The turn's meaning arrives in the gap between the recognition and the writing.
- **The recognition is physical, not dramatic.** Eira is a woman who reads the harbor by posture, load, draft, and sound. She reads Werner Pfeiffer the same way she reads a vessel — by the pattern of it, not by its name plate. The recognition should be rendered in working vocabulary: proportion, distribution of weight, stance. Not a gasp; a placement.
- **Eira does not deliberate about whether to confront Werner.** The checklist of what is available and required is brief and internal — she runs through it the way she runs through a channel-marker check, and the conclusion is the same: no action warranted. This is not reluctance; it is procedural judgment. The drafter should honor this.
- **The log entry at the end is the chapter's closing act.** Complete, exact, Werner Pfeiffer's name not in it. The entry should be reproduced in the prose — the specific columns, the specific measurements — so the reader feels the precision of what is written and the size of the blank it doesn't address.
