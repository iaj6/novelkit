# Chapter 2 — The Fragment

**One-line summary**: A naval officer brings Marcus a water-damaged scrap of German text found near the body; Marcus recognizes a name and does not report what he knows.

**POV**: Dr. Marcus Klein
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: Coldwater Reach High School (briefly), then Marcus's room at the Crow house on Prospect Street. Late October into early November 1943.
**Chapter shape**: Bottle (the chapter resolves to Marcus alone in his room with the document — one location by the end, near-real-time in the final scene)
**Ending mode**: Turn
**Target words**: ~2,400

---

## Displacement Object

**The water-damaged document fragment** — a piece of sodden paper, three or four legible words in German, brought to Marcus for translation and never returned to the officer who brought it. It sits on Marcus's desk by the chapter's end. The fragment is the first physical form of what Marcus knows and will not say.

**Fact introduced**: The document contains a partial name — "J. Lutz" — legible despite water damage, along with partial words suggesting a personal letter or note. Marcus recognizes this name from his fourteen months in the Quebec internment camp at Sherbrooke. The dead man may be Josef Lutz — a stateless German-Jewish refugee he knew there.

**Named fact changed/recontextualized**: The body on the breakwater is not anonymous in the way the official record suggests. It may carry an identity Marcus can read but will not file.

**Object rhyme**: Marcus's unreturned fragment ↔ Eira's unfiled blank — both are records that should move upward to authority and do not. The reader encounters this in sequence (ch1 → ch2) and begins to feel the pattern.

---

## Goal

Be helpful in a way that does not draw attention: translate the fragment as a professional service, return it to the officer, remain the useful German teacher and nothing more.

## Obstacle

The name on the fragment stops him. Recognizing it is the problem; once he recognizes it, he cannot unfeel it. Everything he has built in Coldwater Reach — the usefulness, the cover, the invisibility — depends on not being the kind of person who has complicated knowledge. He is now that person again.

## Action Beats

1. **The visit to the school.** A young naval officer — Lieutenant Graves, from the Rockland district office, perhaps twenty-six, uncomfortable in this small-town school hallway — is directed to Marcus's classroom at the end of the fourth-period Latin class. He carries a small paper envelope. He explains: the county examiner found a fragment of paper in the clothing of the unidentified man from the breakwater. It's in German. They've been told the school has a German teacher. He doesn't look at Marcus in a way that suggests anything beyond the errand.

2. **The fragment is produced.** Marcus opens the envelope. The paper is roughly three inches by two — torn rather than cut, the edges dissolved. The ink has run badly; most of the text is illegible. Marcus holds it near the window light. He can make out: a partial salutation or heading; two words in the body of the text ("...kalte..." — cold — and something like "...ankommen..." — to arrive); a name in what appears to be the heading or address block: "...Lutz, J..." The rest is gone.

3. **The oral translation.** Marcus tells the officer, in measured terms: it appears to be a fragment of a personal correspondence, possibly a letter or a note; the legible words suggest something about cold weather and arrival; there is a partial name — a surname, "Lutz" — and an initial, J. He cannot read more; the damage is too extensive. If there is anything further he can attempt, he says, he is available.

4. **The officer leaves the fragment.** He says they'd appreciate it if Marcus would work on it further — see if there's more that can be recovered. A few days, if possible. He leaves the envelope with Marcus.

5. **Marcus in his room at the Crow house.** That evening, after supper — Lillian has made a potato soup; Henry and Marcus spoke briefly about a broken furnace register in the school — Marcus goes upstairs. He takes the envelope from his coat pocket. He sits at the desk.

6. **Recognition.** Under the lamp, working slowly, Marcus looks at the fragment again. The name "J. Lutz" is clear to him now — he has been looking at it all afternoon, and looking away. Josef Lutz. A man Marcus knew from the Sherbrooke internment camp: a Viennese Jew, slightly younger than Marcus, quiet, with a gift for languages and a habit of watching doors. He was released from Sherbrooke in early 1943 — or late 1942; Marcus cannot be sure. He had no valid Canadian papers. He had no American visa. He had a cousin somewhere in New York. He was trying to get there. Marcus had thought about him occasionally since, and decided he had made it, because the alternative was unthinkable, and Marcus had enough unthinkable things already.

7. **The choice.** Marcus does not pick up the telephone. He does not go to the school to send a message to Lieutenant Graves. He looks at the fragment — holds it under the lamp, as if reading it will change what it says. He puts it in the bottom drawer of his desk.

---

## Outcome

Marcus is in possession of a document he has not returned, which may identify the dead man, and has told the officer nothing of what he knows. He cannot explain why Josef Lutz was in Maine without explaining things about himself he cannot explain.

## New Question or Pressure Raised

Is the dead man Josef Lutz? And if he is, what does it mean — what did it cost Josef to get this far, and what will it cost Marcus to know it?

## Continuity Notes

- Lieutenant Graves: young, naval, from Rockland district office. Not antagonistic; routine. He will not recur unless the drafter wishes.
- Marcus was interned at Sherbrooke, Quebec — a real internment camp under the War Measures Act (the fictional name is fine as long as it's Quebec and under the War Measures Act; the drafter should not name real historical figures).
- Josef Lutz: German-Jewish refugee, approximately 40–45, Viennese. Released from Quebec internment, last known to Marcus in 1942/early 1943. Marcus believed he had reached safety. The name on the fragment reopens that assumption.
- Marcus's room: a single upstairs room at the Crow house. A desk, a lamp, a window looking toward the school. Books stacked — Latin grammars, a German dictionary. He keeps his papers in a bottom desk drawer.
- Lillian and Henry are present but not in the room with Marcus. The house is warm; the stairs creak; Marcus can hear them below. Their presence is physical without being intrusive.

## Craft Notes for the Drafter

- **Open in the classroom.** The chapter begins with the end of Marcus's fourth-period Latin class — students leaving, the smell of chalk, the coal furnace. The officer arrives into this residue of ordinary work. The contrast between the school's routine and what the officer brings should land in the texture of the scene, not be announced.
- **Marcus's interiority is taxonomic.** He thinks in categories, structures, the anatomy of problems. His recognition of the name does not come as an emotional blow — it comes as a classification: *this is the category of thing he feared; this is the specific instance of it.* The feeling is underneath the taxonomy, not on top.
- **The fragment's physical condition matters.** The drafter should make the paper feel real — the dissolved edges, the way the ink has run to brown shadows, the words that are legible only because the ink was pressed harder in those places. The reading of the fragment is an act of physical attention.
- **The turn at the end.** The chapter's turn is interior: Marcus looking at the fragment in the drawer knows he will not retrieve it tonight, or tomorrow. He has made a decision without naming it as one. The chapter does not name it either.
- **Marcus's register**: complete subordinate-clause sentences; precise vocabulary; wary of his own expertise. He is polite to the officer in the way of someone who has learned to be exactly as useful as required and no more. The chapter should feel controlled even as something is slipping.
