# Chapter 17 — What Henry Knows

**One-line summary**: Henry tells Marcus that Werner Pfeiffer sheltered the man from the breakwater one night before the storm — he heard it through the town network; Marcus goes upstairs and spreads his documents on the desk and looks at the whole story in paper.

**POV**: Dr. Marcus Klein
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: Crow house porch and front steps; Marcus's room. A Saturday evening in late March 1945.
**Chapter shape**: Single scene (the porch conversation; Marcus going upstairs)
**Ending mode**: Resolved-beat
**Target words**: ~3,200 (high-pressure chapter — the full assembly; the sub-arc closes)

---

## Displacement Object

**The documents spread on the desk** — the fragment, the Montreal letter, and Marcus's private notebook (a small ledger or exercise book in which he has been writing his reconstruction of Josef's journey over the past months). After Henry's disclosure, Marcus goes upstairs and takes everything out of the drawer. He spreads the papers on the desk. He reads through them. He adds one entry to the notebook: Werner Pfeiffer's name; the date he sheltered Josef; the farm; the connection to the dock.

**Fact introduced**: Werner Pfeiffer sheltered Josef Lutz for one night before the October gale — Werner knew him, or knew of him, from Bavaria. He guided Josef toward the Coldwater Reach harbor to find a boat. Henry heard this from the town's informal network (the African American community and Congregational network in Coldwater Reach, which observes and shares information through channels the white community doesn't attend to). Henry has held this information since winter.

**Named fact changed/recontextualization**: Marcus can now assemble the complete picture of Josef Lutz's final days: Quebec internment → illegal travel through Maine → sheltered by Werner Pfeiffer (a Bavarian who knew him or his family) → guided to the Coldwater Reach harbor → walked to the dock (Karl's testimony via Hannah, though Marcus doesn't know this) → found dead on the breakwater the next morning. Marcus holds this reconstruction. He cannot file it. He cannot report it. But he knows it. The sub-arc closes.

**Object rhyme**: Marcus's documents spread on the desk ↔ Karl's oral testimony about Josef that Hannah holds in the note: both records contain Josef's final movement, assembled from different angles. Neither record is in official hands. The reconstruction exists only in private documents and private memory — it is the novel's form of truth, as opposed to the official record, which holds "Unknown Man, October 1943."

---

## Goal

Come home from school, have the weekend, rest.

## Obstacle

Henry is on the porch in a warm spell, which is unusual. He says: "I heard something."

## Action Beats

1. **The warm spell.** Late March 1945 — the war in Europe is close to its end; the daily news is of the Allied advance into Germany, the Rhine crossing. There is a warm Saturday — briefly, the sun has actual heat in it. Henry is on the porch when Marcus comes home at four, which is unusual; Henry does his outdoor sitting in summer. Marcus takes this in without asking.

2. **"I heard something."** Henry says it the way he says everything — as a fact placed into the conversation. Not urgent; not dramatic. Marcus sits down on the porch steps.

3. **Henry's account.** Henry explains: he heard it through Deacon Pratt at the Congregational Church, who heard it from Edna Wills, who does laundry for several farm families inland. Wills was told, at some point last winter, by a woman at the Pfeiffer farm — possibly Marta Pfeiffer, possibly a hired girl — that in October, before the big storm, a man had stayed a night at the Pfeiffer farm. A foreign man. A man who spoke the same German as Werner. Werner knew him, or knew who he was. Werner had given him a meal and a dry place to sleep and had driven him in the morning toward the coast road, toward the harbor.

4. **The connection.** Henry says: "I don't know if that's the man from the breakwater." His voice is entirely level. He is not theorizing; he is reporting what he heard. Marcus says: "I think it is." A pause. Henry nods.

5. **Henry asks nothing.** He does not ask how Marcus knows. He does not ask what Marcus is going to do with this. He does not ask about the documents in the drawer upstairs. They sit together on the porch for a while. A car goes by on Prospect Street. The maple has a little green coming in at the branch tips.

6. **What Henry understands.** Marcus thinks about Henry — not what Henry knows or doesn't know, but what Henry has made of his knowledge. Henry has been carrying this information since winter without telling Marcus. He told him today. Marcus thinks: Henry told him because Marcus told him in May that he knew the man. Henry has been waiting for the right time to return what he knew. This is a form of exchange — not simultaneous, but balanced.

7. **Marcus goes upstairs.** Henry stays on the porch. Lillian is inside starting supper. Marcus goes upstairs to his room.

8. **The documents on the desk.** He takes everything out of the bottom drawer: the fragment; the Montreal letter from Ehrlich; his private notebook. He spreads them on the desk. He looks at them.

9. **Reading through.** He reads through the notebook — the reconstruction he has been building over eighteen months. Josef's name. Sherbrooke. Released November 1942. Montreal. The network that helped him move. Early September 1943: left Montreal heading south. The coastal route. The fragment, found in his clothing at the breakwater. Ehrlich's confirmation: no word received from Josef; last known location, Maine coast. And now: the farm. Werner Pfeiffer. One night before the storm. The harbor road.

10. **He writes one entry.** He opens the notebook to the last written page. Below his most recent entry he writes, in German, careful and small: *"16. März 1945. Werner Pfeiffer, Landwirt, Kaltbach [the German version of the town name; he can use 'Coldwater Reach'], hat Josef Lutz eine Nacht beherbergt, vor dem Sturm. Hat ihn zum Hafen gefahren. Dies aus zweiter Hand gehört; nicht bewiesen; wahrscheinlich wahr."* (March 16, 1945. Werner Pfeiffer, dairy farmer, Coldwater Reach, sheltered Josef Lutz one night, before the storm. Drove him to the harbor. This heard second-hand; not proved; probably true.)

11. **He looks at the full picture.** Josef reached Maine — via Montreal, via Werner Pfeiffer's farm — and walked to the dock, and died. Whether Werner was there when he died, Marcus doesn't know. Whether it was accident or something else, Marcus doesn't know. What he knows: Josef was trying to get somewhere, and he got as far as the dock at Coldwater Reach, and someone was with him, or had been.

12. **He puts the documents back.** He closes the notebook. He puts everything back in the drawer. He closes the drawer.

13. **Supper.** He goes downstairs. Henry is inside now. Lillian has made something with potatoes and salt fish. They eat. Marcus eats everything on his plate. The radio is on in the next room; the news is about the advance on Berlin.

---

## Outcome / Resolved-Beat

Marcus's sub-arc closes: he now holds the complete picture of Josef Lutz's final journey, in the form of his documents and his notebook. He cannot file these; he cannot report them without explaining himself. But the reconstruction is done. He is the keeper of Josef's story, in private, and he has done what he can: sent the minimal truth to the sister; shared the knowledge with Henry; built the record in his notebook. The sub-arc question — "what happened to Josef Lutz, and why is Marcus the one who knows?" — has been answered, imperfectly, privately, sufficiently.

## New Question or Pressure Raised

What does Werner Pfeiffer know about what happened on the dock? What did he see? This will not be answered. The novel's deliberate residue — the unresolvable question at the dock — stays open. Marcus knows what he knows. The rest is not his to know.

## Continuity Notes

- Deacon Pratt, Edna Wills: minor fictional figures in the town's social network. The African American and working-class community in a small Maine town in 1944 would observe and share information through informal networks distinct from the white professional community. Henry is part of this network.
- The information chain (Marta/hired girl → Wills → Pratt → Henry → Marcus) is indirect. Henry acknowledges this: he does not know if it's the man from the breakwater. He is reporting, not asserting. This is important — Marcus confirms it, not Henry.
- Marcus's notebook: a small ledger or exercise book, in German, that he has been keeping since approximately ch8 — he began writing down his reconstruction after the Montreal confirmation. It is not a diary; it is a working document, like a scholar's research notes.
- Werner's action: he sheltered Josef and drove him to the harbor in the morning. Whether Werner was present when Josef fell — whether Werner was on the dock — is not established by Henry's account. (Eira saw Werner on the dock that night; but Eira doesn't know Marcus, and Marcus doesn't know Eira.) The reader, having followed both threads, can assemble this. Marcus cannot.
- The spring 1945 timeline: this chapter is set in late March 1945. The war in Europe will end May 8, 1945 (VE Day). The news of the Allied advance into Germany is background.

## Craft Notes for the Drafter

- **High-pressure chapter (3,200 words).** The length earns itself through the density of what must be assembled on the page — Henry's account, Marcus's upstairs reconstruction, the notebook entry, the full picture. This is the chapter where the reader assembles Josef Lutz's story as completely as the novel ever allows.
- **Resolved-beat ending**: The chapter ends with the drawer closed and Marcus at the dinner table. Not on a revelation — the revelation happened upstairs. The resolution is the ordinariness of supper: potatoes, salt fish, Henry's newspaper, the advance on Berlin. The sub-arc closes quietly.
- **Henry's account should be specific and restrained.** He is not a dramatic storyteller. He reports what he heard, attributing each step of the chain. He doesn't draw conclusions; he lets Marcus draw them. The conversation on the porch should feel like two careful men exchanging facts.
- **The notebook entry in German.** Write it out in Marcus's POV — in his precise, scholarly German. The drafter should render this as Marcus writing it, word by deliberate word. It is the novel's most complete private record.
- **What Marcus doesn't know.** The chapter should not resolve what happened on the dock — whether Werner was present, whether Josef's death was accident. Marcus acknowledges this gap. The reader has assembled more than Marcus has; this is the reader's reward for following all three threads.
- **The porch geography.** Henry on the porch; Marcus on the steps. A domestic image — two men on the porch of a house that has held Marcus carefully for two and a half years. This should feel like what it is: the steadiest place Marcus has.
