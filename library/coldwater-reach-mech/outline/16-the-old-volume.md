# Chapter 16 — The Old Volume

**One-line summary**: The current log volume is nearly full; Eira takes the old one down from the shelf, opens to October 1943, and writes one line of weather into the blank before stopping.

**POV**: Eira Bowman
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: Harbormaster's shack; the breakwater. A January evening, 1945 — clear and very cold.
**Chapter shape**: Interiority-heavy (Eira alone in the shack with the two log volumes — the old and the new; the old volume and its blank)
**Ending mode**: Literary-fade
**Target words**: ~2,300

---

## Displacement Object

**The harbor log — the old volume** — taken from the shelf, opened to October 1943, the blank found again. The entry Eira begins to write: "October 2, 1943. 2300 hours. Weather: NE gale, 35 knots, continuing." She writes these words and stops. She cannot write what follows — the dock, the figures. She closes the volume. She puts it back on the shelf. She opens the new volume and begins the evening's entry.

**Fact introduced**: After fifteen months, Eira has written something into the blank — one line of weather. The blank is no longer entirely blank. She has committed a partial entry — three facts (date, time, conditions) — and stopped before reaching the dock. The partial entry is now in the log.

**Named fact changed/recontextualized**: The blank has changed state: from empty to partially filled. This is different from absent. A future reader of the log will find an entry for October 2 at 2300 that consists of weather conditions only — no vessel, no person, no event. This is more information than before, and different information than the truth.

**Object rhyme**: Eira's partial entry ↔ Marcus's partial disclosure to Josef's sister (ch14): both are the minimum true thing that can be said. Marcus sent Josef's location but not his name to Eira. Eira writes the weather but not the dock. The pattern of minimum truth — enough to be technically accurate, insufficient to be complete — runs across the final section of both threads.

---

## Goal

Retire the old log volume — file it on the shelf with Owen's volumes — and begin using the new one properly.

## Obstacle

Before filing it, she opens to October 1943. The blank is still there, with the crossed-out attempted entry from January 1944 below it. She has a pen. Fifteen months have passed. She is going to try again.

## Action Beats

1. **January 1945.** The harbor is in its deep-winter quiet. The last few pages of the current log volume are being filled in. She ordered a new volume from Rockland in December; it arrived last week. She needs to file the old volume.

2. **The old volume.** She takes it from the desk and runs her thumb along the pages to Owen's section and then hers. She knows this volume — its weight, its scuff marks, the way the cover has warped slightly from the damp. She opens it.

3. **Finding the blank.** She turns to October 1943. Her October 3 entry: the body, the finding, the notification. Above it: October 2. The gale, the channel-closed notice, and below that — the blank at 2300, with the crossed-out entry from January 1944 beneath it. She reads what she crossed out: "October 2, 1943. 2300 hours. Weather—" and then the single crossing line and the correction notice. She reads it.

4. **She picks up the pen.** This time she doesn't think about it the way she thought about it in January 1944. She picks up the pen and writes, in her usual hand, below the crossed-out entry: "October 2, 1943. 2300 hours. [Correction to above entry.] Weather: NE gale, 35 knots, continuing." She writes this. She stops.

5. **What would come next.** The next thing that should be written: dock observation. One vessel. One person. Two people. She holds the pen. She has the words. She has been carrying them for fifteen months; she knows exactly what she would write. She doesn't write it.

6. **She closes the volume.** She puts the pen down. She closes the old volume. She holds it for a moment — the weight of three years in it, Owen's and hers. Then she gets up and puts it on the shelf next to Owen's volumes. The oldest: 1933. Then 1934–1935 (Owen's last). Then 1936 (the first year of hers). On down to this one.

7. **The new volume.** She opens the new volume at the desk. She writes the evening's entry: January --, 1945. Wind NNW, 20 knots. Clear. No vessel traffic. Tide at --.

8. **She blows the lamp.** She sits for a moment in the dark. The shack is cold; the lamp was doing what it could. Through the small window, the harbor in the January dark — the channel markers' lights, steady and correct. She has kept them right for nine years.

9. **She goes out.** Not because she needs to — she checked the mooring lines at four. She goes out and walks the breakwater in the cold dark, to the end, and looks at the water. Routine check. She turns back.

---

## Outcome

The old volume is on the shelf. It contains, now, a partial entry for October 2 at 2300: weather conditions only. The entry is accurate as far as it goes. It is the first true fact Eira has written into the blank in fifteen months.

## New Question or Pressure Raised

What would it take to write the rest? The dock, the figures. Is there a version of the log that could ever hold those facts? (This becomes the subject of ch18, when Eira finally writes the dock entry — minimal, without Werner's name — in the old volume before the war ends.)

## Continuity Notes

- The log volumes on the shelf: Owen's begin with 1933 (his earlier ones are presumably stored elsewhere or lost). Owen's last volume covers up to September 1935. Eira's first volume begins in January 1936. The volume being retired in this chapter covers roughly 1942–1944. The new volume will begin January 1945.
- The crossed-out entry from January 1944 (ch7): when Eira tried to write the entry then, she wrote "October 2, 1943. 2300 hours. Weather—" before crossing it out. This is in the legal record — still visible below the crossing line. Now, in ch16, she is writing below that crossing-out, in the correction format. This is legally awkward — three attempts at the same entry — but she is doing the best she can within the form.
- The correction format: in a legal log, a crossed-out entry is documented, and a correction follows. Eira is following this format, which means there is now: (1) the original blank, (2) the January 1944 attempt, crossed out, (3) the January 1945 correction — which is just weather. A very attentive reader of the log would find this sequence curious. No one is reading the log attentively except Eira.
- Winter 1945 harbor: quiet, frozen on the flanks, fewer vessels, long nights. The shack is heated by a small coal stove that requires tending. This is Eira's winter routine.

## Craft Notes for the Drafter

- **Literary-fade ending**: The chapter ends on Eira outside, walking back from the breakwater's end. Not on the log; not on the blank. The ending is the harbor at night, the channel markers, the cold. The work resumes; the log is on the shelf; the blank is partially filled. The chapter ends on the ordinary and the quiet.
- **The shelf of Owen's volumes.** This is an important image — the shelf is the physical history of the harbor, the record of two people's work. When Eira puts the old volume on the shelf next to Owen's, she is placing herself in a sequence. The drafter should render this act slowly and physically: the shelf, the volumes in order, the weight of the old one.
- **The pen pausing after the weather.** The moment when Eira writes "Weather: NE gale, 35 knots, continuing" and stops should be rendered in real time. She has the pen; she has the words that would come next. She doesn't write them. The pause should be held — not explained, not dramatized, just present.
- **Owen is present in the shelf.** His handwriting, his volumes, the warp of his binding. Eira puts her volume next to his. The comparison she made in ch7 (his thoroughness versus her blank) is present here but does not need to be restated. The shelf is the comparison.
- **Interiority-heavy, but spare.** Eira's interiority is always short and factual. The chapter is interiority-heavy in the structural sense (it is primarily about an internal act and its meaning) but the prose should stay in Eira's register — observation, procedure, the language of work. No extended reflection.
