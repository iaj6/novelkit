# Chapter 9 — The Road

**One-line summary**: With Oakes dozing in the wagon shed, Hannah asks Karl directly what he saw on the coastal road; his account establishes that Josef walked to the harbor under his own power — recontextualizing everything the reader believed about the body's arrival.

**POV**: Hannah Pfeiffer
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: Pfeiffer farm, barn corridor and yard. A Wednesday afternoon, late March or early April 1944. The POW crew has returned after the winter reduction.
**Chapter shape**: Single scene (the brief window of privacy in the barn; then Hannah alone at the kitchen table that evening)
**Ending mode**: Turn
**Target words**: ~3,200 (high-pressure chapter — the midpoint recontextualization)

---

## Displacement Object

**Karl's note, now written on both sides.** Karl wrote on one side in November; Hannah writes on the other side tonight, at the kitchen table, recording what Karl told her: the time (approximately 2200–2300 hours), the location (the coastal road near Wilder's Crossing), the man's description (older, slight, carrying something, moving fast, heading toward the water), and Karl's last observation (the man turned and looked at the truck and kept walking). The note is now a two-sided document — his writing and hers.

**Fact introduced / MIDPOINT RECONTEXTUALIZATION**: Josef Lutz walked to the Coldwater Reach dock under his own power on the night of October 2, 1943 — he was alive and ambulatory at approximately 2200-2300 hours, heading toward the harbor of his own volition. He was not carried or disposed of; he was going somewhere. This collapses the possibility that the dock was a dump site and establishes it as a meeting place or a destination. What happened at the dock was a meeting — or an accident — not a disposal.

**Named fact changed/recontextualized**: The reader's working theory about how the body came to the breakwater is overturned. Josef arrived at the dock. What happened after he arrived is still unknown.

**Object rhyme**: Hannah's two-sided note ↔ Eira's two-layer log (the official entry and the crossed-out attempted entry below it): both documents now carry more than their original writing; both record something in two hands, two moments, two intentions. The braid's three displacement objects have all been doubled or layered by the midpoint.

---

## Goal

Get Karl alone long enough to ask him directly what he saw — and whether it connects to what she has been thinking about since December.

## Obstacle

Oakes's schedule is consistent but not guaranteed. Werner is in the upper barn that afternoon. The window of privacy may be ten minutes; it may be five. The question she wants to ask could get Karl interrogated, transferred, punished. She cannot ask it casually.

## Action Beats

1. **The crew's return.** The POW crew has been back since the first of March — the farm's spring labor starts early. Hannah has watched Karl for two weeks, waiting for Oakes to drift. Today, Wednesday, Oakes gets his flask from the truck cab around two-thirty and carries it to the wagon shed. Hannah has seen him do this before; she has twenty minutes, perhaps.

2. **The barn corridor.** Karl is sorting harness in the barn corridor, working alone — the other POWs are in the upper field with Werner. Hannah comes into the barn with a feed bucket she doesn't need. Karl looks at her. Neither of them speaks first.

3. **She shows him the note.** She unfolds it — holds it out, his side facing him, the side he wrote. He looks at it for a moment. He looks at her. She says, in German, keeping it level: *"Das hier. Der Mann auf dem Weg. Erzähl mir davon."* (This. The man on the road. Tell me.)

4. **Karl's account.** He sets down the harness buckle. He is quiet for a moment — she can see him deciding. Then he tells her. It was a Thursday night — or Friday — in October. The transport truck was returning from Bangor; they'd been doing a work assignment at a cannery and were being brought back to the main camp. Rain all evening. The road was bad. The truck slowed at Wilder's Crossing because there was a fallen branch across the verge. Through the rain-streaked window, on the left side of the road, Karl saw a man walking.

5. **The description.** Older than Karl — perhaps fifty, perhaps older. Slight in build; not a farmer's build. He was wearing a coat that was wrong for the weather — a good coat, but not an oilskin. He was carrying something — a bag or a bundle, held close. He was moving fast for a man on foot in the rain, moving with purpose. He was heading — and Karl pauses here, places himself in the truck's geography — *toward the water*. Toward what you could tell was the direction of the coast.

6. **The detail that matters.** The truck slowed for the branch; Karl had maybe ten seconds of visibility. The man turned and looked at the truck — directly at it, through the rain. He looked and then turned away and kept walking. He did not stop; he did not flag the truck; he was not in distress. He was going somewhere.

7. **"Was es der Mann?"** Karl asks. *Was it that man?* His German is quiet; Oakes is in the shed across the yard. Hannah is looking at him. She says: *"Ich glaube schon."* (I think so.) Karl absorbs this. He doesn't look away.

8. **Karl: "Was denkst du, was passiert ist?"** (What do you think happened?) She says she doesn't know. She means it. She knows only that the man walked to the dock. What happened on the dock — she didn't see it; no one she knows of saw it; the body was found in the morning.

9. **Oakes in the yard.** They hear Oakes's boots on the wagon shed threshold. Karl picks up the harness. Hannah picks up the feed bucket. They are working when Oakes rounds the corner.

10. **That evening: the kitchen table.** The farmhouse after supper. Werner is at the radio again; Marta has gone to bed early with a headache. Hannah sits at the kitchen table alone, the lamp turned low. She takes the note from the school bag. She unfolds it. Karl's writing on one side.

11. **She writes.** On the other side — in small, careful German letters, her own hand — she writes what Karl told her. The date (approximately). The location: *Wilder's Crossing, Küstenstraße* (Wilder's Crossing, coastal road). The time. The description of the man. *Bewegte sich Richtung Wasser. Sah sich um. Lief weiter.* (Moving toward the water. Turned to look. Kept walking.) She writes it precisely, the way she was taught to take notes at school. She folds the note.

12. **The realization.** She holds the note — both sides. Karl's writing tells her who the note came from; her writing tells her what the note now contains. She is holding a record of the last known sighting of a man who was found dead the next morning. Karl's testimony. The only witness, as far as she knows. In her hands. In a school bag lining on a hook in her farmhouse.

---

## Outcome / Turn

The midpoint recontextualization: Josef Lutz walked to the dock. He wasn't brought; he went there. The dock was a destination. What happened at the dock — who was waiting, what went wrong — remains open. But the theory that the body was disposed of there is now impossible. He chose to go there, or was going to meet someone there. Eira's blank — the figure she saw on the dock with Josef — becomes a new kind of fact: Josef arrived alive, with someone. Something happened between their being together and the body in the morning.

The note has become a different object: no longer just a personal letter from a POW, but a record of the last known sighting of a dead man. Hannah knows what she is holding now.

## New Question or Pressure Raised

What happened on the dock between Josef's arrival and his death? Eira's blank becomes the next necessary fact.

## Continuity Notes

- Wilder's Crossing: a fictional place name on the coastal road between Bangor and Coldwater Reach. It is a crossroads or landmark on the route the POW transport would take.
- Karl's account: Thursday or Friday night, approximately 2200-2300 hours, late October (the gale was October 1-3). Karl is not certain of the exact date; the transport schedule from Bangor may let him narrow it to a few possible nights. He is fairly certain it was within the week before the body was found.
- Karl does not know the man died. This is confirmed when Hannah says "I think so" in response to his question — Karl is inferring the death from Hannah's reaction. He does not press.
- Hannah's written account (on the reverse of the note) is in German. Specific key words should appear in the draft: *Wilder's Crossing* (or the German equivalent she'd use — she might write the English place name in German letters), *Küstenstraße*, *einen langen Mantel* (a long coat), *Richtung Wasser*.
- The note's new status: it is now a two-sided document in two hands. This is the central accretion of the midpoint chapter.

## Craft Notes for the Drafter

- **This is the longest chapter (3,200 words).** The length is earned by the scene's stakes: two people speaking carefully in a language that could expose both of them, in a window of maybe fifteen minutes, about a dead man. Every beat should feel consequential.
- **The barn corridor scene should breathe slowly.** Don't rush to Karl's account. The approach, the silence, the decision to show him the note — all of this earns the account when it comes. Karl's telling should feel careful, deliberate, trustworthy.
- **Karl's German.** He speaks to Hannah in German; she speaks back. The dialect is the Allgäu German they share. The drafter should include German phrases and let them carry their meaning from context. This is not a translation exercise; it is the natural texture of two people using their best shared language.
- **Turn ending**: The turn arrives when Hannah, at the kitchen table, understands what the note now contains. The chapter ends not on action but on comprehension — she knows what she is holding. This should be rendered briefly, without explanation: *she held the note and knew what it was now.* No more.
- **Hannah's interiority is associative.** She doesn't analyze; she feels first. The moment of understanding at the kitchen table should come as a physical sensation — the weight of the folded paper, the way her hands know something before her mind names it.
- **High-pressure pacing.** The barn scene needs the ticking clock of Oakes's imminent return. This should be felt in the scene's rhythm — not named (don't write "she knew she had only minutes"), but present in the dialogue's brevity and Karl's careful word choices.
