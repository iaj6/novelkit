
## 01-the-breakwater
**Summary:** Dawn, October 3, 1943. Eira Bowman walks the breakwater post-gale, finds an unidentified male body on the seaward face at approximately 210 feet from the pier head. Male, 50–60, no papers, non-fisherman clothing (wool overcoat, wool trousers, leather shoes, no oilskin). She checks his pockets — empty — and observes his face: not local, possibly European. She walks back to the shack, hangs Owen's coat, and opens the log. She writes the October 3 body entry completely and precisely. Then she faces the blank at October 2, 2300 hours, and writes only a weather notation there: "Weather: NE gale, 35 knots decreasing at first light. Channel: temporarily impaired." She radios the District Commander in Rockland; calls Constable Tibbetts. Tibbetts examines the body ("Jesus") but does not touch it. County examiner arrives at 1:30 p.m., removes the body. Alone at dusk, Eira reads back through October 2, sees her notation, reads the body entry, and closes the log.
**New facts:**
- Body found seaward face of breakwater, approximately 210 feet from pier head, October 3, 1943 at 0615 hours.
- Body: male adult, estimated 50–60 years, no papers or identification.
- Body clothing: wool overcoat, wool trousers, leather shoes. No oilskin. Not fishing or working-coast dress.
- Eira's assessment: body's facial features suggest non-local, possibly European origin; she cannot say more.
- Sea has had the body several days or more at time of discovery.
- October 2 entry in harbor log: blank at 2300 hours. Eira writes only a weather notation: 'Weather: NE gale, 35 knots decreasing at first light. Channel: temporarily impaired.'
- Eira reports to District Commander at Rockland by radio; constable Aldous Tibbetts called by telephone.
- Constable Tibbetts arrives 40 minutes after call. First word: 'Jesus.' Does not touch the body.
- County examiner arrives 1:30 p.m. with two assistants and stretcher; removes body; files probable-drowning report; no papers.
- Owen's coat is dark, wool, large on Eira — wide at the shoulder, cuffs past her wrists. She buttons it from the bottom up.
- Owen's coat hung on the back of the cottage door (not the shack). Eira moves it only twice since 1935.

## 02-the-fragment

Marcus gives Graves the translation (cold and arrival, surname Lutz, initial J.) and accepts the fragment for further examination. Graves leaves his number with the school secretary. Josef had no valid Canadian papers after release (late 1942 or early 1943), no US visa, had a cousin in New York he was trying to reach. Marcus has told himself for two years that Josef made it. ## 03-allgau
**Summary:** Thursday, mid-November 1943. Hannah carries milk pails in the cold barn while the POW crew disperses to farm work; Werner is deliberately in the upper field; Marta is at the kitchen radio. Karl is working in the barn corridor with Brauer when Hannah hears him speak in the Allgäu dialect — the German of her father's kitchen — and says one word (*Gell?*) before she can stop herself. They exchange names and geography in low Allgäu German: she is from near Kempten, he from near Marktoberdorf — about fifty kilometers. Karl mentions his conscription at eighteen (he had wanted to be a carpenter), then, as a small observed thing, describes seeing an older man on the coastal road in the rain in October — light on his feet, carrying something, moving toward the water. Hannah files the detail without knowing what it points toward. When Oakes begins to circle back, Karl presses a small folded note into her palm — 'Don't read it here' in careful English — and turns away. Hannah carries the note all afternoon without opening it. That evening in her room under the lamp she reads it: Karl's name, town, three sentences of conscription story (including his plan to be a Schreiner), a line about the cattle reminding him of home (not a complaint), and the final two sentences about the man on the road. She reads it twice, folds it along its creases, and holds it in both hands. The chapter ends on that image.

- Object rhyme: Karl's note (received and kept, a record of private knowledge) rhymes with Eira's harbor-log blank (a record left deliberately incomplete) and Marcus's unreturned fragment (a document not passed to authority). All three are things that carry knowledge and do not go where they are supposed to go.

## 04-the-commander
**Summary:** Tuesday, mid-November 1943. Commander Horace Willard of Naval District Rockland visits the harbormaster's shack to take Eira's formal report on the October 3 body finding. Eira prepares with care — real coffee, chairs arranged correctly — and gives her account precisely. Willard reads through October 2 and October 3 in the log; the blank is protected by the weather notation she wrote, and he has no reason to look for a gap. He produces the county photographer's image of the dead man; Eira looks at it carefully and says she does not know him — this is technically true: she saw a living man at distance and at night; the photograph shows the dead. Willard asks the close question ("Any vessel traffic the night before? Night of the second?"); Eira says: "Gale conditions all through the second. Channel was flagged. No vessel traffic." She logged none. He accepts this and moves on. Willard delivers the official disposition: no match on merchant marine rosters, naval registries, or missing-persons reports; the man will be interred in the Coldwater Reach town cemetery as "Unknown Man, October 1943"; the case is closed unless new information comes in. Eira is told to contact Rockland if anything surfaces. He leaves. Eira opens the log and puts her thumb on the 2300 line. She closes the log.
**New facts:**

- The county photographer's image of the body exists; Eira was shown it for the first time during Willard's visit.
- Willard's office ran the body description against merchant marine rosters (Allied and German), naval registries (American and German), and civilian missing-persons reports (Maine and eastern Canada coast, four months prior). No match on any.
- Official disposition: the body will be interred in the Coldwater Reach town cemetery as 'Unknown Man, October 1943.' The case is closed from Rockland's perspective absent new information.
- Eira is asked to contact Rockland immediately if any information surfaces — a missing report, a letter, anything.
- The blank at October 2, 2300 has now survived official review. The Commander read the 2300 weather notation and turned the page; the log gave him no reason to look for a gap.

## 05-the-name

## 07-owens-pages
**Summary:** January 1944. Eira is in the harbormaster's shack overnight, monitoring channel ice — the number-two marker needs watching through a hard freeze. Between the midnight check and the 4 AM check, she takes the old harbor log volume (the one containing the October 1943 blank) from the shelf and reads through it. She turns through her own entries, then into Owen's years — his heavier-pressed hand, his entries going down complete in the first writing, never a correction. She finds the November 19, 1933 storm entry, which she has opened to many times before: Owen logged an unlicensed vessel making for the cove (J. Cates, lobsterman; reported to Rockland 11/21/33; cost the man a week's hauling rights). Owen wrote it because it happened; there was no column in his log for observed-but-not-entered. After the midnight check, she turns to October 1943 in the old volume. October 3: the full body entry — she reads it through, it is a good entry, correct in every particular. Above it: October 2. The 2300 weather notation. The gap. She picks up the pen and writes 'October 2, 1943. 2300 hours. Weather—' and stops. She understands what would come next: dock, male observed, approximately 2310, alive, European features, not alone, second figure she believes she recognized. She cannot write this now because writing it means explaining the October blank, and explaining the blank means naming the choice. She had told herself it was waiting, not deciding. It is January now. She draws a single line through what she has written, initials it (E.B.), and writes below: '[Entry error — see correction below.] Ice report, 0015 hours: Number-two marker holding. Ice: flanks, three-quarters inch. Channel: open.' She dates it. She sets the pen down. The entry is now in the record — crossed out, visible to anyone who reads October 2 closely. She sets the old volume on the shelf, looks at the lamp. Owen's pages are there. She is not Owen. She knew this in January 1936; she knows it differently now. She sits until four, then goes out for the final check (ice not crossed), logs it, waits for dawn, writes the morning report, and goes out to check the tide.
