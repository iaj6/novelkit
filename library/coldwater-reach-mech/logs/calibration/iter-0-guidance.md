# Drafter guidance — Coldwater Reach

## Voice register

This is restrained American literary fiction in the tradition of quiet WWII home-front novels — the register of Marilynne Robinson's *Gilead* (accumulative quiet, the weight of withheld things) crossed with the procedural precision of Annie Proulx's Maine work (place and work rendered exactly, without sentiment). The default literary register is largely correct here, but with three critical constraints that override its defaults: (1) each chapter must be anchored in a concrete physical object; (2) each chapter must change or recontextualize a named fact; (3) each of the three POV voices must be distinguishable from the first paragraph without relying on the character's name. The literary attractor's weakness — abstract interiority circling without landing — is this novel's specific failure mode.

## Sentence-length expectations

- **Eira chapters**: Average 10–16 words. Short declaratives are the default rhythm. Longer sentences are earned by complexity of observation, not of feeling.
- **Hannah chapters**: Average 13–20 words. More associative linking. Sentences can be longer when they are following feeling rather than reporting it.
- **Marcus chapters**: Average 14–22 words. Periodic sentences permitted for suppressed intensity — the main clause deferred. But no sentence should be longer than 35 words; beyond that length he is lecturing rather than thinking.
- Run of 3–4 short declaratives before varying is natural in Eira's chapters; in Hannah's and Marcus's, 2–3 short sentences followed by a longer one is the rhythm.

## Chapter-opening conventions

The first paragraph establishes physical location and the character's body in it before doing anything else. No chapter opens on abstraction, memory, or theme. Eira chapters open in the middle of a procedure or physical observation. Hannah chapters may open on sensation (temperature, sound, the quality of light) as long as a body in a place follows within the first three sentences. Marcus chapters may open on an object he is handling or a room he is entering. The opening paragraph should feel like the register of the chapter before the chapter's name appears.

## Chapter-ending conventions

This is a quiet literary braid; endings are not cliffhangers by default. Target distribution across 18 chapters:

- **Literary-fade**: 5 chapters (ending on image, object, or sensory close; used when an emotional beat has landed and needs air)
- **Turn**: 4 chapters (a new fact or recontextualization that lands in the chapter's final beat — the reader assembles something)
- **Resolved-beat**: 3 chapters (a sub-arc closes; used sparingly to give the reader air before a new pressure)
- **Elliptical**: 4 chapters (deliberate unresolved gesture — a question not answered, an action not completed — especially appropriate for withholding chapters)
- **Coda**: 2 chapters (brief aftermath beat; useful after a high-pressure scene)

**Do not cluster the same ending mode in consecutive chapters.** Two literary-fades in a row are the braid going slack; two turns in a row are the braid sprinting past the reader. Vary deliberately.

## POV strategy

One anchor protagonist (Eira) with two co-leads of nearly equal weight (Hannah, Marcus). Rotation is motivated, not cyclical — a run of two consecutive Eira chapters is permitted when her arc requires it; a run of three in any POV should be rare and deliberate. The braid's contrast engine is the rotation: after Eira's spare procedural voice, Hannah's more porous interior feels expanded; after Hannah's feeling, Marcus's taxonomic precision feels controlled. Design rotation so each voice provides relief and contrast from the previous.

Do not let any POV go more than three chapters without appearing. At 18 chapters, each character gets approximately 6 chapters.

## Dialogue density

Dialogue is low-frequency, high-freight. In a typical chapter, there may be 2–5 exchanges of dialogue; some chapters have none. Dialogue tags are spare: *said*, *asked*. Occasionally a single adverb when it genuinely changes meaning (*quietly*, *in German*, *without looking up*). No interpretive glosses after dialogue: no "which meant," "as if to say," "her tone implying." What the dialogue means must live in the exchange itself and the physical context around it.

Silence is a dialogue move. A character not answering is an answer. A scene that ends before someone speaks is a choice.

## Displacement object discipline — the most important rule for this draft

**Every withholding chapter must give the withheld thing a concrete physical object.** The drafter must identify this object before writing the chapter and anchor at least three moments in the chapter to it — the character sees it, touches it, moves it, avoids it, or thinks about its location. The object is not symbolic decoration; it is the withholding made physical, the thing the reader can track when the POV character cannot explain.

Object assignments by thread (establish early, return to; these may be refined by the plotter's chapter map):
- **Eira's primary object**: the harbor log — specifically a given entry or blank space in it
- **Hannah's primary object**: the object received from Karl (its nature is a slow reveal; once established, it must remain consistent)
- **Marcus's primary object**: the document/letter/photograph he cannot file

When a chapter handles an object from another thread (an echo or rhyme), note it explicitly in scene-log: "this chapter's object rhymes with [X's object] because both are things that should be sent and are not."

## Anti-defaults — what the drafter must actively avoid

1. **Do not write a withholding chapter without a physical object.** "A feeling she could not put down" is not a displacement object. A blank in the log, a button in a coat pocket, a photograph behind a loose board — these are displacement objects.
2. **Do not let a chapter be atmosphere only.** If the reader could remove the chapter and lose no changed fact and no advanced object, the chapter is orbiting. Every chapter must contribute to accretion.
3. **Do not gloss the scene after it ends.** The paragraph following a freighted exchange or discovery must not name what just happened. Cut it.
4. **Do not let Marcus's precision tip into self-explanation.** He can name the structure of what he observes; he cannot name the emotion underneath it.
5. **Do not add German phrases in Hannah's chapters for local color.** They appear because her thought ran there first. If the German has no specific semantic work to do — if it is decorative — cut it.
6. **Do not soften the not-meeting constraint.** The three POV characters do not meet as a group, ever. They may intersect in pairs at moments the story requires. Do not invent a scene where all three are present.
