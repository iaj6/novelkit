# World — Coldwater Reach

## The town and harbor

Coldwater Reach is a working fishing and lobstering town on the Maine coast, small enough that everyone knows which windows are dark and why. Population roughly four hundred, down from five hundred since the draft began pulling men. The harbor sits at the foot of a bluff; the main street above it holds a post office, a Congregational church, Atwood's general store (rationing tickets in a ledger under the counter), a diner called Murphett's that closes at two in the afternoon because there is nothing to cook, and the high school — a two-story brick building that smells of chalk and coal heat. The harbormaster's shack is nailed to the breakwater pier, weatherboard, one oil lamp, the log on the desk.

The breakwater itself is the physical center of the book: a granite seawall extending three hundred feet into the channel. In storm weather it is the first thing battered. The body comes in against its seaward face, October 1943.

## Geography

The Pfeiffer dairy farm sits three miles inland up a gravel road. The farmhouse is white clapboard; the barn unpainted; the fields run to a woodlot. The German POW work crew — four men and an aging Coastal Patrol guard — arrives by Army truck on weekday mornings and departs before dark.

Marcus Klein boards in the Crow house, a two-story frame house on Prospect Street, near the school — maintained with the quiet pride of people who cannot let anything fall into disrepair without it being noticed and read.

There is one road in, one road out. The nearest city with a newspaper of record is thirty miles.

## Period and material culture (1943–1945)

The war is ambient, not plotted. It lives in:

- **Rationing**: sugar, meat, gasoline, rubber; ration books are a weekly arithmetic problem. Atwood's keeps the ledger.
- **V-Mail**: letters to men overseas are photographed onto microfilm, re-enlarged at the receiving end; the paper they come back on feels wrong — smaller, lighter, the handwriting faintly ghostly.
- **Casualty telegrams**: delivered by the Western Union boy on a bicycle. When he turns down your road you go still.
- **The radio**: evening news in kitchens across town. Eira does not own one; she hears it through other people's walls.
- **The POW labor program**: authorized under the Geneva Convention; German prisoners from camps in the interior are transported to farms and canneries under minimal guard. Pay rate set by government. Not secret, but not widely discussed. The Pfeiffer arrangement is legal, logged, and watched — loosely.
- **Coastal Patrol / Civil Air Patrol auxiliaries**: older men, medically unfit men, doing beach watch and perimeter duty. The guard on the Pfeiffer farm, an arthritic man named Carvell Oakes, sixty-one, carries a rifle he has not cleaned in months.
- **Harbormaster duties**: logging vessel arrivals and departures, maintaining navigational aids, reporting unusual findings to the District Commander in Rockland. The log is a legal document. What goes in it and what does not is consequential.

## Mood and atmosphere

Autumn mud. The smell of diesel and seawater. Wood smoke in November. The particular silence of a place where the able men are gone — the town runs on women, old men, children, and the bodies the government sends in. Days are short by October. The light off the water in late afternoon is silver and horizontal and very cold. People are not unfriendly, but they watch newcomers with the careful attention of a community that cannot afford to be wrong about strangers.

There is no spy thriller here. The war does not generate plot mechanics. It generates weather — a constant pressure on ordinary life that makes ordinary choices harder.

## What the prose renders as lived texture (not set decoration)

Real Maine harbor procedure. The way a POW crew integrates into a farm morning. The weight and smell of a harbor log. What a displaced person in a small American town feels when they walk into a room and every conversation stops. Period vocabulary (coal oil, the Merchant Marine, the District Commander at Rockland, PW armbands, Form DD-4 for POW labor requisition) appears because characters live inside it, not to signal research.
