# Reader guidance — Coldwater Reach

## Intended audience

Adult readers of literary historical fiction; readers drawn to quiet, accumulative novels set during WWII on the American home front — the company of *Suite Française*, Kent Haruf, and Marilynne Robinson. Readers who are comfortable with novels that do not resolve cleanly, that trust them to assemble meaning from juxtaposition, and that treat withholding as a narrative method rather than a failure to deliver. Not genre thriller readers. Not readers who need a mystery plot resolved in full.

## Brief-adherence checks to perform

For each chapter reviewed, the Reader must answer yes or no to:

1. **Does this chapter handle a concrete physical displacement object?** The object must be physically present (touched, moved, hidden, destroyed, returned to) at least once. If the chapter withholds through atmosphere or feeling alone, without an object, the answer is NO.
2. **Does this chapter change a named fact about the breakwater body, OR recontextualize a fact the reader already holds, OR advance a displacement object's meaning?** If the chapter does none of these three, it is an "orbiting" chapter — flag it explicitly by that name.
3. **Is the POV register distinguishable from the first paragraph, before the character's name appears?** Eira = sparse procedural; Hannah = porous associative with German; Marcus = precise formal taxonomy. If the register is generic, flag it.
4. **Does any dialogue exchange carry an interpretive gloss after the fact** ("which meant," "as if to say," the narrator explaining what the dialogue revealed)? Flag each instance.
5. **Does the chapter end with a post-scene paragraph that names the subtext?** The final paragraph (or penultimate) must not explain what the scene was about. If it does, flag it.
6. **Do the three POV characters remain appropriately separated?** Check for any scene where all three are simultaneously present — this is a hard constraint violation.

## What "good" looks like for this book

A good chapter:
- Opens with a body in a specific physical location doing a specific thing
- Handles its displacement object in at least two distinct moments across the chapter (it appears, it is moved, it is almost discovered — not just mentioned once at the start)
- Ends with the reader holding one changed fact or recontextualized understanding they did not have at the chapter's start
- Allows the POV character's register to carry the emotional weight without naming the emotion
- In Eira's chapters: the sea and the log do the work feelings cannot; in Hannah's: a German word does the work an English sentence avoids; in Marcus's: an analytical framing stands in for what he cannot afford to feel

A good Eira chapter reads like a harbor log with a lie of omission in it that you can only see by knowing what the log never does. A good Hannah chapter feels like being bilingual in a room where only one of your languages is safe. A good Marcus chapter reads like a man explaining everything except the one thing that would explain everything else.

## What to flag explicitly if the manuscript drifts

**Flag as "ORBITING"** any chapter that cannot supply both a handled object and a changed/recontextualized fact. This is the named failure mode this manuscript variant was built to prevent. Do not soften this flag.

**Flag as "ATMOSPHERE ONLY"** any chapter whose primary contribution is mood, tone, or environmental texture without a concrete narrative or object advance. Atmosphere is texture; it is not structure. The reader already has the town's mood by chapter 3; re-establishing it without adding to it is orbiting.

**Flag as "REGISTER SLIP"** any chapter where:
- Eira becomes reflective or metaphorical about the sea
- Hannah's German phrases feel like local color rather than cognitive necessity
- Marcus explains his own emotions directly rather than naming the structure of his avoidance

**Flag as "SUBTEXT NAMED"** any instance where the prose explains what a scene or exchange meant. This includes: the final paragraph of a chapter naming the emotional content; a passage that tells the reader what a silence or refusal "really" meant; any narrator-level generalization about war, grief, or belonging that arrives outside a character's consciousness.

**Flag as "OBJECT ABSENT"** any withholding chapter where the withholding is accomplished through abstract circling — "a thing she could not say," "a weight she could not name" — without a physical object carrying the weight. Abstract withholding is the one thing this variant was explicitly designed to eliminate. Flag it, name it, and recommend the specific object that would repair it.

## What to applaud

1. **The register distinction holding across all three voices.** When Eira's spare procedural, Hannah's porous associativity, and Marcus's taxonomic precision are each recognizable from the first paragraph of their respective chapters — without the prose having to announce them — this is the manuscript working at the level the brief demands. Defend this if it is achieved; call it out explicitly.

2. **The object rhymes.** When one thread's displacement object echoes or rhymes with another's — both are "things that should be sent and are not," both are handled in the same way across different contexts — and the reader can feel the pattern without it being named: this is the braid doing its structural work. If the reader phase can point to two specific objects in two different threads and trace the rhyme, name it as a success.

3. **Restraint that earns its silence.** When a chapter ends without explaining what just happened, and the withholding feels like weight rather than evasion — when the reader feels the unspoken rather than frustrated by its absence — the prose has done something hard. Call it a success when you see it.
