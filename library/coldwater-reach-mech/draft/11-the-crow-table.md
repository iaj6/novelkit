# Chapter 11 — The Crow Table

The walk home from the school took twelve minutes at ordinary pace — up Prospect Street from the building's front steps, past the intersection at Dunbar, past the Welliver house and the elm that overhung the corner-lot fence, and to the Crow house on the right.

In May the walk was different from what it had been in March. The light came in at a lower angle now and reached the full width of the street in the late afternoon, and the air had a layered quality he did not try to name, the structural cold of a long winter conceded but not yet gone, the warmth of something gathering underneath it. The mud at the road's edge had been solid since before January and was no longer solid. He walked with his coat open. He came through the gate at the Crow house — the latch giving with its familiar slight resistance, the same resistance it had had since the September afternoon in 1942 when Henry had shown him the room and asked no further questions about the Quaker organization or about why a language teacher from Vienna had come by way of Paris, London, and a camp in Quebec to a fishing town on the Maine coast — and he went up the front walk and came through the door.

Lillian was in the kitchen. He knew this before he heard her. The house had a different smell than it had on ordinary evenings — not the coal heat and the particular mustiness of the old wood floors, which he could have described with some precision by now, but something warmer laid over them. He set his briefcase against the wall and hung his coat on the hook and stood in the hallway.

Chicken. Something with chicken.

He had not made a note of any occasion.

Henry was in the sitting room with the newspaper folded across one knee. He looked up over the top of it.

"Evening."

"Evening," Marcus said. "You came back early."

"Three o'clock as usual." Henry turned a page. "You're late."

"The composition prompts," Marcus said. "Third period. I stayed to mark them."

"How'd they do."

"Better than November."

Henry received this and went back to the newspaper. The room settled. From the kitchen came the steady sound of Lillian doing something with water, and then the click of an oven handle, and then quiet. Marcus took the armchair on the near side and sat with his coat across his knees. The school had produced a composition from the second-year German class on the theme of eine Reise — a journey — and the results had been, as the results usually were, a demonstration of the gap between what a student knows in English and what they can render in German, the imagination outrunning the grammar, the subordinate clauses failing at the verb. He had marked them in the faculty room before gathering his things and was not, now that he was in this room with Henry's newspaper turning and the smell of whatever Lillian had in the oven, going to think about them further until morning.

The light in the sitting room came in at the west window from the last of the afternoon sun and fell across the floor in the way it fell in May and nowhere else — a quality of going-away that was not yet dark.

Henry turned another page.

---

They ate at half past five.

The chicken had come, Lillian said, from the Dunbar property. The neighbor three houses up had a laying hen that had stopped laying, and Mrs. Dunbar had preferred to make some use of it before summer than not; what had been given in return was not specified and Marcus did not ask. It was a good meal — chicken jointed and roasted with dried herbs from the pantry, potatoes, the first of the spring greens from the kitchen-garden bed under the south window. Marcus served himself and ate.

Henry mentioned the furnace.

"There was a memo," he said. "Second one. The first one said the budget was under review for April. This one says they reviewed it."

"And the determination?"

"The furnace is adequate."

"For whom?"

Henry considered. "For the principal's office, I would guess. The third-floor corridor's a different question. The third-floor corridor doesn't vote on budget determinations."

Marcus said he had expected the determination. Henry said it was the determination that had always been made in the history of the school's furnace and would continue to be made in the history of the school's furnace until the furnace failed at a moment that could not be deferred or reframed. He ate potato. Marcus said this was the administration of all things. Henry agreed it was. He poured water into his glass and set the pitcher down near the center of the table.

Lillian mentioned, not quite toward the end of the main course, that the Ladies' Auxiliary was doing another wool collection. Third one since November, which had required reclassifying the third as a spring drive, since the previous two had not met the quota and the wool needed was still needed. Socks, she said. Always socks. She mentioned this without particular emphasis, in the way she mentioned things at the table that were observable facts of the town's winter and were now observable facts of its spring. Marcus asked what the deadline was. She said the fourteenth of the month. He asked whether there was a drop point or a collection. She said the church vestibule, Saturdays, which she preferred because it meant she could do it after the marketing and not have to make a separate trip.

He asked something else, he thought — something appropriate, one of the questions that were appropriate when something had been mentioned and acknowledgment was expected without inquiry being intrusive. He ate. Henry ate. The radio in the next room was off; Henry had not turned it on this evening, which was not unusual for a Tuesday.

He was thinking, at a low level of his attention, about the composition on eine Reise. A student named Carroll had written, with some confidence, about a journey by train from Berlin to Vienna, which was geographically functional but which Marcus had been obliged to mark down for the second-person construction that Carroll had used as a way of avoiding the accusative throughout, and which had also contained a sentence that Marcus had read three times in the faculty room before identifying its error: the student had written *ich bin angekommen* and then, in the following clause, *ich bin gereist*, which was not wrong in itself but which had produced a tense sequence that implied the arrival had preceded the journey, the destination reached before the road was taken. Marcus had marked it and written *Zeitenfolge* in the margin and then, because *Zeitenfolge* assumed more knowledge of grammatical terminology than a second-year student was likely to have, had written *sequence of tenses — check* beside it in English.

In the gap between the main course and whatever Lillian was going to bring next, when the conversation had paused in the way it paused at this table without anything being wrong — Henry cutting carefully at the last of the chicken near the bone, the room quiet, the street outside the window gone from late-afternoon into the early grey of a May evening — Marcus said: "I think I may have known who the man on the breakwater was."

He did not look up.

He said: "From before. From another time."

Henry was quiet.

He set the knife down across the rim of the plate. He sat with his hands on the table for a moment, and then he put them in his lap, and he looked — not at Marcus, not at Lillian, but at some point of the room between the two, the wall above the sideboard — and he nodded. Once, slowly. Not agreement; not surprise. The nod of a man who has had the shape of a thing confirmed that he had been carrying as a supposition, without saying so, for some time.

The kitchen was silent. Then the sound of Lillian setting something down.

After a moment Henry said: "That happens in a war." A pause. "A man gets far from home and farther still, and then he runs out of road."

He picked up his fork.

He did not say: who was he? How do you know? Are you going to tell someone? Are you going to tell the harbormaster's office, or the county examiner's office, or the District Commander at Rockland? He did not ask what kind of before, or what kind of another time. He ate the last of the chicken. He reached for his water glass.

The kitchen door opened and Lillian came through with the water pitcher. She went to Marcus's glass first and poured. She went to Henry's and poured. She set the pitcher down on the sideboard and stood for a moment, and then she lifted the serving fork and cut more of the chicken onto Marcus's plate — the last of what was on the serving dish, which she had been keeping on the trivet — and set the serving fork down and sat.

She did not ask whether he wanted it.

She began to eat.

---

The subject did not come back. Marcus did not raise it. Henry did not raise it. Lillian did not indicate, in any way available to her, that there was anything at the table still requiring attention.

Henry asked, when Lillian had brought the stewed fruit and they were eating it, whether Marcus had gotten any further with the Carroll boy and the subordinate clause problem. Marcus said Carroll understood the constructions well enough in isolation; the difficulty was in extended writing, where the clause that was available and the clause that was correct could feel interchangeable at the rate Carroll worked. Henry said that sounded like a student worth the effort. Marcus said he thought so. Henry said his experience of students worth the effort was that they were worth the effort over a longer arc than any individual assignment suggested, and that the assignment was not the measure. Marcus said he had come to the same conclusion. They finished the fruit. Lillian cleared the stewed-fruit dishes.

After the meal Henry moved to the armchair with his newspaper. Lillian went to the kitchen counter and did something there — the sequence of sounds Marcus had charted precisely over twenty months: plates set to the side, the tap water run and shut, the particular sound of the dish rack being populated one plate at a time, and then the far cabinet, and then quiet. Marcus cleared the table. He had always cleared the table; it had been established in his first two weeks in the house and had not been discussed since. He brought the plates through to the kitchen doorway and Lillian took them, and he brought the water glasses and the serving things, and she took those too. He came back through to the dining room and pushed the chairs in.

The table was empty. The last light from the west window was nearly gone.

He said goodnight to Henry. Henry said goodnight without looking up from the newspaper. He said goodnight to Lillian through the kitchen doorway. She said goodnight and told him there was bread on the counter if he wanted it in the morning.

He went upstairs.

---

The room was dark. He lit the lamp on the desk and let it settle to its ordinary height.

The room was the room it had been this morning: the composition papers stacked in the right-hand corner, the pen in its groove beside the inkstand, the German grammar open to the subjunctive chapter on the reference shelf where he had left it before breakfast. The bottom drawer of the desk was closed.

He sat at the desk.

He had said: from before, from another time. He had not said Josef Lutz. He had not said Sherbrooke, or a road in October rain, or the name on a piece of water-damaged paper, or the name of the man in Montreal who had helped with certain practical details of a departure. He had not said these things because to say them would have required an account that no part of this evening had been arranged to receive — not Henry's newspaper, not the table, not the hour. The documents upstairs would have required explanation. Explaining them would have required explaining himself, in ways and to depths he had not been asked to go, in a house that had not asked him to go there.

He had said: I think I may have known. He had said: from another time. Henry had said: a man gets far from home and farther still, and then he runs out of road. None of what had been said at the table was imprecise. It was a partial account offered without terms and received without terms, which was the form the table could hold and which was, Marcus had to conclude, the form that had been required.

The bottom drawer was closed. He sat with his hands flat on the desk surface and listened to the sounds from below — Henry's newspaper settling, the last of Lillian's work in the kitchen, the house at its ordinary Tuesday-evening quiet. No radio tonight. A door somewhere closing.

He was not alone with it.

He had not been certain, until now, that this was a state of affairs that the situation allowed. Henry's nod had covered the question without answering it; Henry's sentence had covered it without requiring it to be asked. This was the skill of the man, perhaps, or the skill of a life in which the accurate reading of rooms had mattered practically, not academically — the reading done and the response made and the conversation continued, because continuation was what the room could hold.

The lamp made its circle on the desk.

After a few minutes he got up, undressed, and went to bed.

Below, the house went quiet by degrees, the way it went quiet on all the evenings when nothing required it to be otherwise.
