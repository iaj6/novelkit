# Chapter 3 — Allgäu

The second pail was the heavier one.

By the time she set the first down by the side door and went back for it, the cold had committed itself — November pulling warmth out of the barn through the gaps in the board-and-bat siding, through the places where the insulation had thinned over the years and the wind had found its way to the bedding straw. The animals gave heat but not enough; by the second pail Hannah could feel the cold in her fingers and in the tops of her arms, where the muscles went into the shoulder. She had been up since five. The barn smelled of what it always smelled of — manure and hay and the particular sourness of cold milk — and when she had come out of the house in the dark she had heard through the kitchen window the radio already on, Marta up early, listening to the weather news with the careful attention she gave it every morning now.

The truck came at seven. She was still in the middle of the milking and heard the engine from the upper barn, then the silence when it cut, then the sounds of men climbing down from the truck bed. She did not stop. The morning milking was hers regardless; the barn animals did not know anything about trucks.

By the time she finished and carried the first pail out, the crew was dispersed to their work. Two of them were at the far fence line — PW armbands pale against their jackets — and she couldn't make out faces at that distance. She had learned to place them by other signs: by build, by where they were assigned, by the way they moved when Oakes was watching versus when he wasn't. The man at the corner post was Brauer, wide across the back, who never spoke to anyone if he could help it. The other she knew less well; he had been put on fence duty since October and had stayed there. Carvell Oakes was near the south wall examining the water pump housing with deliberate slowness. The cold had been getting into his knees since early in the month, and he moved now with the careful economy of a man rationing his joints. The rifle hung across his back with the strap gone slack on one side.

Werner was in the upper field. She had watched him go up before the truck came, collar turned, moving without hurrying. It was a new habit — being elsewhere when the crew arrived, staying up there until they were gone. She had not asked him about it.

She went back for the second pail.

---

She was setting it down inside the barn corridor when she heard the German.

It was not close — from the far end of the corridor, where the stanchion repairs had been waiting since August. She heard a voice first, then heard it was German. And then — not through the words themselves, but through sound alone, through the thing recognition does before the mind arrives — she heard what kind.

Not the German of the radio. Not the newsreel sound, the shouting sound, the specific German that people now heard in their heads when they heard the word at all. Not the flat classroom German of her eighth-grade workbook before Mr. Parkins retired and they dropped the subject. This was the German her father's kitchen made on a Sunday evening when the radio was off and the week had been hard and Werner stopped, for a little while, being someone who had come to Maine in 1922. Something that happened to the vowels at the back of the mouth — a roundness, a giving-way. The *ch* sounds that arrived instead of scraping. The particular way the end of a word kept its weight.

She hadn't heard that from outside the house in years.

The man speaking was Karl. She knew his build by now, the way you learn a person who works in your field of vision five days a week — not by looking directly, but by the shape they make in the day. He was at the stanchion end of the corridor with Brauer. He said something, holding a length of wire in both hands; Brauer shook his head; Karl said it again, slightly differently. Brauer looked at the wire. Neither of them was looking at her.

She said a word. Just one — *Gell?* — before she thought. The Allgäu particle, the tag that meant *isn't it* or *right?* or sometimes only the breath between sentences, the sound that meant *you see what I mean.* It came out in her father's vowels, her father's consonants, and she heard herself say it and could not put it back.

Karl turned.

His expression was not alarm. It was something she needed a moment to name — a recalibration, the look of a person adjusting you from one category to another. Brauer looked at the south wall of the barn. Outside, Oakes had not moved from the pump housing.

"You speak it," Karl said, in the same dialect.

Not quite a question.

She said: "My father does." Then, because German came easier for it: *Meine Eltern kamen 1922.*

She watched him take that in — the idea of a person from that sound, from those vowels, appearing in a barn in Maine with milk on her apron.

"From near Marktoberdorf," he said. "Karl Reinmar."

She gave her name. He would have heard it already; names move through a farm yard. But saying it directly was different. "My family is from near Kempten."

*Etwa fünfzig Kilometer*, he said. About fifty kilometers.

"Yes," she said.

Oakes was still at the pump housing, weight on his right hip, his back to the barn entrance. She had learned his patterns through October: he made his circuit roughly every twenty to twenty-five minutes, the same route each time, the pace adjusted for the weather and the day's degree of discomfort. She had maybe ten minutes left in this window, maybe less.

*Sind Ihre Eltern noch hier?* Karl asked. Are your parents still here?

She heard the weight in it. *Noch* — still. Not a simple word in 1943.

*Mein Vater ist immer noch von dort*, she said. My father is still from there. She paused. *Das versteht man.* One understands this.

She didn't explain further and he didn't ask her to. He held the wire in both hands without fidgeting. He was nineteen — they had been given the crew's names and approximate ages when the arrangement started, the same way you were given the specifications of new equipment — and he had the quiet of someone young who had already learned that most quiet was safer than most speaking.

He said he was conscripted at eighteen, and he said it in the way you state a thing that no longer requires feeling to state: it happened; the army was assigned to him; he had not chosen it. He had been thinking of a trade before that, he said — a trade, not the army — but then a letter came and the plan became something that had been. Now he was here. No complaint in any of it. The absence of complaint had its own weight, the weight of a person who had done the accounting and come out the other side of it.

Then, as a different thing — in the way you mention a strange bird seen from a moving window — he said he had seen something odd. On the transport from Bangor, in October. The truck driving the coastal road at night, rain coming hard, the windows fogged over. He had cleared a circle on the glass with his sleeve.

An older man on the verge of the road. In the rain, at that hour. Moving at a pace that was — he paused for the word — purposeful, he said. Light on his feet. Carrying something under his arm, or over his shoulder; he could not be certain at that distance, through the rain on the glass. *Ein älterer Mann. Leicht auf den Füßen. Er trug etwas.* Moving in the direction of the water, as best Karl could judge from the moving truck.

*Er schien einen langen Weg vor sich zu haben*, Karl said. He seemed to have a long way ahead of him.

He said it the way you say such a thing: here is what I saw, I do not know what it means, it has stayed with me the way small odd things stay. Hannah listened. Something in her attention had caught — a hook without visible anchor — and then she heard Oakes begin to move on the gravel outside, his uneven footfall coming around the south corner of the barn.

---

Karl's hand came out of his coat pocket. A piece of paper, folded four times — small, barely larger than a matchbook. He pressed it into her palm as she lifted the second pail from the floor. One gesture, quick and deliberate, the way you execute a movement you have rehearsed many times in your mind and are now finally completing in the body.

"Don't read it here," he said. In English, very quiet. Very precise.

He turned back to the stanchion. Brauer had not moved from the wall.

Hannah walked out of the barn with the pail. Oakes was coming around the corner in his hip-careful way. He looked at her and said it was a cold one today. She said it was. She kept walking.

In the dairy she rinsed the pail and hung it. She reached into her apron pocket; the note was there. She moved her fingers away from it. She reached instead for the next hook.

---

She carried it through the afternoon.

Werner came down from the upper field before noon and worked on the harness that had needed restitching for two weeks. The crew was in the yard at intervals. Marta called her in to help with the midday meal and she set the table and heated the bread and ate her own portion standing at the counter. She washed the dishes. She went back out. The note was in her apron pocket through all of it — while her hands were in the feed buckets and the water troughs, while she was walking the fence line to count the boards the wind had loosened — and she did not touch it. She could feel it when she moved: the four folds of it, almost nothing in weight, present the way a small thing becomes present when you are not attending to it.

At half past four the truck came back into the yard for the crew. She was in the dairy and she heard it — engine, the creak of the gate, boots on gravel. She stayed where she was until the truck turned out onto the road. She listened to it going down the gravel until the sound went under the wind.

She went upstairs to change her apron. Werner's boots were on the back step; Marta's voice came from the kitchen. She moved the note from her apron to the pocket of her everyday dress and came back downstairs and helped with the evening milking, and the animals did not know anything about notes.

---

After supper Werner settled at the radio. Marta brought the dish water up. The evening news came steady and measured — Italy, the Pacific, convoys, a reporter's voice from somewhere very far away making the cost of things sound orderly. Werner turned the volume up, then adjusted it down a little, looking for the setting between unendurable and inaudible. Hannah said she was going up.

Her room had the small lamp on the table by the window. She sat on the edge of the bed and took the note from her dress pocket. She held it in both hands a moment — its folded density, the four creases — and then she unfolded it.

The paper was ruled, from a workbook or something like it; the kind of paper that you used carefully because you had counted out what you needed before you wrote. The writing was in pencil, very small and deliberate, each letter formed as by someone for whom writing paper was not a thing to waste.

*Karl Reinmar. Marktoberdorf, Allgäu.*

*Man hat mich eingezogen, als ich achtzehn war — ich wollte Schreiner werden, das war mein Plan. Dann kam ein Brief. Nun bin ich hier.*

*Die Rinder erinnern mich an daheim. Das ist keine Klage.*

*Den Mann auf dem Weg habe ich noch in Erinnerung. Er schien einen langen Weg vor sich zu haben.*

She tilted the paper toward the lamplight. She read it through. Then she read it from the beginning again.

*Schreiner* — her father used the word sometimes, speaking about other men's work. Carpenter. Karl had wanted to be a carpenter. A letter came. She set those three sentences alongside every other account she had heard in four years of a plan interrupted, a letter arriving, a person ending up somewhere else, and they fit without revision, in exactly the proportion he had given them. Three sentences. Not a complaint. This is what happened; now I am here.

*Die Rinder erinnern mich an daheim.* The cattle remind me of home. *Das ist keine Klage.* That is not a complaint.

She looked at the last two sentences longer.

*Den Mann auf dem Weg habe ich noch in Erinnerung.*

The man on the road I still remember.

Below the floor the radio shifted — music now, a woman's voice over something slow and stringed — and Werner's chair moved, and the house settled into its evening sounds.

Hannah folded the note along its four creases and held it in both hands in her lap, under the lamp.
