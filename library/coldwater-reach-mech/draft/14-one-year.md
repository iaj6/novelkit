# One Year

The walk to school took fifteen minutes — Prospect Street to the building at the road's curve, the same way he had walked it two hundred and some-odd times in two years. On the morning of October 3 he walked it aware of the date in the way a person was aware of a fixed point on the calendar: not as a surprise arriving, but as a weight already carried, confirming itself when it arrived.

He had woken with it. He had dressed with it. He had come down to the kitchen and found Lillian at the stove, the radio at its usual low volume, the smell of something in the pan — eggs this morning, from the Dunbar property two houses up, their arrangement — and had eaten and thanked her and gone out through the gate. The gate's latch had made the sound it always made. Henry had already left.

The maples on Prospect were in their full October color: the yellow ones already thinning, the orange ones at their peak, the morning light coming off the water at an angle that made the color particular. He noticed it and did not stop. He had been aware of the date for a week.

---

Fourth period was Latin: his five seniors, the room with the high east window, the radiator clicking again with the cooler weather. They were working through Vergil's *Georgics* — the farming poems, the second book — and Phyllis Bowen was picking through a sentence near the middle of the page with the careful method of a student who has decided not to guess anymore.

"*Haec loca non tauri spiro praeverterunt aratro,*" she read, placing each word separately. "The places the bulls have not yet turned — with their breath—" She stopped. "The breath doesn't make sense here."

"It doesn't," Marcus said. "The ablative of means. *Aratro*: with the plow. *Spiro* resembles the verb for breath; it is a prefix of *praeverto* — don't let the resemblance do your parsing for you. The bull provides the force. The plow is the instrument by which the force acts. These are two distinct grammatical facts about the same action."

"Oh," she said.

"*The plow,*" said Horace Dent from the second seat, helpfully. A sound from the back row.

Marcus let them work. In the available portion of his mind he was on the letter — not the words of it, which he had read until they had stopped being words and become only shapes on a page, but the fact of what he was going to do with it in approximately four hours. He had been deciding this for three weeks. The decision had not changed; it was only the doing that remained.

"*In messem,*" Phyllis said carefully. "The places not yet turned for the harvest?"

"Correct." He glanced at the clock above the board. "The *dativus finalis* — the dative of purpose proper. In prose you would use *ad* or *in* with the accusative for the same function; in Vergil you have the construction in its older form. For Thursday: lines one-eighty-nine through two-twenty. Read the structure of the comparison first, before you read for its sense."

He wrote the assignment. The bell rang at ten past eleven.

---

He walked home after school. The northeast cover the radio had forecast was in now — the light flattened, the air noticeably colder than last week, the kind of October cold that installed itself without announcement. The maples were orange in both directions on Prospect Street. Going back was already different from going, though the street was the same street.

He came through the gate, went upstairs.

The drawer held what it had held since April: the fragment in the oilskin piece, water-stained along one edge; Ehrlich's Montreal letter, the dates underlined in pencil; and the letter to the sister, in the envelope he had addressed three weeks ago. The address in Zürich had come in Ehrlich's second letter — a last-known address for a woman whose brother had walked to the coast of Maine in October of 1943 and had not come home, and who did not yet know this.

He took the envelope out and read the letter one more time.

*Your brother Josef was known to me from an earlier time. I have reason to believe he reached the coast of Maine in October of last year. I cannot tell you more than this without risking harm to others. He was not forgotten. He died far from where he was born, and I am sorry.*

Below the body of the letter: *Ein Freund aus Sherbrooke.*

It was the fourth draft. The first had said too much — it had explained itself, which the right reader would not need and the wrong reader should not have. The second had said too little; there had been a version in which the letter said only that Josef Lutz had died in Maine and named no witness at all, and Marcus had read it and understood that this was not enough. The fourth said what it said: he was known; he is dead; there was a witness. It did not say that Marcus had spent eleven months in adjacent barracks with Josef at the Sherbrooke camp, or that he had heard Josef speak his sister's name twice in that time — once during a fever and once while they were waiting for a form to be processed — and that this was how Marcus had a name to carry. What the letter contained was what the letter contained. Nothing further.

The risk was real and the assessment of it had not changed in three weeks: a letter signed with a partial pseudonym and an institutional reference that would mean nothing to a censor and would mean everything to anyone who had been at Sherbrooke, sent to a last-known address across the Atlantic through the wartime post. She might not be in Zürich any longer. The letter might arrive at a wrong address. It might be read before it arrived.

Against all of that: one possibility. The letter arrived at the right address. A woman in Zürich learned that her brother had reached America and was not lost without witness.

He put the letter back in the envelope. He put on his coat.

---

The mailbox was on the corner of Prospect Street near the school building — four-tenths of a mile from the Crow house, a distance he had measured in his first autumn here, the way a person measured things when they arrived somewhere new and needed to understand what distances meant. The afternoon collection was at five o'clock. He had time.

The cloud cover made the maples greyer on the way up the street. Two boys from the younger grades were kicking something in the road ahead of him; they moved to the side without looking at him. A woman he knew by sight came out of the dry-goods shop and went the other direction.

He reached the corner.

The mailbox was the standard issue green, dented at the base where someone had caught it with a car in July. He had put letters in this box before — cards to his cousin in Montreal, the occasional required form. He put the envelope in the slot.

The slot made its sound. A brief, flat click: metal, and then the interior.

The letter was in there now, in the dark box, and it would go out with the five o'clock collection and then it would go wherever the mail went — south to Portland, or north, he did not know the routing — and then by whatever channels wartime post had arranged across the Atlantic, and he could not take it back.

He stood on the sidewalk for a moment. October afternoon: the cold in it, the flat light, two cars along the street. One year, exactly, from the morning the body had come in against the breakwater's seaward face, with the gale still dropping and the light just coming, and the log's October 2 entry at 2300 still showing only weather.

He turned and walked home.

---

Lillian was in the kitchen. Henry was not home yet. She looked at him over her shoulder with the brief look she gave anyone who came through before dark.

He said he had walked a little farther than usual.

She made a sound that meant she had heard him and turned back to the stove.

Henry came in at twenty past five, still carrying the principal's latest memo — the furnace, the third such communication since September — and set it on the sideboard and did not mention it. They sat at table. Lillian had done something with potatoes and what remained of Sunday's roast, a hash of some kind, and Marcus ate without particular thought, which was the best condition for receiving anything with gratitude.

Henry said the Carroll boy had been seen in the hall after the bell in conversation with one of the Haverford girls. Lillian said the Carroll boy was past time. Marcus said the Carroll boy's subordinate clauses had improved substantially in October, which was his way of accounting for a student at the table, and Henry received it with the gravity he brought to everything said there, which meant Marcus's accounting was heard as the thing it stood in for and not pressed further.

The radio was on in the sitting room at the usual volume. Neither Henry nor Lillian asked what Marcus had done on his longer walk. He did not say.

He cleared the table as he always did. He went upstairs at half past eight.

---

He opened the drawer.

The fragment in the oilskin piece. The Montreal letter, its dates in pencil.

The place where the third thing had been.

He looked at it — the small empty slot where the envelope had sat for three weeks — for long enough to know that what it was now was what it would be from here on. He had sent what could be sent. Whether it arrived was not something that would be established in this room, in this house, by a man who had no way to know what crossed the Atlantic and what did not.

He closed the drawer. He turned off the lamp. He went to bed.

Below, the Crow house settled into its ordinary October evening — the sound Henry made reading, the last of Lillian's work in the kitchen, the street outside going quiet by degrees.
