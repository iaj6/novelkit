# Chapter 4 — The Commander

The coffee was the real kind. She had kept the tin at the back of the shelf since Saturday, when she'd walked up to Atwood's and set it aside — a rationed indulgence held back for a use that could justify it. She measured out what was needed and no more. She put the chairs right: her chair behind the desk, the second chair pulled to the side of the desk rather than across it, which was the arrangement she used when she did not want the desk between her and the person she was meeting. She set the log on the stand. She straightened the tide chart where the lower tack had let it slip two inches to the right. She checked the oil burner. She had run out of things to adjust, so she stood at the window and watched the harbor.

The November light came off the water flat and cold. No wind yet, but the sky had the color of something deciding. The channel marker off the south shoulder cycled steady red in the grey. A cormorant worked the near shallows. The two boats still on their moorings this late in the season rode easy.

The Commander's car came down the access road at ten past nine.

---

Commander Horace Willard, Naval District, Rockland. She had spoken with his office by radio on the morning of October 3 and once more in mid-October when the county examiner's report was logged at the district level; she had not met him in person. He was perhaps sixty. The naval overcoat had been broken in correctly — it had lost its stiffness without losing its order. He was a man who had done this kind of work before the war started and would do it after.

He shook her hand at the door.

"Harbormaster Bowman."

"Commander." She stepped back. "I have coffee."

He came in and took the offered chair. She poured two cups and sat down. He placed a small stiff-covered notebook on the desk, opened to a blank page, and set his pen on it. He looked at her once in a level, professional way — the look of someone confirming a match — and then looked at the notebook.

"The third of October," he said. "Start where you started."

---

She started where she'd started. Three-day gale, northeast, wind building from Saturday. Channel closed Monday morning at 1030, conditions deteriorating, navigable water compromised. The walk on October 3: the harbor side first, then the seaward face. The finding at roughly two hundred feet from the pier head. Male, estimated fifty to sixty, no papers on the body, no identification of any kind. Clothing: wool overcoat, wool trousers, leather shoes. Not fishing dress. Not working coast. No oilskin.

She had called the District Commander's office by radio immediately upon returning to the shack and had read them the log entry as written. She had contacted Constable Tibbetts for the local side. The county examiner arrived at half past one with two men and a canvas stretcher. They removed the body, filed a probable-drowning report, divided the form in two and gave her the copy.

The Commander wrote in his notebook without hurrying. When she finished he read back the particulars — dates, times, the chain of contact — and she confirmed them in order.

"The log," he said.

She opened it and put it on the desk in front of him. He read the October 3 body entry once through. He turned back to October 2 and read through the day's entries from the beginning: the morning tide check, the channel closure at 1030, the midday conditions update, the gale report at 1630. At the foot of the October 2 column, below the 1630 entry, he read the 2300 line.

*Weather: NE gale, 35 knots decreasing at first light. Channel: temporarily impaired.*

He read it. He turned ahead to the October 4 and 5 entries. He turned back. He wrote two items from the October 3 entry in his notebook: the distance, two hundred and ten feet, and the phrase *clothing not consistent with fishing or working-coast dress.* He set his pen down.

---

From the inside of his coat he took a manila envelope, opened the clasp, and laid a photograph on the desk.

"Take your time with this," he said.

It was the county photographer's image. She had not seen it before now. The body lay on its side against the seaward face of the breakwater, the granite wet, the overcoat spread around it as the water had arranged it. The man's face was turned slightly toward the camera, the left cheek and jaw and the brow line visible.

She looked at the photograph. She did not reach for it; she looked at it where it lay.

The face of the man in the photograph was the face of someone who had been dead for several days and in the water before that. The features were altered in the way death and water alter things — swollen in places, slack in others, the texture of the skin changed in ways the camera had caught and not softened. She had seen drowned men before. She looked at the cheek, the brow, the set of the jaw. She was looking for what she could say honestly, so she looked with care.

She looked another moment.

She did not recognize him.

"No," she said. "I don't know him."

The Commander returned the photograph to the envelope and closed the clasp.

---

"Any vessel traffic the night before? Night of the second?"

"Gale conditions all through the second," Eira said. "Channel was flagged. No vessel traffic."

He wrote something in his notebook. He looked at the open log where it lay on the desk, ran a finger down the October 2 column without touching the page, and then closed it and handed it across the desk to her.

"Your entries are thorough, Harbormaster."

"That's the requirement," she said.

"It is." He took the notebook in his hand. "Here's where we stand."

---

His office had run the physical description against merchant marine rosters, Allied and German both. Against naval registry, American and German. Against civilian missing-persons reports filed in the four preceding months, coast of Maine and eastern Canada. No match on any of the registries. No match on the missing-persons reports.

"The man will be interred in your town cemetery," he said. "Lacking an identity, that's what we can do for him. The stone will read Unknown Man, October 1943." He looked at her directly. "If anyone comes forward — a letter, a missing report, any information at all — you contact Rockland immediately."

"I will."

"You're the eyes on this harbor, Harbormaster Bowman. You see something that connects, anything at all, you call us."

"Yes," she said.

He closed the notebook. He put his coat on.

---

She walked him out. They went out into the cold. The wind had come up a little from the west. She stood at the shack door and watched him go down the access road to his car. He reversed at the end and turned around and drove out along the road and up the grade toward the main road and was gone.

She went back inside.

---

The shack held its warmth. She moved the coffee cups to the shelf. She put the second chair back to where it belonged. She picked up the log and held it a moment before setting it on the stand.

Then she opened it.

October 2. The morning entries in their order, the channel closure, the afternoon gale report running to 1630. At the foot of the column, in the gap between 1630 and 2300, nothing. Then, at 2300, the weather line:

*Weather: NE gale, 35 knots decreasing at first light. Channel: temporarily impaired.*

Commander Willard had read this line and turned the page. He had not paused at the gap between 1630 and 2300. The log gave him no reason to.

The case was closed. Unknown Man, October 1943.

She put her thumb on the 2300 line. The letters showed at the edges, the rest covered. She held her thumb there.

Then she closed the log.
