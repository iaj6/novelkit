# Chapter 12 — Not Yet

The afternoon was hot in the way June could be before the fog came in from the south — a still, white heat that settled into the upstairs room and didn't move, the air through the open window no cooler than the air that had been in the room since noon. Hannah had pushed the window up as far as it would go. The upper pasture lay flat and white in the light, the haze low over the grass, and along the far fence four shapes moved slowly in a line following the hawthorn hedge. At this distance they were just men working. She had made a practice of not trying to tell them apart at this distance. The habit had cost her something in the winter and spring, and she was keeping it now.

Carvell Oakes was on the fence rail with his hat tipped forward. He had been there the same way for twenty minutes.

She was at her desk with the letter to her cousin Elsa, who lived with the Heger side of the family in Vermont and who wrote back in the careful English of someone who had learned it differently. Hannah had been at the letter since after the midday meal. She had covered the garden — the peas were doing well this year, the carrots still in the ground — and the weather, and the price Atwood's was asking for the sugar that had come through in May. She had said that the county fair might happen in August. She had said she hoped Elsa could come.

She was on her second page and had run, honestly, out of things she wanted to say. She wrote one more sentence about the peas, which was true, and re-read the letter from the beginning. It was the letter of a person who was alive and in Maine and thinking about her cousin in Vermont. That was all accurate. She folded the second page back and held it with her hands flat on the desk.

From below came the smell of the rhubarb Marta was putting up in the kitchen — the sharp, particular sweetness of cooked fruit and sugar, rising through the house.

---

Her father's step on the stairs was distinct. There was a creak on the fourth stair from the top that he hit the same way every time — in the middle, with his full weight — and she heard it before he was halfway up. She put the pen down. He appeared in the doorway without knocking.

"Hanna." The German form — the house form, the private one, the one without the English H at the end. He was already moving past her desk. "Der alte Stallmantel —" He switched without pausing, the way he did when his mind was on something practical. "The barn coat. I think I left my tobacco in the pocket."

"It's in the closet," she said.

He went past her to the closet and opened the door. She picked up her pen and held it over the letter without writing. Outside, through the window, the four shapes at the far fence had rounded the corner of the hawthorn hedge and were working south. She could hear the barn coat come off its hook and heard her father go through the pockets — the soft searching sound of hands finding nothing, the slight collapse of an empty pocket.

"Nichts," he said.

He moved again. She heard his boot settle on the closet floor.

She had moved the school bag to the closet in May, after re-sewing the lining with the note back inside. Before that it had hung on the hook by the stairs — she had put it there last December, when she'd wanted it somewhere she could get to easily, to know it was there. In May she had looked at the bag on the hook by the stairs and decided the closet was better. Her room. The closet inside her room. There was nothing to see in the bag even if you opened it; the note was in the lining, and the lining looked like lining. But she wanted the closet.

She did not hear him lift the bag or open it. She heard his boot shift on the floor, and then the step back.

"Danke, Hanna," her father said.

The German name, said without thinking — the name of the house, the name of the family, the language the note was written in. His footsteps went to the door. She heard the fourth stair on the way down, and then the third, and then the kitchen.

---

She set the pen on the desk.

She counted a full minute by the second hand of the clock on the shelf — the hand going all the way around once, ordinary time. The room was the same room. Through the window the far field was the same field.

She got up and went to the closet.

The barn coat was on its hook, the pockets flat. Below it: the school bag, its strap looped over the low hook she had added inside the closet door in May, the bag resting against the wall. She took it down and held it by the strap and pressed the bottom lightly. The stiffness was there — the faint, dense difference at the bottom inside seam where the note was, where the canvas didn't give quite the same as the rest. Seven stitches and a knot. Black thread, wrong-colored from the first.

She put the bag back. She closed the closet door.

The letter to Elsa was on the desk. She looked at it for a moment and then laid it face-down on the stack of papers beside the inkwell.

---

At four o'clock the crew came down off the slope and formed up near the wagon road for Oakes to walk them to the truck. Hannah was at the pump rinsing the milk pails. She watched without watching: Oakes moving along the line, the truck at the road's end idling, the men going up the step and disappearing into the back. Karl was third. He did not look toward the house.

She scoured the pails and hung them on the fence rail to drain. She went to the henhouse to check the water. The henhouse was hot, the air inside dense with the smell of birds and straw. She pulled the water dish and rinsed it at the spigot and brought it back. The hens moved aside. She latched the door and went back into the yard, the evening light going flat and golden across the fields now, the heat releasing slowly.

At supper Werner mentioned that the lower fence would need mending before the herd went back to the east pasture; he thought that would be by Thursday. He wanted her to pull wire on Saturday morning. Hannah said she would. Marta had saved some of the rhubarb for the table, in a dish, and Hannah ate it with her bread. Werner said the weather would hold for the weekend and then thought of something else and went quiet. They ate the rest of the meal without much said.

Werner went to bed early. He had been going early for several weeks now, coming off the day's work and going straight upstairs, not staying for the radio or the newspaper — just up, the stair creak, the footsteps above. He had been tired, or something that looked like tired; Hannah had not asked.

Marta took her mending to the sitting room. Hannah sat at the kitchen table for a few minutes, listening to the needle from the other room, and then she went upstairs.

---

The room still held the day's heat. She lit the oil lamp and let it steady, and then she carried the school bag from the closet to the bed and sat beside it.

The seam. She found the bottom inside corner and worked her fingertip along the black thread until the first stitch gave. She had done this enough times now that it opened for her, the stitches releasing in order, and she worked the note out slowly, one corner at a time, until it came free.

Four folds, the paper gone soft along the creases. She unfolded it on her knee.

Karl's side: pencil, small and upright, the kind of handwriting from schools where you copied things carefully and exactly. She did not read the first part. She knew it well enough to say it, and that wasn't what tonight was for. She went to the bottom: *Den Mann auf dem Weg habe ich noch in Erinnerung. Er schien einen langen Weg vor sich zu haben.*

She turned it.

Her own hand — smaller and more careful than her ordinary hand, pressed lighter, the way she'd written when she wanted the letters to take up as little room as possible. *Wilder's Landing, Küstenstraße. Oktober 1943 — Donnerstag oder Freitag, nachts, gegen zehn oder elf.* The account below that, in the order Karl had given it: the rain, the branch across the road, the truck slowing. Einen Mann zu Fuß. Älter. Schmächtig. Einen langen Mantel, nicht für das Wetter. Trug etwas, nah am Körper. Bewegte sich Richtung Wasser.

*Sah sich um. Lief weiter.*

---

She got up and carried the note to the lamp.

She held the left corner — Karl's corner, the penciled lines — and brought the paper toward the flame. She stopped when she could feel the heat on the backs of her fingers. An inch from the flame, maybe a little less.

The lamp burned without wavering. The evening had gone completely still; no air moved through the window. She held the note steady.

The yellowing began at the bottom margin. Not burning — just the paper changing from the heat, the way very old paper went at the edges over years in a warm room — except this was happening now, as she stood there. A thin strip of it, the color of old tea, moving upward from the corner where the warmth was closest. The paper stiffened slightly where it yellowed. The smell that came off it was not burning — not yet — just the smell of heat and pencil lead and old paper, something that had no other name, the smell of a thing that had been handled many times beginning to be something else.

She held it there.

Her fingers were warm. The note was warm. Werner upstairs, perhaps asleep. Marta's mending below. The far field dark now, the crew long gone from it, Karl somewhere she couldn't see and had no business thinking toward.

She could not have said how long she stood there. When she looked at the bottom margin again the yellow strip was a little wider than it had been.

She pulled the note back.

---

She took it to the bed. She unfolded it, pressed each fold flat, and folded it again along the same four creases. At the bottom left corner — Karl's corner — the paper had gone a darker yellow-brown, a strip the width of her thumbnail where the heat had been closest. Not a burn mark; it hadn't gotten that far. Just the color of the paper, changed.

She threaded the needle from Marta's sewing box and sewed the lining closed. Seven stitches, the knot she had learned to make so the thread didn't work loose from ordinary handling. Black thread. Wrong color, as always.

She bit the thread and put the needle back.

She carried the bag to the closet and hung it on the low hook and stood for a moment with her hand on the closet door. Then she closed it.

---

She blew out the lamp.

The room came back slowly — dark first, and then the grey-blue of the June evening, the sky outside the window still holding color above the woodlot, the upper pasture a dark shape below it. Past nine o'clock and not full dark. The air through the window was cooler than it had been all day.

The closed closet door. The window and the evening beyond it.

From the sitting room below: Marta's mending. The soft, regular click of the needle case, the thread pulling, the rhythm of work going on in a house that had gone otherwise quiet.
