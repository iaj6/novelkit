# The Old Volume

The coal stove had gone down to coals while she finished the entry for the fourteenth. She got up, added two pieces from the scuttle and left the draft open, and stood for a moment while the fire built.

January in the harbor: the eastern finger and the shallows skinned with ice, the channel open and dark between them. She had walked the mooring lines at four o'clock and found them correct. The temperature had dropped since then, the cold thickening from steady to final. By morning there might be ice in the deep channel, or near enough to it.

She sat back down.

The entry for the fourteenth was done: wind NNW eighteen knots at sixteen hundred, barometric 30.04 steady, clear, no vessel traffic since HELEN J. had departed at 0810. She read through it once. She set the pen in its groove.

The old log volume lay open on the desk with six pages left in it, seven if she wrote small.

The new volume had come from Rockland in December, wrapped in kraft paper, smelling of paper and glue. She had set it at the corner of the desk and kept on in the old one. There was always a reason to keep on. But six pages was not going to last the month, and the time had come to start the new one, and before she could put the new volume into service she had to file the old one.

She had been telling herself this for three weeks.

---

She picked up the old volume.

Two and a half years in it — November 1942 through this past December. The cover had warped along the bottom edge where damp had gotten between the boards and the binding. The front board carried a scuff from August of 1943, when she had set the volume down on the dock planking and a frayed line had dragged the corner. The spine had cracked at its midpoint, a hairline she had noticed in March of the previous year.

She knew this volume: its weight, the give of the cover, the particular way it fell open nearest to whatever she had written last. She ran her thumb along the fore-edge from the back until she reached the October signatures and stopped.

She opened it.

Not to October 3 directly — she let it fall open a page early, to October 2.

There it was. The gale, the channel closed, the conditions logged in columns through 2200 hours: wind and sea and the notice that the channel had been temporarily impaired since the previous afternoon. Then 2300 hours, and the two blank ruled lines below it.

Below those blank lines: a single line crossed out in a careful horizontal stroke, initialed in her hand — *E.B.* — with the correction notice beside it: *Entry error — see correction below.* And the crossed-out text itself, still visible under the line where the legal standard required it: *October 2, 1943. 2300 hours. Weather—*

She had gotten as far as the dash. January of last year, ice watch, twenty below outside; she had opened the volume and tried to write the entry and the pen had lifted from the page before she reached the weather. The ice report she had written afterward as the correction was below the deletion: *0015 hours. Number-two marker: holding. Ice: flanks, three-quarters inch at granite. Channel: open.*

A very attentive reader of this page would find the sequence curious. No one was reading the log attentively except her.

She looked at it now. Her handwriting of a year ago looked back.

---

She found the pen.

The correction format was specific: below the previous entry, in the same column, bracketed notation preceding. She had used the format twice in nine years for genuine errors — a transposed wind speed, a vessel tonnage entered wrong. She had not used it for anything like this.

She wrote, below the ice report:

*October 2, 1943. 2300 hours. [Correction to above entry.] Weather: NE gale, 35 knots, continuing.*

She wrote that. She stopped.

The pen was in her hand. The nib was still wet.

The next column required harbor condition. After that: vessel traffic. After that, the narrow right-margin column for unusual events — the one she had used six times in nine years, the column for what did not belong to the ordinary register of wind and tide. She knew what that column would hold. She had been carrying the words for fifteen months: *One vessel docking at approximately 2300 hours, east dock.* Then the second thing: *one person at the pier, then two.* She could see the exact phrasing in front of her, the way she could see a vessel's heading from its running lights. She had composed and dissolved the words so many times that they had worn smooth, taken the shape of the handling. Short declaratives; harbor vocabulary; nothing she could not write.

She held the pen.

Outside the channel markers blinked their intervals. The harbor was quiet.

After a time she set the pen down.

She blotted what she had written. The weather entry: accurate as far as it went. NE gale, thirty-five knots — the same blow she had been logging since October 1, the gale that had kept the channel closed for three days and had been decreasing through the night of the second. What she had written was true. It was less than what was true.

She waited for the ink to dry.

She closed the volume.

---

Owen had built the shelf in 1933, his first year in the post. Cedar planks on iron brackets, the wood darkened from lamp smoke and salt air. It ran the length of the back wall. She had never done anything to it. It was exactly as he had left it.

She stood and carried the old volume to the shelf.

Owen's volumes stood at the left end. His 1933 — the year he built the shelf and put the first volume on it — was in good condition: spine straight, cover square, his handwriting on the front board in the neat penmanship she had first learned to read when she was his helper, then his assistant, then his wife. The heavier 1934–35 volume beside it had pages cockled from the spring leak she had not found until it had been running two weeks. That volume ended in September 1935. Not the last line of the page, not the last day of the month. Just the end of what he had had time to write — a vessel departed, the wind recorded, the tide height entered in the column — and then the page blank for the rest of September and all of October and November with four entries in her hand completing what the month required.

She had not tried to match his handwriting. She had not been able to.

After his volumes: hers. 1936 beginning in January, the year she had formally taken the post. She had written small and deliberately in that volume — the handwriting of someone establishing a practice she intended to keep. Then 1937, 1938, 1939, 1940, 1941. Then the volume that ran through early 1942 until it filled in November. Then the one she was now about to shelve.

She set it at the end of the row.

The warped cover sat slightly proud of the shelf edge. She pressed the spine back until the volume sat flush with the others. It held.

Owen's 1933 at the left end, and this one at the right: twelve years of two people's work and then the years she had kept alone. The gap between his last September entry and her first January was visible if you knew to look — four blank months in the old volumes she kept in the attic, the four months after the November storm when the post had been empty and the harbor had run without a record. She had not tried to fill those pages. The gap was part of what had happened.

This volume had its own gap in it now. October 2 at 2300: the original blank, then the crossed-out attempt, then the ice report, then the correction with the weather and nothing else. She was the only reader who would come to those three entries with knowledge of what the writing and not writing them had cost. When she was done with the post — whenever that came — the volume would go to the attic with the others, and whatever came after her would find it or not.

She went back to the desk.

---

The new volume was in the center of the desk where she had moved it to make room. She opened it to the first page.

The pages were very white. The columns were straight and sharp, the printed headings clean. The smell of paper and glue was still in the binding. She set the nib at the top of the first column and wrote the date: *January 14, 1945.* Then she wrote the entry below it in the usual order: wind, barometric, sky, vessels in and out, tide heights at high and low. Each column in its place. When she finished she read it through. It was complete.

She set the pen in its groove.

---

She blew the lamp.

The dark came in from the corners of the room. After a moment her eyes adjusted. Through the small window: the harbor, in the January dark. The channel markers blinked at their intervals — red, green, steady and repeating. The eastern ledge marker was visible farther out; she had been watching a slight list in it since November, the mooring cable working in the current, but it had not worsened in three weeks and tonight it showed correct.

The stove ticked once and then the shack was quiet.

She had kept these lights right for nine years. Owen before her, three years at this post. Before Owen a man named Beal, and before Beal a man named Crowther — she had read Crowther's volumes once when she first took the post, the handwriting small and very even, a different century's discipline. The record ran back to 1893. Someone had always kept it.

She sat for a while in the dark. The channel markers did not need her for it. They had been blinking their intervals all evening while she wrote.

---

She had checked the mooring lines at four o'clock. She went out anyway.

The cold took her at the door — below zero, still, no wind, the air dense and exact. She pulled her coat tight and walked down the pier. The planking was dry; the harbor surface in the shallows was white and opaque, the deep channel a black moving line between the ice margins. She walked the length of the breakwater to the seaward end and stood there.

The harbor was as it should be. The channel markers blinked: red, green, the intervals correct. She counted them through, confirming. The eastern ledge marker held steady; if she went out by dinghy next week when the weather allowed she would check the mooring cable directly, but from the breakwater end it looked right. The entrance to the reach was clear.

The sky was very clear. Stars numerous over the water, fewer toward the bluff where the town's few lit windows sat against the dark. The road lamp at the church corner. A light in what might be Atwood's upper floor. The cold that had been building since afternoon had settled now to what it would be through the night: particular, final, the harbor in its deep January quiet.

She turned and walked back toward the pier.

The shack was dark. The cottage beyond it was dark. The old volume was on the shelf next to Owen's. The new volume was on the desk.

She went inside.
