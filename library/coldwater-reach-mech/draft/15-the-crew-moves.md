# The Crew Moves

The army truck came up the farm road at seven-thirty, half an hour early, and Hannah was in the barn with the pitchfork and the left-side mow.

She heard the engine from the road — the particular pitch of it, higher than the farm equipment, higher than any vehicle with reason to come this way before eight — and she waited to see if it would slow for the mailbox and keep going. It slowed for the turnoff instead and came into the yard.

She kept forking.

The hay fell where she threw it. Through the side boards she heard the engine stop, then the doors, then the particular quiet that followed. The barn smelled the way it smelled all October: hay and damp earth and the cold coming through the gap in the west wall where the boards had shrunk over the summer. It was a good smell. It was the record of what the barn had held and for how long. She had worked in this smell since before she could say when. She forked hay and let the morning come in at whatever pace it chose.

The men came at their usual intervals. First Karl, then Franz, then Walther — she had given him that name in her own mind since spring because she had never gotten his actual name and it was too late now to ask — and then Dieter last, who always had a slow walk, as if he had not quite finished sleeping. Carvell Oakes came in behind them and went to the far wall near the door. Today he stood instead of settling on the south rail. She noticed this without looking at him directly. Today he had some performance of attention to give.

Werner had assigned the final tasks before she reached the barn: the fence wire along the south field, the last haul of the season on the manure, the barn gutter. He came in for ten minutes and confirmed the assignments in halting German — the nouns sure, the verbs uncertain, the way his German always sounded when he was trying to be practical with it rather than private. She could hear him from the mow. She did not know whether he knew that. He went back to the house. The men went to their work.

The morning moved through itself the way mornings did when you were waiting for something to happen at the end of them. The fence wire. The manure hauling. The cold bright quality of the light through the side boards, which was not like summer light and not like late autumn light but October light, specific to itself. She heard Oakes cough once from the far wall and shift his weight and cough again.

---

Mid-morning Karl was near her in the center corridor. Both of them moving the last of the hay from the mow — she from the left side, he from the right — two forks working at the opposite ends of the same narrow aisle. Oakes was at the far end of the barn with Walther, who was working on the gutter drain, their voices coming down indistinctly, German and English mixed, neither carrying any particular meaning from this distance.

The light through the side boards was thin and grey. The kind of October light that removed shadows rather than casting them.

Karl's fork did not hurry. He had worked this farm for a year and he knew the work and he did not waste motion. She had always noticed this about him — the economy of it, the same economy her father had. Werner would not want to hear that comparison made. She did not make it aloud.

Karl said, without preamble, in English: "The cattle here are good."

She said: "They are."

He said: "The Braunvieh at home are a little smaller." A pause of two or three seconds. "A little more delicate."

She said: "I know."

Oakes shifted his weight at the far end. Neither of them looked toward him.

"Good luck to you," Karl said. In English. Without looking at her.

She said: "You too."

They went on moving the hay.

She did not say: *Ich habe die Notiz noch.* She did not say: *Ich weiß nicht, was ich damit anfangen soll, oder ob es irgendjemanden außer mir noch interessiert.* She did not say: I wrote down what you told me the way you would write a witness statement, because I didn't know what else to do. She did not say: I don't know if you'll be safer where they're sending you than you were here. She kept forking. He kept forking. Oakes walked the length of the barn at ten-thirty and stood at the door a moment and came back in and returned to the far wall.

That was the whole of it.

---

The truck came at three instead of four. The dispatcher had moved the schedule, or the driver had come early; there was no way to know and no one explained. Hannah was in the south field with the fence pliers when she heard the engine on the farm road. She walked back to the yard.

The men came from the field and from the barn gutter and set down their tools in the places where they had been setting things down all year. They lined up along the truck's side in the way they did when Oakes needed to count: a few feet between each man, faces forward. Oakes walked the row. His lips moved. He made a mark in his roster, looked up, made another mark. He said something to the driver. The driver nodded.

The men climbed in over the tailgate — Franz first, then Dieter, then Karl, then Walther. The canvas flap dropped behind the last of them. The truck pulled around the yard and went down the farm road. The dust came up fine and pale in the cold afternoon light.

Karl did not look back. He had climbed in third, she had watched him climb in, and then the flap had come down. He did not look back because looking back was a gesture, and he had spent a year learning not to make gestures. It was not a dramatic thing. It was just how he had learned to move in this country, on this farm, under Carvell Oakes's occasional attention.

She went back into the barn.

She finished the hay work herself — another hour, maybe a little more. Werner came in at four with his coat and took the other pitchfork from its corner peg and they worked at their usual distance without speaking. The barn was quiet in the particular way it went quiet in autumn when the work was getting toward its end for the season — the sounds smaller, the spaces larger. Near the old stalls at the far end, the barn cat had caught a shrew and was moving with it from place to place without anywhere particular to go. Hannah watched it for a moment, then stopped watching and worked.

---

Supper was potatoes, salt pork from what remained of the ration, Marta's picked beets from the September canning. Werner ate without comment. Marta mentioned the Heger cousin in Vermont who had written last week about the egg prices, and Werner said mm. That was the conversation at the table.

After supper Werner went to the radio in the sitting room. The Western Front again — the advance east into Germany, the cities in the Belgian borderlands, the announcer's careful voice moving through what had been gained and what was still contested. Werner sat in his usual chair, arms on his knees, leaned toward the set. She had watched him sit that way every night since last October, since before last October, and she did not know what he heard when those place names came over the radio. She had not known for a long time.

She did the dishes. She banked the kitchen fire. She said good night to Marta and went upstairs.

---

The school bag was on the low hook inside the closet door. She took it down and carried it to the edge of the bed and sat. She pressed the bottom of the bag through the canvas: the stiffness was there. She worked the needle from her sewing kit under one of the seven stitches and drew the seam back enough to open the lining.

The note was where she had left it.

She unfolded it along the four creases. The paper opened by itself once she started it — the folds that deep, the paper soft from being handled since March. Softer now than it had been in June. The bottom left corner had the yellow-brown mark, the width of her thumbnail, from holding it near the lamp flame. She had not decided anything in that moment when she pulled it back from the flame. She had just pulled it back.

She read Karl's side first.

He had written it in the Allgäu dialect but carefully — the German of someone setting something down to be understood and not only felt. His name and his town near Marktoberdorf in the Allgäu and the number of kilometers from there to the village her father was from, which Karl had known and she had not thought to count. Then four lines about the Pfeiffer cattle — about the way they moved in the lower pasture in the early morning when the frost was still on the grass, the particular sound they made coming in from the field — that she had read until the words had ceased to surprise her and become something she did not have a name for. And then:

*Den Mann auf dem Weg habe ich noch in Erinnerung. Er war allein. Es war der Abend vor dem Sturm, und er trug einen langen Stadtmantel und ledersohlige Schuhe, die ich auf dem nassen Kies hörte, bevor ich ihn sah. Ein älterer Mann, schmächtig, nicht ein Bauer. Er hielt etwas nah am Körper. Bewegte sich Richtung Wasser. Sah sich um. Lief weiter.*

And then a space — two or three lines of it, as if he had considered whether to write more and decided not to. And then, last:

*Er schien einen langen Weg vor sich zu haben.*

She turned the paper over.

Her own handwriting on the back was different from Karl's: smaller, pressed harder, the hand of someone writing quickly and trying to be exact at the same time. She had written it on a Thursday afternoon in March in the kitchen with the crew in the far field and fifteen minutes she had not been certain of, moving fast. The date and location as Karl had given them to her. *Zwischen der Hauptstraße und dem Hafen. Küstenstraße, kurz vor Wilder's Landing. Früher Oktober 1943. Gegen Abend, ungefähr 2200 Uhr.* And then the description, the things Karl had said a second time while she wrote: *Ungefähr fünfzig Jahre alt, vielleicht ein wenig mehr. Schmächtig. Langer Stadtmantel, kein Ölzeug. Trug ein Bündel oder eine Tasche, nah am Körper gehalten.* And then the motion, which was what mattered: *Bewegte sich Richtung Wasser. Sah sich um. Lief weiter.* Below that, Karl's name in her hand and the date she had put it down.

Two sides. Two hands. Two different moments in two different rooms — one spring kitchen and one autumn barn corridor, one question asked without knowing how to ask it and one answer that had not been meant as testimony until she made it so.

She folded it along the four creases. She threaded the needle and re-sewed the seam with seven stitches and pressed the lining flat with her thumb. She put the bag back on the low hook inside the closet door and closed the closet.

---

The window faced west. The farm road was dark. No lights on it. The barn light was off — Werner must have gone out while she was in the kitchen and turned it off, or he always turned it off at this hour and she had simply never paid attention to when. Below, the radio murmured: the Western Front, the advance, the names of cities being taken and held.

She did not know where Karl was tonight. The main camp, or a truck toward Steuben, or already at the cannery. She could not picture the cannery, only the word. She did not know whether the barracks there were warmer or the guard different or whether the four men were kept together or separated and assigned elsewhere. She did not know if she would see him again, or whether the war would end before the spring rotation, or whether there would be a spring rotation if the war ended.

She did not know whether what she had in the lining was evidence of anything, or whose desk it should go to, or what kind of building held that kind of desk. She did not know whether the man in the cemetery — the stone that said *Unknown Man, October 1943* — had anyone left to wonder where he had gone, or whether the wondering had already ended without answer.

*Er schien einen langen Weg vor sich zu haben.*

He had not gotten there.
