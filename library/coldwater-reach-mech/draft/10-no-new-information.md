# Chapter 10 — No New Information

The form had been under the log for a week. She had put it there on the Thursday it arrived—folded inside the larger Rockland envelope along with two vessel-movement notices—and had not taken it out again until now.

She sat at the desk. The lamp was burning though the morning light from the east window was sufficient; she had lit it at six and not blown it out. Outside, the harbor lay in the grey of an overcast April morning, the water running with the spring chop, the mooring buoy at the channel bend turning in the tidal draw. Agnes Louise was on the inner mooring. The east channel was clear.

She had until the fifteenth. Today was the thirteenth.

She took the form out from under the log and set it on the desk.

One page. *Office of the District Commander, First Naval District, Rockland, Maine.* Printed letterhead, a case stamp at the upper right: *Follow-Up Inquiry — Case No. CLD-43-081.* The case number was the one from the October filing, which she had entered in the log's reference column when Rockland's acknowledgment had come back. Below the letterhead, three sections, each with a ruled blank line:

*Date of original finding.*
*Location of finding.*
*Cause of death as determined by county examiner.*

Then:

*New witnesses, physical evidence, or information bearing on identity or cause of death:*

And at the bottom: *Signature and date. Return by: April 15, 1944.*

She opened the log to October.

The spine gave at October without pressing—she had turned to these pages twice in winter and the binding had learned them. The page showed October 2 at the top: the 2300 weather notation, which she had written before she went down to the dock. *Weather: NE gale, 35 knots decreasing at first light. Channel: temporarily impaired.* Below it, on the same page: the crossed-out line. Her own hand, a single stroke through it, the initials E.B. in the margin, the correction notice and the 0015 ice report below that. She had written those in January during the ice watch. She did not read the crossed-out line now. She turned forward one page to October 3.

The body entry. She had written it in the early morning of the third, between Tibbetts's first call and the county examiner's arrival. She read it through. It was a good entry—complete in every particular. Time of discovery: 0615. Location: *seaward face, approximately 210 feet from pier head.* Description: *male, estimated fifty to sixty years. Heavy dark wool overcoat; wool trousers; leather-soled shoes, smooth, no grip. No papers of any kind.* Cause of death as later determined by county examiner: *undetermined drowning.* Signed E. Bowman, initialed A. Tibbetts.

She copied what she needed onto the form. October 3, 1943. Seaward face, 210 feet from pier head. Undetermined drowning. She copied exactly, without alteration. The pen moved steadily. The facts did not change with the copying.

Then: *New witnesses, physical evidence, or information bearing on identity or cause of death.*

She wrote: *None.*

She set the pen down.

She looked at the word. Then she picked the form up and read it through from the top.

The date was right. The location was right. The examiner's language was copied exactly.

She held the form and moved through what she had. The same order she used for a channel checklist: one item at a time, no skipping.

October 2, 1943. The east dock. Twenty-three hundred and ten hours, approximately—she had not checked the watch when she left the shack but it had read 2308 on the desk, and the dock was fifty yards. The gale had been building since mid-afternoon; cloud cover solid by 2100, the wind clocking east-northeast and increasing. The dock lamp at the seaward end had gone dark sometime after twenty-two hundred. She had noted it on her 2200 circuit and had not replaced the bulb because the ladder in that wind was inadvisable and it was not an emergency. She had gone to the east dock on the mooring circuit because something had moved.

The man had been alive. Standing upright, not slumped, not in distress she could see. She had registered him from thirty or forty feet—the shape of him in the poor light, the weight of his coat in the wind, the way he shifted his weight from foot to foot in the way of someone waiting rather than someone who had fallen or been struck. A long coat, dark wool, heavy. Not an oilskin.

Not from here. She had known the bodies of the harbor's men for eight years—their particular weight, the posture of men who worked the water or the fields, the way they stood in weather. This one was none of those. Something about the coat or the stance or the way he had turned when she first noticed him: European, she had thought. She had not gone close enough to be certain. The light was poor and the wind was building and she had stopped at the dock's edge.

He was not alone.

A second figure was standing to his left, ten or twelve feet away. She had seen the height and the way the weight sat—the particular arrangement of the shoulders, the particular way of standing with the weight slightly forward—and had thought, in that moment: someone from here. Someone whose shape she had encountered somewhere in the ordinary course of the town's days. She had not been able to put a name to it. The dark; the distance; the wind.

She had not spoken to either of them. She had stood at the dock's edge long enough to see what she had seen and then had turned and walked back to the shack and had made no entry.

By 0615 he was against the seaward face.

She set the form down.

*None.* The word sat on the Rockland letterhead without apology. No new witnesses had come forward to her office. No new physical evidence had been submitted. No information bearing on the examiner's determination had been received in her official capacity.

She folded the form along its three original creases. She fitted it into the envelope—the District return address already printed at the upper left—and ran the gum strip along the back of her tongue and sealed it.

The envelope lay on the desk beside the log. Two documents with her name on them, each covering October 2. One of them covered it with a line of weather and a crossed-out attempt. The other covered it with a word.

She took her coat from the back of the door and put the envelope in her pocket.

---

Outside, the harbor was running.

Two boats had gone before five—she had heard the engines from the cottage before first light—and a third had cleared the channel at half past seven, the Beaumont forty-footer that had docked in Sunday evening. The spring grounds were opening; several of the winter-idle boats had gone out in the last two weeks and not yet come back. The mooring field was thinner than it would be by June—three vessels down in Rockland for bottom work—and the channel had that clean look it got in early spring, before the summer traffic filled it in. The buoy at the channel bend was turning steadily in the tidal draw.

She walked the access road up the bluff. The mud at the road's edges had not crusted yet; she took the center. The roadside ditches on the south side were down to a trickle, the snowmelt finished, and the grass along the verge was still winter-flat but beginning to show color at the root. The air was cold with the honest cold of a coast in April—diesel and seawater and under them the turned-earth smell from somewhere inland, the farms beginning to plow. The light was flat and partial, the overcast thinning in places but not breaking.

She passed Atwood's. The door was open; someone inside, a voice she did not place. The church steps were empty. The war notice board outside the church had two new names since she had last looked, pinned with the paper tabs Willard Oakes cut from the cardstock he kept under the post office counter. She did not stop to read them.

The post office was in the brick building past the church. A bell on the door. Mrs. Welliver was behind the counter with the incoming tray.

She looked up. "Morning."

"Morning. For the outgoing."

"Morning pick-up's gone. This'll go with the two-thirty."

"That's fine."

Eira set the envelope on the counter. She did not wait for it to be taken.

---

She did not take the direct road back.

The access road ran down the bluff to the breakwater's landward end, and the shack was a five-minute walk from there. She had come by that road this morning. She had gone to the breakwater's mid-point twice over the winter—once to check the forty-foot navigation light after a complaint from the Loomis boat, once without a stated errand—and both times she had stopped at the mid-point and gone back.

Today she walked to the end.

The granite was wet with spray. A stone in the fourth course near the pier head had lifted slightly in the winter freeze—she had reported it to Rockland in February, flagged it in the maintenance log, not yet heard back. It was not dangerous; she stepped around it. The pier-head housing for the navigation beacon stood dark, as it would until dusk. The iron railing on the channel side was furred with salt.

Below: the seaward face.

The spring tidal flush had turned the water grey-green and turbid, the bottom material from the winter storms still working up. The channel ran south through the passage and the tidal draw showed as a slight corrugation in the surface—the pattern of deep water moving at pace. Cold still; the sea did not warm in April. The northeast wind came in off the open water at twelve knots or so, steady, and the spray reached her intermittently.

She looked down at the seaward face.

The outer course of the granite sat slightly proud of the inner, the way it had always sat—not design, just settlement over the years of frost and load—and made a concavity at water level that caught the backwash in storm weather. In the three-day blow last October the foam would have climbed to the third or fourth course. Now the water worked at the stone with the ordinary persistence of spring and the concavity was clear. The barnacles ran heavy from winter. The kelp had started in the lower angle, dark green and moving with the current. A length of fouled line had lodged in the upper angle sometime in the last week, tangled around the old ring bolt; she made a note to send Noss to clear it.

The harbor behind her was quiet in the early-afternoon way, the outgoing boats out on the grounds and the incomings not yet in sight. The wind was in her ears. The water moved below.

She stood for a time with her boots close to the stone's edge and looked at the seaward face and the water working at it.

Then she turned and walked back along the granite.

---

She made the afternoon log entry at quarter to five.

Tide height at 1417: eleven feet two inches above chart datum. Wind northwest, variable, ten to twelve knots. Weather: improving; the fog advisory had lifted at 1100, which she had logged at the time and carried forward now. One vessel movement since the morning: the Beaumont forty-footer docking at 1623, inbound from Rockland.

She read the entry through.

All columns accounted for.

She closed the log.

The oil lamp made its steady light on the desk. The afternoon was failing—the sun past the bluff, the east window showing the harbour sky going grey at the horizon, the water below it darkening. The lamp's circle fell on the desk surface, the log's worn cover, the pen in its groove beside the inkstand. The shack was quiet.

She put her hands in her lap.

Outside, the harbour ran.
