# Chapter 7 — Owen's Pages

The ice came in from the south. By ten o'clock it had a width to it — not frozen through, but a skim at both flanks of the channel, pressing against the granite of the jetty and the base of the number-two marker. Eira sat at the desk with the lamp turned low and watched it through the window. The channel itself ran open and black between the margins of white. The marker stood plumb. The bell had rung once, at eight-thirty, when the first ice shifted against it; since then, nothing.

She had been in the shack since four in the afternoon. When the temperature dropped below what the tide chart predicted and the south flank had begun to skim, she had carried her overnight kit and supper from the cottage and come down. The number-two marker was five seasons old — new enough that she did not entirely trust it in a hard freeze. The old one had held through worse; she had replaced it for maintenance, not failure, which meant she had less evidence for the new one than she would have liked.

She had gone out at 1800 and again at 2200. Both times the flanks had thickened and both times the channel had been open. She had logged each check in the current volume, come back, and eaten at the desk. Owen's coat was buttoned to her collar; her hands went into the pockets between entries. The oil lamp was as low as she could manage and still write. The tide chart showed the predicted low at 0135; she had penciled a note beside it.

Between the 2200 check and midnight there were two hours in which nothing was supposed to change. The temperature had steadied, had stopped dropping somewhere below twenty, and the flanks had probably put on what ice they were going to put on for the night. The predicted low would narrow the open water slightly and press the flanks, but she had enough history with this marker's anchoring to trust it through moderate pressure. The channel was not going to freeze solid. But she was going to watch it anyway.

The shack was very cold and very quiet. She could hear the harbor — the slight sound of water against the granite when the current shifted, the far-off creak of a dock line somewhere in the marina. No other sound. The lamp flame was the only thing moving in the room.

She would go out at midnight.

The shack frame had a sound at night in winter — settling in the cold, quiet when the temperature had steadied. Tonight it had been quiet for two hours. She reached back and took the old volume from the shelf below the stand.

---

The old volume — started in February of 1941, closed out in early November when the last column filled. The current volume was what she wrote in now. The old one sat on the shelf where old volumes went.

She opened it at random. July 1943: her own hand, the familiar left-ward slant, tide report and vessel traffic, a marginal note about the chart tacking. She turned back. March 1942. December 1941. The columns were neat and complete. She turned further.

The pages changed.

Not the paper — the same stock, the same ruled columns — but the hand. Heavier-pressed, the pen held at a steeper angle, so the downstrokes were thicker than hers and the upstrokes finer. Owen had never corrected in the log; his entries went down complete in the first writing, because he set down what had happened and what had happened was settled.

She had learned to read his entries the way you learn a handwriting after enough years — not the words first but the pressure and the angle, the habit of the pen. His downstrokes were weighted; hers ran light. When she had first taken the log in February of 1936, the shift was visible in the columns from a distance — had there been anyone to see it, which there was no one to be; she had been alone in the shack with his log and her pen. She had not tried to match his hand. The record was the record; it did not ask to be a copy of itself.

She did not need to read his entries. She knew them by the feel.

But she turned through them anyway. April 1936: a vessel logged on a clear morning, the name passing under her thumb without taking. June of the same year: a fog week, seven entries each noting reduced visibility at the channel markers, each complete. The winter of 1937: his hurricane entry ran to three-quarters of a page, wind speeds noted at hourly intervals, the Corcoran vessel's mooring line parting at 0200 and the repair completed and timed. He had logged the man going home.

She had gone looking, years ago, for a gap in his record — some storm week or illness winter where the pressure had been too much and an entry had come up short. She had not found one. Not once in the years she had read backward through the old volumes had his record failed to note a thing he had observed. What he observed, he put down.

His standard was not impossible. It was just his.

She turned to November 1933.

---

Her thumb fell between the pages there. The spine had broken slightly from the number of times she had opened to this section.

*November 19, 1933. Nor'easter. Wind NE, 45 knots gusting 60. Channel: closed 2000 hours. Mooring check 0300: all vessels holding. Agnes Louise on mooring; Corcoran vessel; three at south pier.* Owen had listed them all by name, the way he always did. Then below the main entry, in its own block:

*0100 hours. Vessel observed: unlicensed, running without lights, approximately 30 feet, making for the cove from the NE. Heading 280. Speed approx. 6 knots. Vessel identified (subsequent) as J. Cates, lobsterman, Coldwater. Grounds: outside licensed area. Reported to Rockland: 11/21/33.*

Jared Cates. She had looked him up in the older volumes once, years after, wanting to know what the report had cost him. A man with a decent 34-foot vessel, a habit of working the outer banks when the season went badly enough to require it. Owen's report had cost him a week's hauling rights. He had accepted the warning without a formal appeal.

Owen had written it anyway. The thing had happened; the log recorded what happened. A vessel observed was a vessel reported. There was no column in Owen's log for *observed but not entered* — no room in his practice for that distinction.

Eira set the old volume on the desk and looked at the lamp.

---

She went out at midnight.

Clear and still — the cold that comes when the sky goes empty of cloud and the temperature can fall where it wants. She walked the dock with the lantern to the channel head and knelt at the water's edge.

The ice at the flanks was three-quarters of an inch at the granite. The channel between ran open, the tidal current keeping it moving. The marker stood plumb and the bell was still. She straightened and walked back.

Inside she wrote the 0000 hour entry in the current volume: *Marker: holding. Ice: flanks only; three-quarters inch at granite. Channel: open. Temperature: seventeen degrees.* She blotted it and closed the current volume and set it on the stand.

She picked up the old volume again and turned to October 1943.

---

October 3: the full entry. Body found on the seaward face of the breakwater, 0615 hours, approximately 210 feet from the pier head. The sex, the age as she had estimated it, the clothing in order — coat, trousers, shoes, the leather soles and the absence of grip. The call to Rockland, the constable's arrival, the county examiner's at 1330 with a truck and two assistants and a canvas stretcher. She had written it correctly. She had given it what it required: precision, sequence, the observable facts in the order observed.

She read it through now as she had not read it since the Commander's visit in November. The entry said: Body found. Male. Estimated fifty to sixty years. Clothing as follows. Not identified; case closed. She had written all of that correctly and it was all there and it was all true.

The entry was good because the body had been there to describe. What she had observed on October 2 at 2300 was not a body but two figures at distance, in a storm, for a brief time. She could have described what she observed. She had not done so.

It was, she had always thought, a good entry.

Above it: October 2.

The 0600. The 0900. The 1200. The 1630: *Channel: gale conditions. Vessel traffic: suspended. Wind NE, 40 knots, increasing.*

Then: *2300 hours. Weather: NE gale, 35 knots decreasing at first light. Channel: temporarily impaired.*

The gap between the 1630 entry and the 2300 notation sat on the page without calling attention to itself. If you knew to look for a gap, the notation did not fill it. If you did not know to look — if you were a Commander from Rockland reading through with no reason to find one — the column read as a column reads.

She picked up the pen.

She set the nib just below the 2300 notation, in the space where an additional entry would fall, and wrote:

*October 2, 1943. 2300 hours. Weather—*

She stopped.

What came after the dash: *Dock. Male observed, east dock, approximately 2310. Alive. Approximately fifty-five years. European features. Not alone. Second figure present; not clearly seen. Second figure: person I believe I recognized, though I spoke to neither.*

She could not write it now. Writing it now — three months on, in a log that a Commander from Rockland had reviewed and found satisfactory and closed a case on — meant explaining why she had not written it in October. And explaining October meant the choice had a name.

She had told herself it was waiting, not deciding. It was January now.

She drew a line through what she had written. One line, clean, straight — the standard in a legal document; the original words must remain readable beneath it. She could see them through the line: *October 2, 1943. 2300 hours. Weather—* She initialed it: *E.B.* Then she wrote below:

*[Entry error — see correction below.]*

*Ice report, 0015 hours: Number-two marker holding. Ice: flanks, three-quarters inch. Channel: open.*

She dated it. She blotted it. She set the pen on the stand.

---

She looked at what she had done.

The entry was in the record now. Crossed out, initialed, corrected according to procedure — which was how a legal document handled an error. *October 2, 1943. 2300 hours. Weather—* with a line through it and the initials and the correction notice below. Anyone who read this log closely would see the line. They would see what she had started and not finished. They would not know what came after *Weather—* because she had not written it. But they would know there had been an entry attempted and set aside.

Owen's 1933 entry was thirty pages back, unaltered.

She set the old volume on the shelf. Owen's pages were there. She was not Owen. She had known this since January 1936, when she sat at this desk for the first time — the post and the log all his, carried over to her by the selectman who had been embarrassed to ask a woman and more embarrassed by the others who had declined. She had kept the record eight years. She knew what she had kept and what she had not.

She was not Owen.

It was just past midnight.

She moved her chair to face the window. The channel lay between its white flanks — the open water darker than the ice on either side. The cold came in around the door frame, the same draft it always came in, and the lamp flame bent toward it and then straightened. She put her hands in Owen's pockets.

Three hours until the next check.

The harbor was quiet. The ice was doing what it was doing. She had done what she was going to do tonight and not done what she was not going to do. The lamp burned. The cold held at what it had settled to.

She thought about nothing in particular. She sat.

---

She went out at four.

The ice had not crossed. The channel ran open between the flanks and the marker stood plumb and the bell was still. She knelt on the granite and checked the flank thickness — no thicker than at midnight, possibly thinner at the low-water line where the tide had pushed under it in the night. She stood. The harbor was completely still.

She walked back to the shack and wrote the 0400 entry in the current volume. Temperature, wind — none — channel, ice: *flanks only; channel open; marker: holding.*

---

In January the light came after seven. She sat at the desk with the lamp turned down and waited. The window showed nothing for a long time and then showed the harbor emerging from the dark — water first, a grey movement between the banks, then the far bank itself, the boathouse roof against the beginning sky, the fields beyond it. The ice at the flanks showed white in the first light, two margins of white with the black channel running between. The marker held. The bell was still.

She wrote the morning report. Date, temperature, wind, channel, ice: *flanks only; channel open; marker: holding.*

She closed the current volume and set it on the stand. The old volume was on the shelf below it. The crossed-out entry was in it — correct in its form, visible to anyone who looked closely at October 2.

She put on her coat and went out to check the tide.
