# Chapter 8 — Montreal

The school was empty except for him from eight until noon. He had carried the materials over in the briefcase: the second-year Latin quiz, two composition prompts for the German class, a reading passage adapted from a newspaper text dated to the spring of 1940, which was old enough by now to be permissible in the current circumstances. The policy was not written down but Marcus had read it correctly without being told. He sharpened the pencils in the German room's window crock — a Saturday habit of some months' standing — and spread his notes on the desk and spent two hours drafting quiz questions.

The school produced a particular kind of quiet when the students were absent: different from a library's quiet, different from an empty house. A building designed for forty people at a time held the residue of its ordinary noise in the walls, the corridors, the arrangement of the desks. He found it easier to think in this quiet than in most others available to him. He could hear himself clearly here without the accompanying sense that someone was watching him hear himself.

He drank two cups of coffee from the percolator in the custodian's room. Henry was not in on Saturdays; the percolator ran until whatever time Henry turned it off on Friday afternoons, and it was still on when Marcus arrived at eight. He had been availing himself of this for several months without mentioning it.

He locked the school at noon, came down the front steps, and turned up Prospect Street into the March wind. The quality of the cold had changed in the past two weeks — not warmer but different in its character, the structural cold of deep winter giving way to something thinner, a cold that had conceded its argument without quite leaving. The mud at the road's edge had been frozen solid since December and was now beginning not to be. Bare dirt showed along the south side of the Dunbar fence where the snowpack had thinned. He walked with his collar up and came to the Crow house on the right.

He came in the front door and set the briefcase against the wall.

Lillian was in the kitchen — a drawer pulled open, and then water from the tap, and then quiet. The hallway table held the day's mail: a circular from Atwood's, an envelope bearing the telephone company's mark, and one other piece of mail, set slightly apart from the other two.

He stopped.

A plain white envelope. The return address in the upper left was a suite number on a street in Montreal he did not recognize — the correct caution; Ehrlich would not send from his own address on an envelope going through the American domestic post. The postmark read *Montréal, P.Q.* The date: February 26.

Three weeks in transit. He had addressed and mailed his own letter in the second week of February — an early Saturday morning, before Henry came downstairs, the house quiet, a four-minute walk to the post-box on Prospect Street. He had put the envelope in the box before he had stood there long enough to reconsider the matter. Three weeks from that morning to this.

He took the letter upstairs.

---

His room had the smell of the lamp and the still cold of a room whose radiator had been cycling down since Thursday, when the furnace grate had started to partially block. He sat at the desk without taking off his coat. The envelope opened on ordinary gum.

The letter was two pages, written on good commercial stationery in a hand he recognized: German cursive corrected toward English legibility, the descenders held close, the capitals moderate, the overall impression of a man who had learned to write in one system and was disciplined enough, writing in another language, to remain readable. Ehrlich had always written to him in English. This was the practice between them, established in their first years of correspondence — when correspondence had been possible without the weight it now carried.

*Dear Dr. Klein,*

*Thank you for your letter of the third. I was glad to receive news of your situation in Maine.*

*Regarding your inquiry about German-language reading texts for secondary school use: I would recommend, if obtainable, the Diesterweg graded readers, which were widely used in German schools through the late thirties and are pedagogically sound. Some stock may still be available through the academic book trade in Toronto. The Langenscheidt intermediate series is another possibility; I believe a Montreal supplier carries current stock, and I can provide the contact if it would be useful.*

*I was glad to hear from you in other respects as well. It has been some time.*

*As to the Josef Lutz you mention: I knew him from Sherbrooke, yes. He was released — if my recollection is correct — in November of 1942, some months after I had left myself; he was still in the block when I was discharged. He had a cousin in New York, in the garment district, whom he had been trying to reach for a considerable time. This was still his intention when I last saw him. I know that he left this city in the first days of September of last year, as I helped him with certain of the practical details of the departure, the nature of which I will not set down in a letter. His contacts here and I had expected to hear from him by November at the latest. We have not heard.*

*I had hoped he had simply lost touch. These things happen. If you have heard anything of him, I would be grateful to know it.*

*With regards,*
*S. Ehrlich*

He read it twice. He set it on the desk.

---

The structure of what Ehrlich's letter said, set out in order:

Josef had left Montreal in the first days of September 1943. Ehrlich had helped with the practical details of the departure — which meant, as a matter of inference from what letters of this kind could and could not specify, documents of some kind, a route, possibly money, because organizations of the type Ehrlich ran seldom had money to distribute beyond what each case required, and they weighed cases against one another according to an accounting Marcus could imagine in its categories if not its specific arithmetic. For a man without valid papers and without an American visa, the route south from Montreal to New York did not run directly. It ran east into New England first, along the roads that crossed the Quebec border without much scrutiny, and then south through Maine along the coast, through whatever arrangements obtained in the coastal towns — the kind of arrangements that were available to people who knew the right names and were trusted with them. And then New York at the far end of it.

Six weeks minimum for such a route, traveling carefully and resting where shelter was available. Eight weeks at the outside.

September plus six weeks: mid-October. September plus eight weeks: the first of November.

The body had come in against the breakwater on the morning of October 3, 1943.

This was not certainty. It was an argument from four independently sourced facts — the departure date from Ehrlich, the route from the geography, the indication of destination and timing from the fragment's text (*ankommen*, to arrive; *nicht mehr warten*, can wait no longer), and the date from the newspaper account Marcus had read in the school library the previous October, which had said: *unidentified man, October 1943, found on the Coldwater Reach breakwater following a three-day gale.* Four sources for one conclusion did not produce certainty. They made the distance between the argument and certainty short enough that the distinction had ceased to carry practical weight. He had been treating it as certainty since November; Ehrlich's letter confirmed what he had already concluded, with the fixed departure date added and one further dimension he had not fully accounted for: the waiting. Ehrlich and his contacts in Montreal had been expecting word since November. They had not received any. *I had hoped he had simply lost touch.*

It was March. He had not simply lost touch.

Marcus opened the bottom drawer of the desk. The fragment was face-down under the manila folder where it had been since October. He lifted the folder and turned the fragment over: the brown shadows of the dissolved ink, the heading block with its partial address. *...Lutz, J...* He set Ehrlich's letter on the desk beside the fragment, face-up — the found object and the received reply, the one that had arrived from the sea and the one that had arrived from the post-box on Prospect Street. He looked at them for a moment. Then he placed the manila folder over both of them and closed the drawer.

---

At Sherbrooke, in the B-block, the men had organized a lending arrangement for whatever reading material came through the compound gates. Someone kept a notebook; the books and pamphlets and periodicals were tracked and distributed, and returned when finished, and tracked again. Josef had come to the lending table every two weeks and borrowed everything in German. It did not matter what — the theological tracts, the primer for English-speakers learning German as a foreign language, which Josef did not need and read anyway, perhaps for the particular pleasure of reading something familiar in a form organized for someone who had never known it, the water-stained Fontane novel that had been circulating through the block since spring. He read in the evenings with his back to the wall and his lamp at its highest setting, which was not very high, and he read with the specific attention of a man who is not certain about the next book. Not anxious — specific. He handled the volumes carefully; he returned them without damage. He was an archivist by training and there was something in the way he held a borrowed book — the care with the spine, the way he turned pages from the corner rather than the edge — that could not entirely be trained out of his hands even when the book in question had no particular value and would have survived rougher handling.

He had said once, in the third winter, in German: *The problem with travel is that it is incompatible with libraries.* He had said it as a complaint with a joke inside it. Marcus had laughed.

A man with that habit, moving south from Montreal with whatever Ehrlich's practical details could arrange — he would not have gone without books. One volume, perhaps two. The papers he could not abandon, and the books beside the papers in the same bundle, the same weight carried because it was the only kind of weight worth carrying. Both in the bundle when he came down the coastal road in October in the rain.

Marcus put his coat on the chair. He sat on the edge of the bed.

The radiator ticked once and did not tick again.

From downstairs came the sound of Lillian in the kitchen.

---

At six he went downstairs.

Lillian had made potatoes with salt pork worked through them and dried parsley from the autumn kitchen garden — a ration-book meal in the way of most meals now, the meat distributed through the vegetables rather than present as its own fact, filling and correct in its intentions. Henry was already at the table with his hands folded on the placemat, his newspaper set to the side. From the next room the radio carried the evening news in an even voice — something about the Pacific, the announcer reading from a prepared text without particular urgency.

Marcus sat. He served himself. He ate.

"Furnace grate," Henry said. "I'll look at it Monday."

"I was in my coat at the desk all morning."

"Monday," Henry said.

They ate. Lillian served herself and sat without remark about the meal; she was not a woman who performed satisfaction with food, and Marcus had found this quality restful over the months of eating at this table. The food was the food. They were eating it. The radio shifted from news to a program of music sometime in the middle of the meal, and Henry reached around the doorframe without getting up and turned the dial down. The quiet of the house settled in behind the sound of forks on plates.

After a while the table was cleared.

---

Marcus went upstairs and came back with the composition exercises and spread them at the corner of the table. Henry moved to the armchair; Lillian went to the kitchen. The lamp at the table's center threw a steady light across the papers.

The exercises were from the first-year class: twelve sentences in English rendered into Latin, or attempted. Four had not been attempted; he marked these as blanks, not as errors — a student who understood the limits of their knowledge and declined to guess had made a reasonable accounting. Of the eight attempts, the errors fell into the predictable range: case endings confused under certain prepositions, the subjunctive absent where the construction required it, one student in the consistent habit of declining the second declension as if it belonged to the third. He marked each error in red pencil. He wrote the correct form small in the margin beside the wrong one, so that when the student got the paper back they could see both forms adjacent — the one they had written and the one the sentence wanted.

Henry's newspaper turned in the armchair. From the kitchen came the sound of cups placed in the drying rack, one at a time, and then the water turned off, and then the quieter sound of Lillian putting something away in the far cabinet.

He corrected the genitive in the sixth exercise — *amico* where *amici* was wanted, a confusion between the dative and the genitive that was common in the first year and would not, in most students, survive the second — wrote the form in the margin, and moved to the seventh.
