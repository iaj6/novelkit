# What Henry Knows

The walk from school on a Saturday afternoon in late March took twenty minutes on the standard route — left on Pine, right on Prospect, the Crow house four houses in from the corner. He had internalized its intervals over two and a half years: the corridor to the faculty room, the compositions remaining from Friday, the particular way the school's coal heat loosened its grip the moment you stepped outside. This afternoon he had stayed later than he intended, working through the senior Latin compositions with their ambivalent subjunctives and their occasional inspired moments. By the time he turned from Pine onto Prospect Street it was nearly four o'clock.

The warmth caught him before anything else.

The angle of the sun had shifted in the last week — not enough to call it spring, but enough that the light had temperature in it again for the first time since October. He had forgotten what that felt like. March in Maine was still more winter than not; the warmth was provisional, something the cold had not finished with. But it was there.

He turned onto Prospect Street.

Henry was on the porch.

Not in the chair — the chair was pushed back against the clapboard wall beside the door, where it stayed from October through June and where Henry retrieved it on the first reliably warm evening of the year and not before. He was standing at the porch railing with his hands on the rail, looking at the street. His work coat was unbuttoned. He did not look cold. He looked settled, which was different.

Marcus came up the porch steps. He set his satchel against the post.

"Afternoon," Henry said.

"You're outside," Marcus said.

"Spell like this doesn't come often."

They both looked at the street. The maple at the yard's edge had a faint green at its branch tips — the first suggestion of leaf, barely visible, a week from showing properly. The light off the neighbor's roof lay flat and even, the late-winter light that made everything look a little further from the house than it was.

Marcus sat down on the top step.

---

Henry let the warmth have the silence for a minute, which was his way; he did not begin things until he was ready, and Marcus had learned, in two and a half years, not to reach.

Then Henry said: "I heard something."

He placed it into the conversation the way he placed most things — as a fact, not a disclosure. He did not look sideways to gauge how it landed. He was reporting; the interpretation was Marcus's business.

"About that situation," Henry said. "From before."

"I'm listening," Marcus said.

What Henry had heard had come to him through Deacon Pratt at the Congregational Church. They had been stacking chairs after the men's meeting in February, and Pratt had mentioned it in the way things got mentioned in this register — without urgency, as information shared between men who understand information's weight without dramatizing it. Pratt had heard it from Edna Wills, who did laundry for several farm families out past Route 1. Wills had been told at some point in the winter — December, she thought, or possibly January; she was not certain — by a woman at the Pfeiffer farm. Henry did not know whether it was the wife or a hired girl. The chain had not preserved that detail.

What the woman at the Pfeiffer farm had said: that in October, before the big storm, a man had stayed one night at the farm. A foreign man. A man who spoke the same German as Werner.

Henry's hands were still on the rail.

"Werner knew him," Henry said. "Or knew who he was. Gave him a meal and somewhere dry to sleep. In the morning, drove him toward the coast road. Toward the harbor."

He paused.

"I don't know if that's the man from the breakwater."

His voice was entirely level. He was not theorizing; he was reporting what had traveled to him and was being careful, as he was always careful, to mark the boundary between the report and the conclusion.

Marcus looked at the maple's branch tips.

"I think it is," he said.

A pause.

Henry nodded once.

---

They sat together on the porch — Marcus on the top step, Henry at the rail — while a car went by on Prospect Street and disappeared in the direction of the church corner. The afternoon held its warmth. The light across the road made the neighbor's clapboards look amber. From somewhere on the block there was a door closing, footsteps on a walk.

Henry did not ask how Marcus knew. He did not ask what Marcus planned to do with it. He had given what he had; that was done.

Marcus thought about what kind of transaction this was.

He had said, in November of 1943 — the first winter, the early months after the body — that he thought he may have known who the man was, from before, from another time. He had said no more; Henry had said "that happens in a war" and passed the bread. Nothing further. Marcus had understood, from the quality of Henry's not-asking, that Henry had heard and absorbed what was offered and had held it alongside whatever else Henry held, which was more than he named. Henry had heard the Pfeiffer information in December or January. He had carried it through the rest of the cold months without bringing it to the dinner table or knocking on Marcus's door. He had brought it out today, on a Saturday afternoon in late March, because today was the day for it — because Marcus had come up the walk and Henry had been on the porch and the time had been, apparently, right.

Not simultaneous. Not symmetrical. The balance held across months, each man in possession of what he had, waiting for the right moment of exchange.

A small wind moved through the maple's bare branches.

The green at the tips was there if you looked for it. Small, definite, a fact about what was coming rather than what was here.

---

Henry went inside first; Lillian had started supper and the smell of salt fish had reached the porch. Marcus stayed on the step a moment longer. Then he picked up his satchel and followed.

He said hello to Lillian, who was at the stove. Two sheets of salt fish lay soaking in fresh water in the shallow enamel pan she used for that purpose. She told him half an hour. He went upstairs.

The third step creaked, and the seventh.

---

His room. The desk faced east, toward the school, the window catching the last of the afternoon light at a low angle. The lamp was off. He set his satchel on the chair by the door and crossed to the desk and opened the bottom drawer.

The fragment was there at the front, in its fold of oilskin — the same piece he had found in his spare kit bag and repurposed for exactly this in the autumn of 1943. He took it out. Behind it: Ehrlich's February letter in its Montreal envelope, the pencil-underlined dates still visible through the paper if you held it to the light. He took that out. And at the back of the drawer, where he had placed it in the early spring of 1944: the notebook.

He laid all three on the desk.

The notebook was a narrow exercise book, the same gray cardboard cover as the record books he kept at school. He had begun it in February of 1944, the week after Ehrlich's reply arrived, when he had understood that what he knew required a form outside of memory alone. The entries were in German, in the small compressed hand he had developed in the camp as a way of conserving paper. He had not shown it to anyone.

He opened the oilskin first.

The fragment was what it had always been: the surviving characters of the registration line, the break in the typescript, the syllable of the surname and the initial following it. He had been able to read it without the magnifying glass since approximately March of 1944. He folded it back into the oilskin and set it aside.

He unfolded Ehrlich's letter.

Two pages, handwritten, in Ehrlich's hasty French-inflected German, the sentences running close together without much punctuation because Ehrlich wrote in emergencies even when the situation was not, strictly, an emergency. Marcus read through it. Josef Lutz had been known to Ehrlich's network: released from the Sherbrooke camp in November of 1942, housed in Montreal through the following year while the question of the southern route took shape. He had left in the first days of September 1943. Ehrlich's organization had provided what it could — documents for some portion of the journey, the names of people at certain points along the coastal road; Ehrlich was careful about specifying which — and after September, nothing. No word received. The coastal route had worked for others. What had gone wrong, if something had gone wrong, Ehrlich could not establish.

Marcus refolded the letter into its envelope.

He opened the notebook.

---

The reconstruction he had been building occupied twenty-five pages in the small hand, in the form of a scholar's working notes: entries by date where possible, by place where not, each piece of evidence identified by its source and its degree of certainty. He read through it from the beginning.

*Sherbrooke. Block 7-C. Josef Lutz. Ich kannte ihn.* (I knew him.)

The bunk placement. The borrowed books — handled with archivist's care, spine supported, pages turned from the corner, as if the books were documents rather than objects for reading, which to Josef they may have been. The habit he had of sitting with his back to a wall and watching the room's two doors. The German he spoke in the camp: Viennese, educated, the standard form kept deliberately distinct from the dialect of home, the vowels exact.

*November 1942. Freigelassen. Montreal.* (Released. Montreal.)

*Simon Ehrlich. Das Netzwerk.* (The network.)

*Abreise: erste Septembertage 1943.* (Departure: first days of September 1943.)

*Die Küstenstraße, Richtung Süden.* (The coastal road, heading south.)

*Das Fragment. Fundort: Breakwater, Coldwater Reach, Maine. Oktober 1943.* (The fragment. Found at: breakwater, Coldwater Reach, Maine. October 1943.)

*Ehrlich: kein Wort empfangen. Letzter bekannter Aufenthaltsort: Küste von Maine.* (Ehrlich: no word received. Last known location: coast of Maine.)

He came to his most recent entry, written in early October of 1944:

*Brief an seine Schwester gesandt. Mehr ist mir nicht möglich.* (Letter sent to his sister. More than this is not possible for me.)

He turned to the blank page below it.

He uncapped the pen.

He wrote, in German, in the small careful hand:

*24. März 1945. Werner Pfeiffer, Landwirt, Coldwater Reach, Maine, hat Josef Lutz eine Nacht beherbergt, vor dem Oktobersturm 1943. Hat ihn am folgenden Morgen zur Küstenstraße gefahren — in Richtung Hafen. Dies aus zweiter Hand, dreifach gehört; nicht bewiesen; wahrscheinlich wahr.*

He set the pen down.

He read what he had written.

*March 24, 1945. Werner Pfeiffer, dairy farmer, Coldwater Reach, Maine, sheltered Josef Lutz one night, before the October storm of 1943. Drove him the following morning to the coastal road — in the direction of the harbor. This heard second-hand, passed through three hands; not proved; probably true.*

---

He sat with the notebook open and considered what it now contained.

Josef had left Montreal in early September of 1943 and had traveled south along the coastal route, with documents and the names of contacts along the way. He had reached inland Maine — three miles from the harbor — and Werner Pfeiffer, who had known him from Bavaria or known of him through whatever channel had guided Josef this far, had taken him in for one night. Had given him a meal and a dry place to sleep. In the morning had driven him toward the coast road.

He had reached the harbor. Karl Reinmar — a German POW on the Pfeiffer farm — had seen him on the coastal road in the evening, moving in the direction of the water. Marcus knew this only because a detail from an unrelated thread of information had reached him indirectly; he did not know how he knew it, precisely, except that he did.

He had walked to the dock.

What had happened at the dock was not in the notebook, because Marcus did not have it. Whether Werner had stayed with him — whether Werner had driven him to the harbor road and turned back toward the farm, or had accompanied him to the dock, or had been standing on the pier when Josef fell — Marcus could not say. Henry's account ended at the coast road in the morning. The dock was several hours later and three miles further, and the gap between them was not his to cross.

He had the departure from Montreal. He had the night at the Pfeiffer farm. He had the coastal road. He had the fragment found in the clothing.

Between the coastal road and the breakwater's seaward face, two hundred feet from the pier head: nothing. That was the shape of what he knew.

He closed the notebook.

He returned it to the drawer at the back, behind Ehrlich's letter in its envelope, behind the fragment in its oilskin fold. He closed the drawer. The catch engaged.

He sat for a moment at the desk. Through the east window, the school building was visible in the late afternoon light — the bell cupola, the flagpole, the flag moving slightly in whatever wind remained. He had been in that building for two and a half years; he had made himself useful without drawing attention; he had kept this drawer closed against everything that had pressed against it. The reconstruction was as complete as it would be. He had assembled what he could reach, through the sources available to him — memory, correspondence, the informal network that moved through communities where formal channels did not apply. He had sent the minimal truth to the sister. He had told Henry he thought he had known the man. He had written it all down in a language most of the town could not read easily, in a book that looked like a record book, in a drawer that held what it held.

He got up and turned on the lamp.

---

Henry was at the kitchen table when Marcus came downstairs, the newspaper open in front of him and not being read, which was how Henry sat with a newspaper sometimes — holding the reading position while the newspaper went unread, which Marcus had decided was less inattention than a form of thinking that required the newspaper as a prop. Marcus asked Lillian if she needed anything from the kitchen and she handed him the salt bowl to put on the table.

They ate at seven.

She had soaked the fish long enough that it separated properly in the pan, with onion and with the potatoes she had cooked down until they absorbed what the fish gave off. She set a pot of coffee beside the stove. Henry poured for both of them without being asked.

Marcus took what was on his plate and ate it. The fish was better than it had any right to be. Lillian did something in the soaking that he had observed over two and a half years without learning to replicate. He ate everything and placed the bones correctly in the small bowl on the side.

The radio was on in the front room, left running while they ate; the evening news came through the doorway in fragments — the European Theatre report, the Allied armies moving east of the Rhine now, the German cities falling in a sequence that the newsreader named with the steady precision of someone reading coordinates rather than places. Marcus knew some of the cities. Others he did not. He was aware that the place-names the newsreader was saying were the names of places where people lived, had lived, had arranged their ordinary lives around, and that those places were changing faster than ordinary life could absorb.

Henry turned his head slightly toward the doorway.

"Getting close," he said.

"Yes," Marcus said.

Lillian set the coffee pot back on the stove and sat down.

They ate without hurrying. The radio continued through the doorway. The kitchen held its warmth from the stove. When Henry's cup was empty, he refilled it himself and did not offer commentary on the newsreader's names, which was, Marcus had learned, Henry's way of listening carefully.

---

After supper, Marcus helped carry the plates to the kitchen and said goodnight to Lillian, who had the dishes in the basin and was working through them without ceremony. He said goodnight to Henry, who had moved to the front room and was sitting close to the radio, the newspaper folded on his knee.

He went upstairs.

The third step creaked, and the seventh.

In his room he sat down at the desk without turning the lamp on. The east window held the last of the evening light — a dark blue that was not yet full dark, the school building a shape against it. The radio voice came up through the floorboards from below, a man naming a place, another man's voice confirming the distance, the bridgehead, the units in position. Each name was a fact about the closing shape of the thing.

Josef had wanted to get to New York. A distant cousin who had made it in the 1920s, before the window for such things closed. He had been three hundred miles short of it when Werner Pfeiffer drove him to the coast road.

Marcus sat in the dark for a while.

Then he turned on the lamp, reached to the shelf for the atlas, and opened it to the European page.

He found the Rhine. He put his finger on it and followed it east along the map, naming the cities to himself as the radio continued below.
