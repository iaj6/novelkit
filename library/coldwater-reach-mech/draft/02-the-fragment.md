# Chapter 2 — The Fragment

The window in the fourth-period classroom faced south, over the road and the bluff edge, and from it on a clear day the harbor was visible — the channel, the mooring field, the gray stone run of the breakwater. Marcus stood at the window after the Latin students had gone, the eraser still in his hand, looking down at the water. The channel was open. He had passed the harbormaster's shack on his walk to school that morning and it had looked like any morning; the CHANNEL CLOSED board was gone from its post. He turned back to the board.

He was erasing the conjugation table when someone stopped in the doorway.

A young man. Naval officer's uniform, a lieutenant's bars on the collar, a manila envelope held at his side with the precise care of something he had been told to account for. He was perhaps twenty-five, perhaps twenty-six, and he had the look of someone who found school buildings subtly wrong — the chalk smell, the low ceiling, the small benches arranged in rows, the entire category of it. He looked at Marcus with the expression of an errand.

"Dr. Klein. Lieutenant Graves, Rockland district office. The secretary said I might find you here."

"You have." Marcus set the eraser on the chalk rail. He came around the nearest bench. "What can I do for you?"

Graves produced the envelope. He held it out flat, face up, with that same precise care. "We recovered a fragment of paper from the clothing of the man found on the breakwater last month. There are words on it. In German. We were told you're the German teacher."

"I am," Marcus said. He took the envelope.

---

The flap was sealed with adhesive tape. Marcus worked at the tape carefully with a fingernail and drew the contents out onto his palm.

Three inches by two, roughly. It had once been part of a larger piece — the edges were not cut but dissolved, the paper's fiber failing outward from what had been an original tear, so that the boundary between document and nothing was a gradation rather than a line. The ink had run badly. In most places it had spread through the grain of the paper and been taken by the water altogether, leaving brown shadow rather than black letter, abstraction rather than text. He moved to the window and held the fragment up.

In a few places the writing held. Where the ink had been laid down with more pressure — more attention, or more urgency, something that drove the strokes deeper into the fiber before the water had its chance — the letters had outlasted the damage. In the upper third, the remnant of what might have been the start of a salutation: a curved form that the water had met at the second stroke, leaving the first intact and nothing after it. In the body of the letter, two words he could read without hesitation: *kalt* — cold — and, a space away, *ankommen* — to arrive, to reach a destination. Between them a gap from which several lines of text had been taken entirely, the paper there as blank as if it had never been written on.

Near the top of the fragment, in a position consistent with a heading or address block, written in a slightly more upright hand than the body text — a different register, as if the person writing understood that the name was the thing most necessary to survive — a surname, and an initial.

*…Lutz, J…*

He looked at the name for a moment. Then he turned from the window.

"It is a fragment of a personal letter," he said. "Possibly a short note. The legible words in the body suggest cold and arrival — *kalt* means cold; *ankommen* means to arrive, or to reach one's destination. The heading or address block contains a surname and an initial: Lutz, J. Everything else is unrecoverable. Where the paper's fiber has failed, the text is gone."

Graves wrote this in a small notebook. He was thorough in the unhurried way of someone whose thoroughness was a professional trait rather than a personal one.

"Is there more that might come clear? Better light, more time?"

Marcus considered the paper in his hand. He was holding it by the edges. "No," he said. "What is legible is legible because the ink reached further into the paper. The rest has been taken. There is no better light for a question the material itself cannot answer."

Graves put the notebook away. He looked at the fragment, then at Marcus. He was not a suspicious man; he was a man with an errand, and the errand was nearly finished. "I'd appreciate it if you'd keep it a few days," he said. "Have another look. The county examiner's office is still working the case."

"Of course," Marcus said.

He put the fragment back in the envelope. He put the envelope in his coat pocket. Graves thanked him, left his number with the school secretary, and went; his steps went down the hallway in the ordinary unhurried way and diminished. The room was quiet: chalk dust in the window light, the conjugation table half-erased on the board, the passive forms of *amāre* fading from left to right where he had stopped.

---

He walked home along Prospect Street in the last of the afternoon, past the post office and the church and the maple that had shed most of its leaves. The air was cold and held no wind. The envelope was in his coat pocket. He was aware of it the way one becomes aware of a small thing that has been present long enough to feel native to its location — not heavy, not attended to constantly, but present in the way that a specific weight acquires specificity over the course of a day spent not attending to it.

At the Crow house Lillian had made potato soup. She set it on the table without ceremony, brought the coffeepot, and went back to the stove with the economy of a woman who had long since decided that the space between the stove and the table was where she thought best. Henry was already in his chair. He had come home early, he said, because the furnace register in the second-floor corridor had pulled loose from its fitting — a screw problem, easily addressed — and he had wanted to address it before the end of the day.

"The building superintendent would have sent someone," Marcus said.

Henry said: "The building superintendent would have sent someone on Friday." He did not look up from his bowl.

They ate. The radio in the parlor was on at low volume — the evening news, Italy, a road, the calculated cost of something being taken. Marcus had been a European before he had been a resident alien, and when he heard the Italian cities he conducted, privately, the reckoning that the cities required of him, and then he ate his soup. Lillian came in once to refill the coffeepot. She moved through the room the way she always moved through it, which was to say she moved through it as if she owned it, which she did, and as if she were aware of what was in it, which she was.

Marcus thanked her for the soup when she cleared the plates.

"It's potato soup," she said. "I'll take thanks when it's worth the thanks." But she paused in the doorway with the stack of bowls, and the pause had in it the character of something in the neighborhood of affection, and she went in.

Henry folded his hands around his coffee cup. Outside, the maple stood bare against the last grey light. "Good soup," he said.

"Yes," Marcus said.

He went upstairs. The third step creaked, and the seventh. Below him, after a moment, the sound of the kitchen tap.

---

His room was the front room on the second story: a desk, a lamp, a narrow bed, a shelf of books along the south wall — three Latin grammars, a German dictionary, and a copy of Rilke's *Duino Elegies* in a paperback edition that had been in his bag since London and that he had not opened in nearly two years. The window looked east toward the school. The school was dark now; the light over the entrance burned its small steady amber on the sidewalk below.

He draped his coat over the back of the chair. He stood at the window a moment, looking at the entrance light and the empty sidewalk. Then he sat at the desk and took the envelope from his coat pocket and set it under the lamp.

Under the lamp the fragment looked different from how it had looked by the classroom window. The lamp was warm; its warmth brought the shadows in the paper up more clearly. He spent some time — he did not count the minutes — with the paper tilted at different angles in the light, working at what had been asked of him: more, if there was more. In the salutation there was a curve before the dissolved letters that might be the surviving arc of an *A*, or the bottom of a *D*, or neither of these things. The phrase in the body — *kalt* and then the gap and then *ankommen* — gave up nothing further. The gap held what it held, which was a shadow, which was not a word.

He put the paper flat on the desk. He looked at the name.

*Lutz, J.*

The ink there was steady. The letters held their edges without bleeding into the surrounding fiber. It was written in the hand of someone who had understood, when writing it, that the name was the thing most necessary to get down.

Josef Lutz had been at the camp at Sherbrooke for some months when Marcus arrived. He was Viennese — not in the way Marcus was Viennese, not the university version, the assimilated professional version that had believed for too long in its own acceptability and had been made to understand this error in specific terms — but from a different district, a different formation, a different set of available strategies for surviving the discovery that one's acceptability had been provisional. He was a few years younger than Marcus — thirty-nine or forty or forty-one; he was the sort of man who did not offer his age, not from vanity but from the habit of withholding the details that made you locatable. He spoke four languages and said he had a fifth poorly, and Marcus had never learned which fifth it was.

He was quiet. His quietness was not timidity but economy — he spoke when speech served a purpose and was still otherwise, and he had the habit of always knowing where the doors were. Not nervously. He watched them with the settled attention of a man for whom the character of doors — which way they swung, whether they latched, how loud they were, how often they opened and which kinds of people used them — had, at some point, been information worth having.

He had no valid Canadian papers after the camp, which was in some respects why he had been in the camp. He had a cousin in New York, a distant cousin who had gone there in the twenties and was, presumably, still there. Josef was trying to get to him. He said this as a man states a next step that has been interrupted by circumstances he did not generate and does not consider to have permanent authority over his plans. He was trying to get there.

Marcus had been released in the late summer of 1942, by the intervention of a Quaker relief organization that had found him a position at the high school through channels he had never been able to reconstruct to his satisfaction, and he had arrived in Coldwater Reach in September. He had not seen Josef after his own release. He had heard, in the uncertain way of displaced persons whose networks depend on letters that do not always arrive and on people who are not always where they were last heard from, that Josef had been released in late 1942. Or perhaps early 1943; the date did not hold its shape when Marcus tried to fix it.

In the time since, Marcus had told himself that Josef had made it to New York. This was not knowledge. It was a practice — the same practice he had adopted for a number of people from the camp whose subsequent fates were not available to him, the assignment of the better outcome, because the worse outcome required a kind of reckoning he did not have room for. He had been, on the whole, disciplined about it. He had not thought about Josef Lutz since April, and when he had thought about him then it was briefly, and the conclusion had been the same: the cousin, the apartment, the matter resolved. He had thought about him the way one thinks about a closed question.

He looked at the name on the paper.

---

Graves had left his number with the school secretary. In the morning Marcus could find it without difficulty. He could call and report that a further examination had yielded nothing beyond what he had said in the classroom, which was close to accurate: the paper had not given him more than he had translated on his feet. He could return the envelope. He could remain the German teacher, the useful man, the one you brought your translation problems to — occupying the precise amount of usefulness that was required and no further amount.

The difficulty was categorical. He could report nothing further only if what he now held was nothing further, and what he held was a name he knew. A name he knew was not nothing. He had spent the day in the category of useful-and-no-more, and the category had been available to him, and he had occupied it with what he judged was reasonable competence, and it was now past nine in the evening and the category had closed.

He pulled the envelope toward him. He folded down the flap, tucked the fragment inside, and creased it shut.

He opened the bottom drawer of the desk. He set the envelope face-down at the back of it, under the edge of a manila folder. He closed the drawer.

He sat for some time with the lamp on. Through the window the school's entrance light made its small circle on the empty sidewalk. He had known Josef Lutz, who watched doors, who had a cousin in New York, who had been — by Marcus's careful, disciplined, two-year accounting of the matter — all right.

He reached up and turned the lamp out.

The entrance light glowed amber through the glass. Below the floor the house was still.

He did not open the drawer.
