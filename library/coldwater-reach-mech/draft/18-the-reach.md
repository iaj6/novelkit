# The Reach

By four-fifteen she was at the desk. The lamp was on, its light across the log table and the pen tray and the spring chart folded under the glass weight at the far corner — the eastern grounds, current lines she'd updated from the new tide tables. The shack was cold; she had not lit the stove, and in late April the cold before dawn was still the winter's cold, coming off the water through the boards. She had the window cracked. She could hear the McCready brothers on the dock, loading bait boxes onto the *Annalisa K.* before the engine was running.

She shut the window when they ran the engine. She heard them come out of the harbor mouth, the engine note rising and then dropping when the bow hit open water. Then nothing but the water and the markers.

She wrote the preliminary entry in the new volume. *April --, 1945. Wind WSW, 8 knots. Barometric 30.12, steady. Clear. One vessel, Annalisa K., departed 0430 for the grounds.* She wrote the vessel's name in full, the departure time exact; she had the time by the engine sound, the pitch-drop when a vessel cleared the mouth, which she could place to within three minutes. She set the pen in the tray.

The channel markers held their interval outside the window. The east was not light yet. She could hear the water at the breakwater stone — the tide on the ebb, working at the granite in the particular repetition she had listened to every morning for nine years, the way you no longer hear the clock in a room you have lived in long enough. Across the access road, Tibbetts had his light on. She could see the yellow square of it above the road. He had kept the radio going through the night since January; she was here before dawn often enough to have learned the pattern of it. The war in Europe was ending. Everyone with a radio was sitting with it. She had heard it through walls and glass for months now — cities named, distances closed, the tone of the announcer's voice when what he was saying was different from the day before.

She stood.

She crossed to the shelf. The old volume was between her 1942 volume and the new one she'd started in January — *Nov 1942–Dec 1944* in her own hand on the label, the label straight, the spine undamaged. She had shelved it in January, on the evening after she had written the weather line into the October space, and had not taken it down since. She took it down.

She had not planned this morning specifically. She had known since the Berlin news that the morning was coming — had known it in the way she knew a weather change before the barometric fell, the pressure sitting differently against the work. She carried the volume to the desk and set it down beside the new one.

She opened it to October.

The page had not changed since January. She knew its layers the way you know a piece of ground you have crossed many times: her own gale entry from October 2, the weather and the channel conditions and at the foot of the column in the 2300 row the notation she had put down that morning. *Weather: NE gale, 35 knots, continuing. Channel: temporarily impaired.* Below it: the single crossing-out from January of 1944, the line she had drawn through the sentence she had started and stopped, her initials and the date beside it per the correction procedure. The correction notice in its column. The ice report she had entered as the correcting entry. Then, in the block she had opened below it last January: *October 2, 1943. 2300 hours. [Correction to above entry.] Weather: NE gale, 35 knots, continuing.*

Below that: the space she had been carrying since January of 1945, and in a different sense since October of 1943.

She had been thinking about what it should say for several weeks. She had not changed what she thought.

She picked up the pen.

She wrote, below the January correction, in the continuation format:

*Dock: one vessel observed docking at approximately 2300. One person at the pier.*

She set the pen down.

The column below was blank.

She looked at it.

She waited for the ink to dry.

Then she closed the volume.

She crossed to the shelf and put it back.

Owen's volumes were at the far end: 1933, then 1934–1935, the pages of that one cockled from the spring that had come through the eave before she'd had the roof re-done, the spine straight. She knew his handwriting without opening them — the vessel names in a column with the departure times to the right, the heading notation he had used that she had learned alongside him and kept because it was correct and because it was his. After Owen's volumes: her own, beginning with 1936. She had written small and deliberately in that first year, the handwriting of someone establishing a practice she intended to keep. Then 1937, 1938, 1939 — the Depression years, thin harbor traffic, thin volumes. Then the war years, thicker with convoy coordination and the additional reporting requirements from Rockland. The volume she had just shelved fit where it belonged in the sequence: after Owen's, after her own years, in its place.

She stood with her hand on the spine of it.

She stood there for a moment.

Then she took her hand away.

---

Across the road a door opened. The radio was briefly loud — she heard the word *unconditional* — and then the door swung back without closing fully, Tibbetts's screen door sitting the way it always sat. She could hear the announcer's voice through the gap in the door across the road. She could not make out the sentences.

She heard *surrender*. And then *Germany*.

She stood in the shack. Outside the window the channel markers held their interval; the breakwater ran its straight line from the pier head. The harbor was the same.

She went out.

The access road was dark. Tibbetts's porch light was the only light on this side. She turned the other way, toward the breakwater.

In late April the harbor before full light smelled of low tide and cold salt and the faint diesel from the overnight work. The fog signal at the outer station was off; the visibility was clear in both directions. She walked out along the pier and then onto the breakwater itself, the granite stones underfoot, the sound of the water on both sides — the harbor's sheltered surface to her left and the open water to her right with its longer, heavier motion. The cold was the cold of late April in Maine, which was still the winter's cold but thinner at its edges, something you could see through to the morning coming.

The channel markers were lit. The outer ledge marker was holding its position; she had noted the list since November, the mooring cable working in the current. She had been meaning to get out to it by dinghy when the weather held for a day. Tonight it was showing correct.

She walked to the end of the breakwater.

At the end the seaward face was on both sides of her. She stood at the pier head and looked at the angle below — the granite footing, the place where the current ran in toward the stone when the wind came from the northeast. She knew this place. A broken trap frame had come in there two weeks ago; she had logged it and arranged for the harbor authority to collect it. Before that: planking from one of the outer islands, loose in the December blow. Before that: what the water brought to this corner in the ordinary course of things.

In October of 1943, a man had come in there. Against that stone, in that angle.

The stone was the same. The water was the same.

She looked at it.

Then she turned and walked back.

---

The light was coming. The east was still dark but the quality of dark had changed — deep blue now where it had been black, the blue that preceded the sky separating itself from the ridge line. The harbor surface had gone from black to dark silver. She could see the shapes of the vessels at their berths clearly now, not only their running lights. A cormorant pushed out from under the dock as she came back down the pier; she heard its wings on the water and then it was up and gone into the grey.

By the time she reached the shack the sky above the eastern ridge had gone pale. She stood outside the shack door for a moment. Tibbetts's radio was still going across the road. It was just sound now. The harbor was ordinary in the way it was every morning — the water, the markers, the vessels at their mooring, the cold that would be gone by noon.

She went inside.

She wrote in the new volume: *0524. Breakwater walk completed. Seaward face and channel markers: condition normal. Outer ledge marker: list unchanged from prior entry; cable check pending when weather allows.*

She set the pen in the tray.

The mooring lines.

She went back outside. The commercial slips ran east from the pier head. Three vessels were in overnight: two lobster boats at the outside berths and the McCready relief boat at the inside berth, backed in the way the McCready family had always backed it, stern to the dock, bow toward the channel. She went down the slips in order, east to west, the way she had always worked them. Each cleat first: the condition of the steel, the mounting bolts, nothing backing out from the winter's movement. The chafing gear where the line ran over the dock edge, and whether it was wearing through. The line's tension, which you felt through the hand before you looked at it; a slack line was the one thing you acted on without pausing to note it first. The condition of the bitter end, coiled on the cleat and dry. The bow painters. The spring lines. The stern lines. She worked through each boat without hurrying, which was the only way to do it, because hurrying was how you missed the thing that was working loose.

The light changed while she worked. When she came off the first boat the sky to the east was pale grey. By the second boat it was beginning to separate into color — the ridge line dark against a sky that was no longer the same color as the water. By the time she finished the last cleat on the third boat and stood up and stepped back onto the dock, the light was full and clean. The harbor had gone from dark silver to the pale green of spring mornings when the air was still cold and the sun was coming. The color she knew better than she could name. The outer ledge marker blinked green against the sky. The ridge to the east was bright. A gull came over the breakwater low and went south without pausing.

She stood at the end of the commercial dock for a moment, her hands at her sides.

The harbor was sound.

---

She walked back to the shack and went inside. The stove was still cold; she would light it now or wait. She sat down at the desk.

The new volume was open to the morning's entry. She looked at what she had written so far — the preliminary weather and the vessel entry, the breakwater walk and the channel condition and the outer ledge note. She looked at the old volume on the shelf at the back wall. Its spine was there among the others, in its place. The sequence ran from Owen's 1933 to this morning.

She picked up the pen.

She wrote in the new volume:

*Mooring lines checked and found sound.*
