# Chapter 9 — The Road

The harness wanted sorting, and Karl was doing it: collar from collar, the throatlatch lines unwound from where they'd gathered through the winter, each piece held briefly and appraised and set on the appropriate nail or left on the bench for a second pass. He worked with the same unhurry he worked everything, and Hannah had been watching him for two weeks now — from across the yard, over a fence rail, from the farmhouse window above the kitchen sink — and she had known, watching, that the sorting was not what she was waiting for.

The wagon shed door stood open across the yard. Oakes had carried his flask over at half past two, moving with the slow care of a man whose knees had their opinions about the cold. She had watched him cross. She had not seen him come out.

Werner was in the upper field with the other three. She had watched him go up the hill at noon.

She picked up the feed bucket from beside the tack room door and walked into the corridor.

Karl looked up when she came in. His hands didn't stop — they found the buckle they'd been reaching toward and set it on the bench — but he looked at her and the looking was a full sentence, the kind that didn't need words to say what it said.

She came in far enough for the barn to close around her. The smell of the winter still lived here: old hay, the cold that kept itself in the boards long after the air outside had shifted, the particular breath of the milk cows below. Outside, along the south fence, the snow had pulled back and the dark mud had come up in long tongues. In the upper field the other three were moving between fence posts, Werner among them, his back toward the barn.

She set the bucket down. She did not pick up anything else.

Karl's hands were quiet on the bench.

The silence between them had a texture. She had learned, over these weeks, that Karl's silences were not nervous — they were a kind of patience, an economy her father's silences had the same quality of, though she would not have said this to either of them. She stood, and he waited, and outside a crow called once from the direction of the upper pasture and did not call again.

"Ich hab' etwas," she said. *I have something.*

He waited.

She had opened the bag seam the night before, by the lamp in her room. The sewing scissors, the blade turned sidewise, the black thread cut one stitch at a time so the canvas didn't pucker. Six stitches and then the seventh came with them, and she had pulled the note out flat and looked at it under the lamp — his handwriting, the four folds, the pencil lines slightly silvered under the lamplight — and then she had folded it back into its four folds and set it in her coat pocket. She had lain awake for a while after that, listening to Werner's radio come through the floor.

She had carried the note in her pocket all through the morning, through the first milking and the hay and the carrying of things that needed carrying. It had pressed through the fabric when she moved.

She reached into her coat pocket now and took it out.

She held it toward him with his side facing him. His handwriting — the Allgäu hand, the pencil-pressed letters she had read often enough to know without reading.

He looked at the note in her hand. He looked at her. He didn't take it.

"Das hier," she said. "Der Mann auf dem Weg. Erzähl mir davon."

*This. The man on the road. Tell me.*

She kept her voice level — the level that meant nothing to someone listening for a particular language, the level she used for German that was not meant to be German from across a yard. The barn corridor was not private: Werner could come back through the upper door, Oakes could round the corner from the wagon shed. But the flask was across the yard and Werner was in the upper field and these were the minutes she had.

Karl looked once toward the open barn door, toward the light of the April yard. Then he looked back at her. She could see him deciding — not whether to speak but how, the same care that had gone into the note in the first place, the care of a person who understood that words, once out, couldn't be called back.

He pulled a length of leather from the pile on the bench and began working it through his hands — the motion of someone doing something while saying something else.

"Ein Donnerstag," he said. "Oder Freitag. Anfang Oktober." *A Thursday. Or Friday. Early in October.*

She was still holding the note.

"Der Truck kam von Bangor zurück." *The truck was coming back from Bangor.* His voice stayed low and even; his eyes stayed on the leather. "Wir hatten einen Auftrag in einer Konservenfabrik, den ganzen Tag. Auf dem Rückweg hat es geregnet — den ganzen Abend schon, schlechte Straßen." *We had an assignment at a cannery, the whole day. On the way back it had been raining — all evening, the roads were bad.*

She knew about the cannery runs: Oakes had mentioned it once, in passing, the way he mentioned things he considered procedural fact and not conversation. October, early in the month, before the winter reduction had come.

"Bei Wilder's Landing," Karl said — *at Wilder's Landing* — "lag ein Ast auf dem Weg. Groß genug, dass der Fahrer langsamer gemacht hat." *there was a branch across the road. Large enough that the driver slowed down.*

The Landing. She had driven past it with her father perhaps a dozen times in daylight: a wide curve in the coastal road where the white pines overhung the verge and you could see a long way south on clear days, toward where the town lights would be. At night, in October rain, through the window of a transport truck — the headlamps would reach only so far, and beyond them the dark and the trees and whatever was on the road.

"Auf der linken Seite," Karl said. "Durch das Fenster — ein Mann zu Fuß." *On the left side. Through the window — a man on foot.*

He was quiet for a moment, not for effect but genuinely placing himself back in that cab, in the wet and the dark and the branch in the headlamps.

"Wie alt?" she said. *How old?*

He considered. "Älter als ich. Vielleicht fünfzig. Vielleicht ein bisschen mehr." *Older than me. Perhaps fifty. Perhaps a little more.* "Schmächtig. Kein Bauer — das konnte man sehen, auch in dem Regen." *Slight. Not a farmer's build — you could see that, even in the rain.*

Not from here, then. The shape of something she had been carrying since December began to settle against his words, the way a fit you weren't expecting turns out to be exactly right.

"Er trug etwas," Karl said. "Eine Tasche, oder ein Bündel — er hielt es nah am Körper, schwer zu erkennen im Regen." *He was carrying something. A bag, or a bundle — he held it close to his body, hard to make out in the rain.*

She thought about smooth-soled shoes. What Jess Atwood had said at the counter in November, relaying Tibbetts: *came from inland, you could tell by the shoes.* She thought about the note in her hand and the four folds of it and what was written in the last two lines.

"Er hatte einen langen Mantel," Karl said. *He had a long coat.* "Nicht so einen für den Regen. Einen Stadtmantel." *Not one for the weather. A city coat.*

Not a fisherman's coat. Not an oilskin.

"Er ging in Richtung Wasser."

*He was moving toward the water.*

He let that sit. She let it sit with him. Outside, the April yard was quiet — the scratch of a branch somewhere against the upper barn's east wall, the distant sound of Werner's voice to one of the others in the field.

"Ich hatte vielleicht zehn Sekunden," Karl said. "Wegen des Astes hat der Fahrer gebremst und dann sofort weitergemacht." *I had perhaps ten seconds. Because of the branch the driver braked and then moved on immediately.*

Ten seconds of a rain-fogged window on an October night. Enough. She thought: enough to see what he was and where he was going.

"Und dann," Karl said — and there was a particular quality to the pause before it, the pause of a thing said for the first time — "hat er sich umgedreht. Er hat den Truck gesehen. Direkt hineingeschaut, durch den Regen." *And then — he turned around. He looked at the truck. Looked directly at it, through the rain.*

She waited.

"Und ist einfach weitergelaufen." *And just kept walking.*

He had not stopped. Had not flagged the truck, not moved toward the road's center, not raised his arm. He had looked at the headlamps coming through the rain and turned back and kept on, moving in the direction of the water, at the pace of a man going somewhere.

Going somewhere. She held on to that.

Karl set the length of leather down on the bench. He was looking at the barn floor now, at the boards that had held horses and cattle and men's boots through decades she couldn't number.

"War es der Mann?" he said, quietly. *Was it that man?*

He meant the one. The one with the smooth-soled shoes and no papers and no name on the stone in the town cemetery. He had put it together from her questions or from her face or from the fact of the feed bucket she didn't need; she couldn't have said which. He had put it together and he was looking at her and he was nineteen years old and he had written those two sentences in November and pressed the note into her palm and he had known then, she thought, in the way you know things you can't say.

"Ich glaube schon," she said. *I think so.*

He absorbed this without moving. He didn't look away from her, and she didn't look away from him. There was something in his stillness that she recognized from the night she had first read the note — the quality of someone who has been carrying a thing for a while and now has set it down, not into safety but into the world, where it would have weight in a way it hadn't had before.

"Was denkst du, was passiert ist?" he said. *What do you think happened?*

She opened her mouth and closed it.

She thought about the ten seconds at Wilder's Landing, the man looking at the headlamps through the rain and then turning away and continuing on his own direction. She thought about the harbor, which she had not seen herself but had heard about from Jess Atwood's cousin's wife the following week: the gale that had started that night or the next morning and the body on the breakwater seaward face at 0615. Between a man walking with purpose toward the water and the body found in the morning — that distance was not something she could close. She did not know who was at the dock or what the dock had looked like that night or what had happened between his arriving there and his not leaving.

"Ich weiß es nicht," she said.

She meant it.

He nodded once, a single slow dip of his head, and reached for the harness buckle again.

---

Across the yard: boots on the wagon shed threshold, the familiar sound — a flat first step, a heavier second step where Oakes's right knee found the drop.

Karl was already working. She picked up the feed bucket.

By the time Oakes came around the corner of the barn she was tipping grain into the nearest bin, the dry sound of it filling the corridor, and Karl had a collar on the right nail and was reaching for the next.

Oakes looked in at them. He pulled the front of his coat together against the April wind, which still had winter in it this close to the water.

"Cold today," he said.

"Ja," Karl said, without looking up.

Oakes moved on, toward the lower gate, his boots loud on the mud.

Hannah set the empty bucket down. She didn't look at Karl and he didn't look at her. She picked up the bucket and walked out of the barn and across the yard toward the milk house, the note in her coat pocket pressing through the fabric in its four folds, and she carried it the way she had learned to carry it — as a plain fact about the day, neither more nor less.

---

Marta had gone to bed early, headache reaching up from the back of her neck in the way that meant the next morning too. Werner was in the parlor with the radio, the sound of it coming through the wall, words she had stopped listening to. He had been sitting that way most evenings — not attending to the radio, she thought, only needing it there the way you need a lamp in a room you're not using.

She washed the supper dishes and stacked them in the rack and wiped down the table and then sat at it. She turned the lamp down and then up one notch, to the right level for close work.

She took the note out of her coat pocket and set it on the table.

Karl's writing face up: the four paragraphs, the pencil lines faintly silvered in the lamplight, the Allgäu spelling of certain words she had stopped noticing long ago — *daheim* for home, the particular way he wrote his r's so they came down from the top. She had read it enough times that she could have said the words without seeing them. She looked at it for a moment and then turned it over.

The other side was blank.

She got up and went to the parlor drawer where Marta kept the spare pencils and brought one back. She sat. The blank side of the note was small-ruled — the lines faintly blue-gray in the lamplight, the kind of paper from a workbook, the kind of paper that turned up in a POW camp's limited supply and was used for whatever needed to be written. She looked at it for a moment.

Then she began to write.

Her own hand: lighter-pressed than Karl's, the way her teacher had shown her for keeping the point fine on a soft pencil. Small letters, even — she wanted everything legible, wanted no excuse for misreading later. German, because it was the right language for what she was doing. The language her father had said was the language of exactness: you said what you meant, you meant what you said, you didn't put in what wasn't there.

*Wilder's Landing, Küstenstraße.* The place first, the way you began a proper account.

*Oktober 1943 — Donnerstag oder Freitag, nachts, gegen zehn oder elf.* October 1943, Thursday or Friday, nighttime, around ten or eleven.

She wrote what Karl had told her. She wrote it in order, the facts as they had come: *Einen Mann zu Fuß auf der linken Straßenseite.* A man on foot on the left side of the road. *Älter. Schmächtig. Einen langen Mantel, nicht für das Wetter — einen Stadtmantel.* Older. Slight. A long coat, not for the weather — a city coat. *Trug etwas, ein Bündel oder eine Tasche, nah am Körper gehalten.* Carrying something, a bundle or a bag, held close to the body.

She paused. She wrote: *Bewegte sich Richtung Wasser.*

She wrote it and it was as exact as she could make it, which was all she had to offer it.

*Sah sich um — schaute den Truck direkt an, durch den Regen.*

Turned to look — looked directly at the truck, through the rain.

*Lief weiter.*

Kept walking.

That was Karl's account. She had written it down in the order he had given it, without adding to it or taking anything from it. She set the pencil down.

She looked at what she had written.

Then she picked up the note in both hands.

His writing on one side, hers on the other. His in November; hers tonight, in April, at the kitchen table with the supper things put away and the lamplight low. His four paragraphs about himself — Marktoberdorf, the conscription, the cattle that reminded him of home, the man on the road he still remembered — and on the other side her lines about the man on the road, where he had been, what he had been doing, what Karl had seen in the ten seconds before the truck moved on.

Two sides. Two hands. Two moments.

She held the note and felt the weight of it — the small-ruled paper, barely larger than what you could cup between two palms, both sides filled now. In the parlor the radio talked in its even voice about something in the Pacific. Werner's chair was still.

She had thought she knew what she was holding. In November under the lamp, in December at the bag lining: she had thought she understood the note, what it was, what it meant for her to have it. She had been wrong. What she had understood then was a note from a boy who had seen something on a road in the rain and written it down because it had been sitting in him without a place to go.

What she held now was the last known account of a man who had been alive and walking under his own power at Wilder's Landing on a night in early October 1943, heading toward the harbor, and had been found on the breakwater in the morning. Karl had been in the truck. She had written it down. Both their hands were in this paper, in this kitchen, in this house, and as far as she could determine, no one else had been on that road at that hour who had seen what Karl had seen.

She folded the note.

She put it in her coat pocket. She would put it back in the bag tomorrow, sew the seam back. Tonight it needed to be where she could feel it.

In the parlor Werner shifted once in his chair and was still again.

Hannah set both hands flat on the table and looked at the lamplight on the wood and did not move them.
