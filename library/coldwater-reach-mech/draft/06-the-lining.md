# Chapter 6 — The Lining

The ice had come in the night. Thin, pale at the edges of the drainage channels where the water moved slowest, and the fields beyond had a grey shine to them that meant the ground had given up whatever warmth it had been holding. Hannah carried the first bucket from the pump house to the barn and her breath came out white and the mud at the barn door had gone hard as fired clay underfoot.

In the yard Werner was already out, his work coat buttoned to the collar, one hand on the wire at the far fence post. He hadn't started on the fence.

The truck was late.

It came up the road at nearly eight-thirty — she could hear the engine a quarter-mile off, coming through the tree line before the headlights showed. Werner turned when he heard it but didn't come in from the fence. Hannah set the bucket just inside the barn door and waited.

The truck stopped in the yard and the men got down — Karl first, then Brauer, then the two others. Oakes climbed down last from the cab, rifle strap gone slack on one side, and said something to the driver; the truck backed up and turned and went back out the way it came. Karl had already lifted a coil of fencing wire from the truck bed and was carrying it toward the lower field. He moved the same way he always moved, deliberate and even, his breath clouding against the cold air.

Werner watched him the whole way.

She was looking at her father's back, at the set of his shoulders, the hand still resting on the wire. His face was turned toward Karl. He was not examining the fence post; she had understood this from the moment she came out into the yard, before she was ready to understand it. What she read in the set of him was not anger. Not the careful blankness of a man performing unconcern. Something that cost more than either of those things — a quality of recognition and what the recognition required of him, visible in his back before she could name it in his face, which she could not see.

She went inside with the bucket.

---

The morning moved. Hannah worked the barn interior — the feed, the stall cleaning, the second watering, a gutter to clear that she had been meaning to get to for a week. Werner came through twice: once to find a post maul, once to say something about the south fence needing another post before the ground froze solid. He did not explain the fence post by the road. She did not ask.

She could hear Brauer and Karl on the fence line, intermittent — the sound of the wire stretcher, the knock of a post hammer in cold air. Oakes moved through the yard once, his slow careful steps, and went to the wagon shed and didn't come back. Hannah kept to the barn interior. The cattle were restless by mid-morning, the wind coming from the northwest, and she worked through their agitation without hurrying. Outside, the frost had not melted. The drainage channels held their skim of ice all morning.

She thought, *drei Wochen*, and caught herself. Three weeks she had had the note — had transferred it from dress pocket to coat pocket every morning and back every evening, a practice she had settled into so gradually that she hadn't noticed it becoming a practice until now, counting in German.

She put the thought down and cleared the gutter.

Brauer came to the barn door late in the morning with a question about the wire stretcher's left jaw. She answered him in English without looking up from what she was doing, and he thanked her and went back. She didn't know whether Karl was close enough to hear. The wind was up.

---

The truck came back at three.

She heard it at the gate and went to stand in the barn door. The men lined up without ceremony — the practiced sorting of people who have done this many times — and Oakes walked them over. Karl set something in the truck bed before he climbed up, and she couldn't see what, a coil of wire maybe, and he moved without pausing. The truck turned in the yard.

He did not look toward the farmhouse window as it went.

The not-looking. It was not the same as looking away.

She stayed in the barn door until the truck sound had gone back through the tree line and there was nothing left of the afternoon but low December light coming flat over the east fields, and then she went in to finish the work.

---

In the farmhouse it was warm and already dark outside by five. Marta had the kitchen lamp on and the table half-set — the American flag placemats laid at each place, the ones with the small gold eagle in the corner that she had ordered from Atwood's last spring after reading something in the newspaper. Marta set them out every evening now with a care Hannah recognized as effort, a thing requiring daily renewal. Hannah did not say anything about the placemats. She took off her coat and hung it on the hook behind the door.

She put her hand into the right pocket.

The note was there, folded in its four folds, the paper cool against her fingers. She had moved it from the dress pocket this morning the same as every morning — three weeks, the same motion, until the motion no longer felt like a decision. She held it a moment through the wool.

Werner was at the radio, his chair pulled close. The evening news was on — she could hear it from the doorway, a man's voice steady and distant — but Werner was not following it. He had the posture of listening without the attention. His eyes were on the floor somewhere between himself and the stove, and his hands were on his knees, opened and not quite resting. She had seen him like this more often lately, sitting alongside the radio without attending to it, the way you sit in a room where you know what you used to do there and have forgotten what that was.

She drew her hand out of the coat pocket.

She looked at the coat on the hook.

If he were to go through the pockets looking for something — his lighter, a key, anything he thought she might have borrowed — he would find it before he found anything else.

She went upstairs.

---

Her room was cold. The grey last light from the window was enough. She sat on the edge of the bed and unfolded the note in her lap.

She knew it by heart, the way you know a thing you have gone back to too many times. Still she read it. She moved through the conscription sentences and the line about the cattle — *das ist keine Klage* — and came to the last two.

*Den Mann auf dem Weg habe ich noch in Erinnerung. Er schien einen langen Weg vor sich zu haben.*

And underneath it, remembered from the afternoon in the barn, what Karl had said in the Allgäu dialect before Oakes came back around: that the man was older, quick on his feet, carrying something, heading in the direction of the water. She had taken it for the kind of detail that sticks because it was strange — a man on a road at night in October rain, not expected.

She thought about what she had heard at Atwood's in October, Jess Atwood speaking to her mother, not particularly to Hannah: *they think he came in from inland, is what Tibbetts said. Because of the shoes.* Smooth-soled, Hannah had gathered — not waterproof, not sea-work shoes. A man who had walked somewhere to get to the harbor.

She had heard at school, in November, that there were no papers, or the papers had gotten damaged — she had been half-listening at the time. She was listening to it now, from inside the memory.

Karl's transport from Bangor came down the coastal road past Wilder's Landing and then south into town. The man he had seen — older, fast for his age, carrying something, heading toward the water — would have been on that same stretch of road.

She did not know what this meant. She could feel it pointing toward something without being able to see what was at the end of the pointing. A man on a road in the rain. A man washed up on the breakwater. A night in October she had not been part of, between those two.

She folded the note back along its lines.

She thought about her father's hands on the fence wire this morning, his back turned toward Karl, watching him carry the wire to the lower field.

She thought: if he went through her coat. If he went through her room.

She got up.

---

The old school bag was in the back of the closet, behind the winter coat and the rubber boots she had outgrown two years ago. Canvas, tan, the strap worn to softness at the buckle. She had carried her textbooks in it when she still went to school with any regularity — before the spring and then the summer and then the fall had pulled her away from it, the farm needing more than school could wait for. She took it out and set it on the bed and looked at the inside bottom.

The seam there was a doubled fold of canvas, stitched with heavy factory thread in a burnt orange that had never matched the bag's color. She pressed her finger along it. Three-quarters of an inch of fold, perhaps more. Enough.

She went to the hall closet and took her mother's sewing box down from the shelf. She did not ask. She chose a darning needle — the eye wide, which was what she needed — and a spool of thread. The thread was black. Wrong, but it didn't matter; no one was going to look at the color of a seam.

Back in her room she sat on the bed with the bag across her knees. She threaded the needle in the dim window light, which took longer than it would have with the lamp. She ran her thumbnail along the seam's edge until she found a stretch where the original stitching had loosened slightly from use — not failing, just softened — and pushed the needle through.

The needle was blunt. She had to lean into it.

She worked three inches of the seam open, keeping the original stitches intact so she could press them back. She took the note and unfolded it flat and smoothed it against her knee, pressing the creases down until it lay as straight as it was going to. She worked it in along the inner canvas, easing it between the two folds, pressing it down with her thumb until it lay flat against the bottom. She could barely feel it through the canvas. The thickness of a note, four times folded and then unfolded, was not very much.

She began stitching the seam closed.

Not even — not machine-tight, nothing that would pass close examination for what it was. But enough. She pulled the thread taut each time without puckering the fold. Six stitches and then a seventh, and then she knotted it twice and cut the thread by pulling it sharp against her thumbnail.

She turned the bag over and ran her fingers across the inside bottom — the canvas, the worn inner lining, the slight change in density where the note lay flat.

Nothing visible. She checked with the back of her hand. Nothing. If her father put his hand into the bag he would feel old canvas and that was all.

She sat for a moment with the bag in her lap.

---

Downstairs she hung the school bag on the hook by the staircase, below her coat. Werner's hat was already on the hook, and her mother's good scarf. The bag hung the way it had used to, before everything else began to take the time school had used — as if it had not been away.

She went to the kitchen.

Werner was at the table now, not the radio. He must have turned it off, or it had gone between programs. He sat with his hands flat on the wood in front of him, no glass or plate between them, looking at his hands the way he had looked at Karl carrying the fence wire across the yard: that same quality of attention that was not presence, not the posture of a man who has arrived where he is sitting.

Marta was at the stove with her back to them both.

*Was denkst du, Vater?*

The thought came in German and stayed there. She said nothing.

She took the water glasses from the shelf and set them on the table — her father's, her mother's, her own. Werner did not look up. His hands were on the table, and the school bag was on the hook in the hall, and outside the kitchen window the December dark had come all the way down over the fields.

She sat in her chair and waited for Marta to bring the pot to the table.
