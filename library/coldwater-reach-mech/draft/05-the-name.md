# Chapter 5 — The Name

The house had been empty since before eight.

Henry had taken his coat from the hook and gone to the school. A window in the boiler room had lost its latch, he said — the superintendent had been after him about it since October. Lillian had her Ladies' Auxiliary meeting, or possibly the post office; she had been out the door before Marcus finished his coffee. He came upstairs and sat at the desk and looked at the stack of student exercises to his right.

He had not touched them.

The light through the east window was thin and early. The birch stand on the far corner of the school's lot threw long shadows across the frozen ground, and he watched the shadows shorten for something close to twenty minutes without arriving at a decision. The furnace cycled below. The radiator made its knock. No human sound from below, or adjacent. This was the silence of an unoccupied house — different from the silence of his room when Henry and Lillian were below; different again from the silence of the school on a cold morning. This was the particular silence of a house where no one was waiting for anything of him, which was the silence in which a person ran out of reasons to hold off.

He opened the bottom drawer.

---

The magnifying glass had been in his left coat pocket since Vienna. He took it out as he lifted the manila folder away from the fragment — the glass small, German-made, the brass casing worn smooth along the top and left side where his thumb rested. A scholar's instrument: bought secondhand at the University quarter, carried through every subsequent address. He set it beside the lamp.

He turned the fragment face-up.

He had looked at it before. Three evenings since Graves left, perhaps four. He understood the difference between looking and working. He had been looking: the lamp at the wrong angle, the glass out but not properly held, some reason each time to put it back. He adjusted the lamp now so the light fell across the surface at an oblique angle rather than directly down. At direct angle the dissolved ink disappeared into the fiber. Oblique brought the surface into relief — the impressed marks where the nib had pressed fiber down, the shadow of what was there even where the ink had gone.

He held the glass over the heading block.

*Lutz, J.*

The letters were block-formed — a more deliberate hand than the body text, the kind of lettering a person used when they needed a name to remain legible above everything else. *L-u-t-z,* comma, space, *J,* period. He had known this the evening Graves set the envelope on his desk. The glass confirmed it; the letterforms were complete, each one unambiguous.

He moved to the body text.

*Kalt.* Cold. One word, context dissolved on all sides. *Ankommen* — to arrive; the prefix compound was clear, and the fiber below the *k* showed the density of a double consonant. He had given Graves these two words. It was as far as he had permitted himself to go.

The oblique angle changed what was available.

A date block appeared in the upper right quadrant — he had seen its shape before without recognizing it for what it was. *Okto—*, the rest consumed in the damage to the page's lower quarter. October. The year's last digit was gone, and the three before it; the range was 1940 to 1945 and he could not narrow it further.

To the left of the date: a name fragment. The first two letters clear, the rest dissolved to fiber. *Qu—.* Upper case, a proper noun. He brought the glass closer. The letterforms were not ambiguous: *Q* and *u*, and then the fiber had failed. He did not let himself treat the obvious reading as the only reading, but he did not exclude it. *Qu.* Quebec, if the obvious reading held.

Near the bottom left corner, the fragment he had seen before but not trusted — the ink there damaged differently, smeared rather than dissolved, dragged across the fiber before it had dried:

*...nicht mehr warten...*

*Warten:* to wait. *Nicht mehr:* no longer. The surrounding sentence was gone; what remained was the idiom and the state it named. *Can wait no longer.* Or: *no longer able to wait.* Both readings came out in the same place.

He set the glass down.

The case Marcus had declined to construct for ten days could be constructed as follows. A German-language note recovered from the body of an unidentified man found on a Maine breakwater in October of some year. A name, *Lutz, J.*, written in block letters above text mentioning cold and arrival. A proper noun whose first two letters suggested Quebec. A phrase about the end of waiting. The man Josef Lutz, whom Marcus had known at the Sherbrooke internment camp in Quebec, was Viennese, Jewish, trained as an archivist, and had been released from the camp sometime in late 1942 or early 1943, under papers too limited to be safe, with a destination in New York that was accessible from the Maine coast by routes a man without proper papers might reasonably take.

This was the argument from available elements. It was not a proof. Marcus was not in the business of proofs.

---

Josef Lutz had been in the bunk two rows from Marcus's in the B-block at Sherbrooke. Between them in that direction: forty men. In the other direction: twelve. Marcus knew these numbers because in the early months he had counted — the bodies between himself and each door, each exit, each possible approach. Josef Lutz had done the same thing. Marcus recognized it without acknowledging the recognition, because acknowledging it would have required naming what both of them were doing, and there were things you did not name in the camp.

Josef watched doors. When a door opened his attention moved to it first — before the face of whoever was behind it, before the content of whatever was being announced. Which way the door swung. Whether it opened from inside or outside. How quickly and how far. It was not a nervous habit. It was an attentive one. The distinction was not small.

They spoke carefully, as everyone spoke carefully, in the language that sorted you into categories. Josef's German was Vienna German — the university and the archive, the phrasing Marcus recognized from before. He had been training as an archivist. He knew the building on Josefsgasse, the card catalog organized by provenance rather than subject, the particular smell of the basement stacks where the leather bindings had softened in the damp. He described these things with the close, specific attention of a man who could see them clearly because they were not in front of him anymore.

He spoke four languages fluently and a fifth poorly; he mentioned the fifth without identifying it, and Marcus did not ask, because you did not ask questions that invited reciprocal questions about things you also preferred not to name.

When Marcus left — the Quaker placement had come through, the late-summer transport was waiting — he shook Josef's hand at the gate. He remembered the hand. The grip brief and calibrated, exactly the kind of handshake that communicated nothing, which was therefore communicating everything it had available. Josef said something. The gate was making noise; the transport truck was running. Marcus did not hear it clearly. He nodded. He got into the truck.

He had told himself, because a person has to tell themselves something, that Josef had reached New York. That the cousin in the garment trade had been found. That the arrangement had worked, in the way arrangements sometimes did. He had told himself this for fourteen months, with the consistency of a man who understood that the alternative was not something he had room for in the accounting he was running.

He looked at the fragment.

*Nicht mehr warten.* October. *Qu—*. A long way from New York. Not the route Marcus would have chosen; but Marcus had not been the one doing the choosing.

---

He took a sheet of paper from the center drawer. He wrote the address from memory — Simon Ehrlich, at the house in Côte-des-Neiges; he kept the address in his head, where it was available when needed and not available to anyone else. Ehrlich had been at Sherbrooke for eight months, released through a sorting that Ehrlich had the sense not to contest. He had moved to Montreal and set up a clearinghouse for refugee inquiries, operating under cover of a book distribution concern. Marcus had not written to him in fourteen months.

He wrote three paragraphs. He wrote them as a man writes when the only reader he is imagining is the one he intends: directly, the names named, the question stated. He wrote Josef's name twice, the surname once. He wrote the word *Maine* in the third paragraph and stopped and read back what he had.

He read it the way a censor would — not for meaning but for configuration, for the shape that the elements made together. A German-speaking man, formerly in a Quebec internment camp, at a Maine coast address, writing to a refugee clearinghouse in Montreal, asking about a missing German Jew. The censor would not have his particular context. The censor would not need it.

He carried the sheet downstairs to the kitchen.

The stove still had coals from the morning. He opened the front gate and put the paper in. The edges caught first, curling before they took; then the face went brown and then black, the words going last at the center where the fiber was thickest. He watched it until it was finished. He was not disturbed by this. He had burned letters before, in other cities, for other reasons, and the practice was the same each time: you waited until it was done, and then you closed the gate. He closed the gate and went back upstairs.

---

The second letter required a different architecture.

He began with the school, because the school was the ground on which he was permitted to stand without explanation. His advanced German students had exhausted the school's existing materials; he was writing to ask whether Ehrlich's distribution network might have access to German-language graded readers or grammatical workbooks suitable for secondary classroom use; were there channels through which such texts might become available? This was a genuine problem — he had been meaning to address it since September. He added a sentence about the school's coal situation and the dependability of the furnace. This occupied another line.

In the second paragraph: he found himself thinking often of people from the Sherbrooke period; he hoped Ehrlich and his family were well; he wondered, as an aside, whether Ehrlich had heard anything of Josef Lutz — he believed Josef had intended to go to New York after his release, to a cousin there, but he had lost touch entirely and would be glad of any news, if Ehrlich happened to have it.

He read this version twice. Then a third time, from the beginning, more slowly.

The academic paragraph was true and therefore stable. The personal inquiry was dressed as the kind of inquiry old acquaintances made when they had lost track of mutual friends. There was nothing in the letter that named the harbor. Nothing that named the month or any circumstance that connected his Maine address to anything besides teaching and cold weather.

It was possible. Not safe; he did not believe in safe. But possible.

He folded the letter once and put it in the bottom drawer, next to the fragment. He did not address an envelope. He did not take his coat from the hook or count his ration of coins for the post-office window.

He sat at the desk for a moment. Then he pulled the student exercises in front of him and uncapped the red pen.

---

Henry came back in the early afternoon smelling of coal dust, the boiler-room window latched now, and mentioned that the superintendent's approach to window problems in that building was to nail the affected window shut and open a different one — workable, Henry allowed, if you didn't need the original window, which apparently nobody had in three years. Lillian came in from her errand with sugar and a view on the current rationing situation at Atwood's that she held until dinner. Marcus drank his tea and helped carry the packages from the entry.

At dinner they had bean soup from the pot Lillian had started before she left, and bread, and butter. Henry had a letter from his nephew — the young man was in the Pacific, in good health, and had written a passage about the Navy's approach to food that Henry read aloud once and then read again because it was worth hearing twice. They were quiet a moment after the second reading. Marcus ate his bread.

He washed the cups after and dried them and put them on the shelf and came upstairs.

He sat at the desk and brought the lamp to its reading angle and opened the first exercise. He had started it before supper; the case error was marked, the note written in the margin. He turned to the second. The student was working through the third conjugation with more confidence than accuracy; Marcus marked four instances and wrote two corrections, brief and specific, and moved to the third exercise. He worked through to the end of it. His marks were small and took up little room on the page.

He put the cap on the pen.

Outside the window, the November dark had settled over the school and the birch stand and the frozen road. The furnace knocked once below and was still. The drawer was in the desk, and the desk was in the lamplight, and the house was quiet around him.
