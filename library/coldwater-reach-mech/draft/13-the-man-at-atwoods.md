# Chapter 13 — The Man at Atwood's

She had been at the shack since six. By eight the morning log was in order. Two trap boats in overnight: HELEN J., owner Noss, in at 0312, no incident; REENIE D., owner Farrar, in at 0447, half a hold of lobster and a report of fouled ground gear on the eastern ledge. She had filled the lamp and replaced the wick. She had entered the channel marker night reports and noted Farrar's buoy observation in the maintenance log and flagged it for Rockland.

By ten the harbor was easy. Peak of the season — all the surviving trap boats on their own schedules, the channel clear, a light southwest air moving off the water and doing nothing about the heat that had been building over the breakwater since nine. She took the ration book and the kerosene order form from the top drawer. The form was already filled out except for Atwood's countersignature: delivery address, account number, Tuesday slot circled at the bottom. On her list: the form countersigned, a spool of rope-repair twine, lamp wicks in the No. 2 brass size if they had them in. She folded the ration book and the form into her jacket pocket, closed the log, and went up the main street.

---

August Saturday traffic meant women with market baskets and a few men coming back from the wharf without much to carry. Gasoline had been tight since spring; people walked to Atwood's now, which was not very different from how it had always been except that now they walked knowing it. Two women she recognized stood outside the post office going through what one had just collected. A pair of boys crossed the street toward Murphett's with the loose-limbed ease of boys not where they ought to be. Summer people from the point cottages came toward her with baskets that looked heavier than last week's — the fish market had been good, or Atwood's had had a Friday delivery. She stepped around them and went in.

The inside was cooler than the street. The door had been propped open with a wooden crate of tinned goods and the air moved through. Four people ahead of her in the queue, one man at the counter already in a transaction with Atwood's son. She took her place at the back.

She checked the kerosene form again. It was in order. She put it in her pocket. The line moved one place. She waited.

---

The man at the counter finished and the next person moved up. She was third. She glanced at the list. When she looked up again, the man at the counter had shifted slightly to face Atwood's son, and she could see the back of him more directly than before.

She could not have said, after, what drew her attention to him. He was simply there — his back toward her, the transaction still going, his ration book open on the counter. She looked at him the way you looked at a person in a queue: briefly, without settling. Then something in the set of him asked for a second look.

She gave it.

The shoulders. Broad, carrying the upper body of a man who had moved weight most of his life — bales, milk cans, fence posts, the ordinary tonnage a farm accumulated and required. The right shoulder was lower than the left. Not by much — perhaps an inch, perhaps a little more — but consistently lower, in the way of a body that had a long habit of loading one side. And the feet: planted in work boots that had shaped themselves over years to a particular gait, the toes angled slightly outward, two or three degrees past straight, just enough to be a fact about the body and not a stance chosen for this moment. His weight was on the left leg. The left hip carried it, the right hip slightly higher.

She looked at this.

She was not yet thinking. She was doing the thing she did before thinking: reading the load and the trim and the particular way a thing sat under its own weight. She had been doing this for thirty years, first alongside Owen and then alone — identifying vessels by their waterline and their running light pattern before the binoculars reached the name board, reading which boats were coming in heavy and which light by the way they rode the channel. She had named boats in fog and in November dark by the pattern of how they moved. She was doing that now.

She had read this posture before.

October. The second. The NE gale still above thirty knots when she checked the near mooring cleat before bed — she could still hear the note the roof made at thirty, the particular pitch of the wind above the eaves of the shack, lower than at thirty-five, lower than at forty, a tone she had learned to calibrate without looking at the instrument. The channel impaired; she'd noted it in the log before she turned the lamp down. She had opened the shack door with the lantern in her hand to check the near cleat, and she had looked out at the east dock.

Two people on the dock. Ten or twelve feet between them.

The first she had seen in more detail because the first was the one that wanted looking at: long dark coat, not an oilskin, the kind of shoes she could not hear but could imagine on the wet planks of the dock. Standing with the fixed stillness of someone who had walked a long way and arrived at a destination. She had looked at that figure and understood she was looking at something she would need to record, and then she had looked at the second figure.

The second was standing the way a person stood when they were there on purpose and knew where they were. The height, the build. The way the weight was held. She had taken him for local — not from any specific fact but from posture, from the ease of a person on familiar ground. She had not been able to name him at that distance in the wind and the lantern's limited reach. She had filed it: local, or nearly. Someone she had perhaps seen before, without being able to say where. And she had gone back inside and closed the shack door, and the next morning Josef was against the seaward face.

Ten months ago.

The man at the counter said something to Atwood's son, a brief exchange she couldn't make out over the general noise of the store, and she heard the son say something back. The man pocketed his ration book. He picked up the paper sack from the counter. He said one more thing — the tone of a pleasantry or a closing remark, a Saturday-morning sort of exchange — and Atwood's son nodded, and the man picked up the sack with his left hand and turned.

She had been ready for his face before he turned. The way you were ready for the name board before the binoculars found it, because the hull had already told you what to expect.

Werner Pfeiffer.

Dairy farmer, three miles up the gravel road inland from the Wilder's Landing junction. She knew him the way she knew most of the four hundred people left in Coldwater Reach who were not her direct business: by occupation, by location, by the face in the queue. He was somewhere in his middle fifties, broad through the jaw, a face that showed work and weather and offered nothing beyond those two facts. He looked across the store toward the exit and his eyes moved over the line with the automatic categorization of a man orienting himself toward the door.

His gaze found her. There was a pause — one beat — while he placed her. The harbormaster. The woman at the shack. His expression arrived at her and settled into the nod that was the standard currency between two people who knew each other at the community level and not otherwise.

He nodded once.

She nodded.

He came past her toward the open door with his paper sack. She heard his boots on the step outside. She heard them on the sidewalk boards heading south along the main street. She did not hear them stop.

---

Atwood's son called her up. He was twenty-three or twenty-four, lean, and had been running the counter since March when his father's hip gave out, managing it with the quiet capability of someone who had taken over without requiring the situation to be announced. She gave him the kerosene order form and the ration book. He countersigned the form, confirmed Tuesday delivery, noted the account number.

She asked for rope-repair twine, No. 2 size, a full spool. He had it. She asked about lamp wicks, No. 2 brass fitting. He checked the back shelf. He had two of the right size; she took both.

She paid. She thanked him. She went back out to the street.

---

The main street going back toward the harbor was the same main street going the other way. She didn't hurry.

Werner Pfeiffer. Dairy farm. The gravel road north from Wilder's Landing, the unpainted mailbox at the junction she had passed twice and not turned up. She had never had occasion to go to the farm and she had no reason to go now.

She walked past the church. The war notice board on the post outside it had names she didn't want to read on a Saturday, so she didn't. She walked on.

Werner Pfeiffer's family had come from Bavaria. Sometime in the twenties — she didn't know the year. She had known they were there the way she knew most things about this town: by accumulation, by the slow deposit of other people's conversations over years. The word had gone around early in the war, the winter after Pearl Harbor: German ancestry, German-speaking household, the draft board was aware. She had heard it said about the Pfeiffers in the same week she heard it about two other families and she had not found a reason to single any of them out. She had set it aside.

Werner Pfeiffer had the POW crew this spring and summer. She had filed the labor requisition paperwork in January — Form DD-4, four prisoners from an inland camp, one Coastal Patrol guard, authorized weekday transport schedule. She had seen the Army truck on the coast road in the early mornings twice since May, and both times she had noted it against the authorized schedule and found it matched, and put the thought down.

The chandlery was on her right. She stopped in its awning shade, not for any purpose except that the shade was there and the knowledge needed a moment to settle into its shape.

---

What she knew: Werner Pfeiffer had been on the east dock at approximately twenty-three ten on the night of October 2, 1943. The man buried in the town cemetery under the stone that said *Unknown Man, October 1943* had been on the east dock at the same time, standing ten or twelve feet from Werner Pfeiffer in a coat that was not right for the weather. By 0615 the following morning he was against the seaward face of the breakwater, two hundred and ten feet from the pier head.

What she did not know: why Werner Pfeiffer had been on the east dock that night. Whether he had known the man in the long coat before October 2, or whether that night was their first meeting. What had passed between the two figures on the dock in the hour or so after she went back inside and closed the door. Whether Werner had been there waiting, or had arrived after the other man, or had come looking for him. Whether something had happened on the dock before Josef reached the breakwater, or whether what happened was that a man in smooth-soled shoes went off a wet edge in a forty-knot gale and Werner Pfeiffer had not been close enough to stop it.

She did not know whether the correct word was *witness*.

She had a working certainty about what she had seen. She had built it the same way she built any working certainty: from posture and proportion and the particular evidence of a body under its own weight, the same methodology she used to identify a vessel in Sow Channel on a foggy November night. The back of a man at thirty feet in lantern light and gale darkness, and the back of a man at twenty feet in Atwood's general store on a clear August morning, and the two backs being the same back — same shoulder, same foot angle, same left-heavy carry. She was not guessing. But she was not certain in any way a report could hold.

These were two different things. She had never confused them, in the harbor or anywhere else.

---

She thought of Aldous Tibbetts.

His office was over the hardware store. Less than two hundred feet from where she was standing.

She thought it through the way she thought through a channel-marker checklist: what was required, what was available, what the procedure called for at each step. She could go to Aldous and say: I believe I have identified a person who was present on the dock on the night before the body came in. Aldous would ask: how do you know? She would say: I recognized his posture and his build from behind, in a store, ten months later. Aldous would ask: did you see his face on the dock that night? No. Did you make a note of this observation at the time? No. Is there any evidence this person was involved in the man's death? None.

She had no evidence. She had a working certainty that Werner Pfeiffer had been on the east dock on the night of October 2, 1943. That was not evidence of anything except that Werner Pfeiffer had been on the east dock on the night of October 2, 1943, which was not in itself a reportable fact. Men used the dock. The dock was not closed that night, only the channel. She had not even been certain enough at the time to write it.

She had two official documents with her name on them. The harbor log for October 2 at 2300, which said: *Weather: NE gale, 35 knots decreasing at first light. Channel: temporarily impaired.* And the Rockland follow-up inquiry, Case No. CLD-43-081, mailed April 13, in which she had written *None* in the section for new information, new witnesses, and new evidence, and signed her name below it.

She walked down to the shack.

---

The afternoon was the afternoon's work. The fouled buoy report went to Rockland by radio at 1335 — position, nature of obstruction, estimated navigation priority. The kerosene delivery entered in the requisition file for Tuesday. The HELEN J. came back to the float at 1545 for additional trap gear, Noss lashing three more pots to the stern, the float bumping the near piling once as he pulled off. She logged it. The tide gauge at the wharf pier recorded the afternoon sequence: high water in at 1109 at 10.4 feet above chart datum, the flood already past its peak and dropping, the predicted low of 0.4 feet expected around half past five. The REENIE D. made its evening departure on schedule — Farrar standing off the mooring at 1752, clearing the channel head and heading south for the outer grounds. She logged it.

She did not think about Werner Pfeiffer while she did this. She thought about the fouled buoy, and about whether Farrar was running on enough fuel given the outer grounds were another twelve miles, and about the Tuesday kerosene delivery and whether she needed to be at the shack to sign for it or whether she could leave the requisition copy on the desk. She thought about the morning's lamp wick and whether she had put it in the correct drawer. She had.

At half past five she cleared the maintenance files from the desk and opened the log to the August 12 page.

She took the pen and wrote the evening entry:

*August 12, 1944. Weather: Wind WSW, 10–12 kts. Sky: clear. Visibility: unlimited. Barometric pressure: 29.92, steady. High water 1109 hrs, +10.4 ft chart datum. Low water 1726 hrs, +0.4 ft chart datum.*

*Vessels: HELEN J. (Noss, trap), arrived 0312, departed 1617. REENIE D. (Farrar, trap), arrived 0447, departed 1752.*

*1335: Fouled buoy, eastern ledge, pos. approx. 46°22'N. Reported to Rockland District via radio. Maintenance flag entered.*

*1545: HELEN J. at float, trap gear collection.*

She wrote it in order: tide heights, wind, visibility, barometric. Vessels: two arrivals, both logged; two departures, both logged. The buoy: position and report and action taken. Column by column, the way she wrote every entry — complete, nothing out of sequence, the handwriting even throughout.

She read it back.

Tide heights: correct. Wind: WSW, confirmed at noon and confirmed at four. Vessels: HELEN J. and REENIE D., arrivals and departures, times and owners. The fouled buoy: position, radio report, maintenance flag. Every fact in its column.

She closed the log.
