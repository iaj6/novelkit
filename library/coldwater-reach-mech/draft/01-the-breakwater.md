# Chapter 1 — The Breakwater

The gale had blown three days. It had died before she woke — not the way a storm moves through, running its course and departing, but the way a fire goes out when it has used what it had. She lay in the dark and listened to the absence of it for a moment. Then she got up.

Owen's coat hung on the back of the cottage door. She had moved it twice since 1935: once when the door frame behind it went to mildew and she needed to repaint the wood, and once when she had put a cut on her own hand and gotten blood on the left cuff that she needed to get out before she forgot she'd done it. Both times she put it back. She took it down now and shrugged it on.

It was large on her, wide at the shoulder, the cuffs falling past her wrists when she let them. She had learned to work the buttons from the bottom up — the only way the front sat without pulling.

Outside, the air was still and very cold. The sky over the bluff had gone pale grey at its lower edge and dark above, the color coming up from the east in slow degrees. She went down the track from the cottage to the pier. The harbor lay flat. The smell was kelp and diesel and the particular brackish smell of water that has worked through wreckage for several days and settled into something new. The sound was what was not there: no wind in the stays of the moored boats, no swell against the breakwater's seaward face, only the low rhythmic lap of a harbor going still after long violence.

In the mooring field, she counted: four boats still on their moorings, two gone. The *Agnes Louise* had come off hers and gone aground on the shingle at the channel's edge; her mast showed at a slight angle in the grey light. The channel marker off the south shoulder: red, steady, cycling normally. The inner pair: both present and lit. She noted these and walked on to the shack.

The shack was as she had left it Monday evening — the lamp low on the desk, the log closed on its stand, the tide chart and the channel-closed notice both tacked above it. She had issued that notice Monday morning at 1030 and had not walked the full breakwater since. This was the first morning she could. She left the shack door and went out onto the granite.

---

The breakwater ran three hundred feet into the channel, granite-cut and solid, the joints between courses packed after any serious blow with kelp and mussel shell and whatever the gale had driven in. She walked it the way she always walked it: feet to the centerline, eyes moving from the stone in front of her to the harbor side and then to the seaward face. The harbor side held the expected — pot warp tangled around the second iron cleat, a trap-lath wedged in a joint twenty feet along, a mooring buoy canted against the toe of the wall. Small losses, none of them new. On the seaward face — the face that took the weather, where wreckage collected after a northerly blow — she would go more carefully.

The light was coming in. Above the eastern bluff the sky had gone pale rose and the channel ran dark grey, the long swell moving through in slow even sets, each one a little lower than the last. No wind. The cold that follows a blow is its own kind: not driven, not moving, simply present and settled into things, as if it had been there all along and the gale was only what had to happen first.

She walked out. At a hundred feet she stopped to check the south shoulder marker. Its light was cycling. She went on.

At roughly two hundred feet she stopped.

Something was against the seaward face below the level of the walkway, caught in the angle where the last granite course met the waterline. The swell lifted it and set it back.

She went carefully to the edge and crouched.

A man.

Male, perhaps fifty, perhaps older. He lay on his side against the granite, partly in the water; the swell moved through him at its own rhythm. His coat was heavy dark wool — a good coat, a city coat, not an oilskin. Below it, wool trousers, the crease long since gone. His shoes were leather. She could see the sole of his right foot where it had come up against a seam in the stone: smooth leather, waterlogged, no grip at all. A man in leather-soled shoes on this granite in this weather. She marked the fact and moved on.

She checked his outer coat pockets, both sides. Empty. She worked the lapel back for the inside breast pocket. Empty. The trouser pocket she could reach: empty. His hands were curled at an angle against the stone, the knuckles bloodless. She did not move them. His face was turned away, giving her the jaw and the cheekbone and the pale flat of his temple. Not a face she knew. She looked at what she could see: the broad plane of the cheek, the particular set of the jaw. Somewhere else, she thought. Not this coast. She could not say more than that with any honesty, and she did not. The sea had had him several days, perhaps more.

She stood. The swell moved through the seaward face and lifted him and set him back. She looked at him for one more moment.

Then she walked back to the shack.

---

Same pace as before. The granite was wet and the joints were packed with weed. She did not run. There was nothing to run toward that would not wait.

In the shack she hung Owen's coat on its hook. She sat at the desk and opened the log.

---

The October 2 column: the 0545 tide check, the morning conditions, the gale advisory, the channel closure at 1030, the midday update, the 1630 report. *Wind NE 35 knots, sustained. Channel conditions deteriorating. All vessels accounted for or stood off.* The entries ran to 1700 hours and stopped.

At 2300, a blank line.

She turned to October 3 and wrote the date at the head of the column: *3 October 1943.* Then the time: *0615 hours.* Then, in the body of the entry:

*Discovery of body, seaward face of breakwater, approximately 210 feet from pier head. Male adult, estimated 50–60 years. No papers or identification found on the body. Clothing: wool overcoat, wool trousers, leather shoes. Clothing not consistent with fishing or working-coast dress. No oilskin. Death by drowning presumed. Condition consistent with several days' exposure. Channel: closed, debris present in navigable channel.*

She set the pen down. She read it back from the heading. It was accurate. Every particular she had observed was in the column.

She looked at the October 2 entry.

At 2300, the blank line.

She picked up the pen. She held it over the column. Then she wrote, in the narrow space under the 2300 heading:

*Weather: NE gale, 35 knots decreasing at first light. Channel: temporarily impaired.*

She set the pen down. She looked at what she had written.

She closed the log.

---

The radio on the shelf to the left of the desk. She warmed it up and made the call to the District Commander's office in Rockland. Discovery of body. Location on the seaward face of the breakwater. Description: male adult, approximate age, no identifying papers. She read from the log entry. The man at the other end took it down and read it back to her correctly and said someone would be out by late afternoon. She asked about the reporting form. He said they would bring the form. She asked whether she should contact the constable. He said that was her affair locally. She said yes and hung up.

She went to the telephone on the shack's outer wall and rang Constable Tibbetts. The line rang seven times. She was ready to hang up when it picked up.

"Yes," Tibbetts said.

She gave him the facts in order: the location, the condition of the body, no papers, the District Commander already reached, his presence needed at the breakwater.

"Twenty minutes," he said.

She went back inside. Through the one window she looked at the *Agnes Louise* on the shingle. The tide still going out; the hull would be on the bottom within the hour. She made a note in the margin of the tide chart to reach the Bellamy family. The pot warp on the second cleat was a hazard and would need clearing before she could open the channel. Another note. She put the kettle on the oil burner.

Tibbetts came in forty minutes.

---

She walked out with him to the two-hundred-foot mark. He had a flashlight. The light was better than at first look, though still thin and grey.

He stood at the edge and looked at the body for a long moment.

"Jesus," he said.

He did not touch it.

"Papers?" he said.

"Nothing. Both outer pockets, the inside breast. One trouser pocket."

He played the flashlight along the body and over the granite above and below where it lay. He was thorough in his way. She waited.

"Not from here," he said.

"No."

A herring gull came in low over the seaward face and turned and was gone. The swell ran through below them at long intervals.

"County examiner," Tibbetts said.

"Yes."

"That'll be this afternoon. They'll drive out from the county seat."

She had assumed so. They walked back to the shack and she gave him tea. He sat in the second chair. He was not a man who talked when there was nothing decided to say, which had always suited her. She drank her tea and watched the harbor through the window — the *Agnes Louise* on the shingle, mast swinging slowly now as the hull settled; the pot warp on the second cleat; the channel still closed, the debris from three days of blow still distributed through the navigable water.

She went out twice while they waited. Once to check the *Agnes Louise* from the pier end — the hull had settled hard on the bottom; whoever owned her now would need to wait for the next high water and assess from the water, not the pier. Once to look at the south shoulder marker in the strengthening light. Still cycling. Good.

The things that could be done, she did.

---

The county examiner's man came at half past one in a truck with two younger men and a canvas stretcher. He walked out with Tibbetts, looked at the body, came back to the shack, and made out the form in duplicate. He kept the signed copy and handed her the other.

"Probable drowning," he said, to the air between them. "No papers."

"No papers," Tibbetts confirmed.

The two younger men brought the body back on the stretcher and loaded it into the truck. The truck drove out along the pier and up the road toward the county seat. Tibbetts stayed another twenty minutes and then put his hat on.

"Not much to report," he said.

"No," she said.

He went.

---

She was alone in the shack. The lamp on the desk. Outside, the sky had gone pewter — by four o'clock in October the light gave up, and it was close to four. She opened the log.

She read back through October 2. The 0545 tide check. The morning conditions. The channel closure. The midday entry. The 1630 gale report. Each entry in her hand, clear and deliberate, the way she had kept the log for eight years — the way she had learned to keep it in the years before, when Owen kept it and she sat at the other end of the same desk and wrote in it beside him. At the bottom of the October 2 column, the day's accounting ran out.

At 2300, in her own hand: *Weather: NE gale, 35 knots decreasing at first light. Channel: temporarily impaired.*

She read it.

She turned to October 3 and read the body entry from its heading to its last line. Accurate. Complete.

She closed the log and set it on the desk.

Outside, the harbor moved against the breakwater in long slow pushes, each one a little less than the last. The lamp made a small sound — oil in the reservoir, settling. Above the town, somewhere, a gull called once.
