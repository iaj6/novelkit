# Chapter 09 — Riding at Anchor

**One-line summary:** Two days at anchor off Cumberland Head: the carpenters still finishing, Prevost's repeated demands, the gunlock problem resolved, and the long silence of a ship waiting for an order it hasn't fully chosen to receive.

**POV character:** Thomas Briggs  
**Tense:** Third-person limited, past tense  
**Setting/time:** H.M.S. *Confiance*, at anchor off Cumberland Head, Lake Champlain. September 8–10, 1814.

**Chapter shape:** Bottle (one location — the ship at anchor — compressed time)  
**Ending mode:** Literary fade

**Target words:** ~2,800

---

## Goal / Obstacle

- **Goal:** Keep the ship and crew at readiness for an engagement that may come tomorrow, or the day after, or the day after that.
- **Obstacle:** The waiting itself. The fact that the ship is still not fully ready; the carpenters still completing small works; another dispatch from Prevost demanding the fleet move sooner; and the private knowledge — Thomas's, unspoken — that a ship is not made ready by being ordered to be ready.

---

## Action Beats

1. **Morning at anchor.** The first full day at the anchorage. The routine of a ship not yet in action: watches running, guns drilled twice, the carpenter and his mates still working on small details of the upper deck. Thomas runs his crew through the sequence in the morning and again in the afternoon. The second run goes faster — almost well. He notes the specific man who has improved most and the one who has not. Neither fact is announced.

2. **A second dispatch from shore.** The dispatch boat comes again from Prevost's headquarters. The word moves through the ship: the army is ready and wants the fleet to move. The engagement was supposed to be yesterday; it wasn't. Now it is to be the day after tomorrow. Or possibly tomorrow. The precise date is unclear in the retelling, as it always is. What is clear is the pressure: Prevost wants action, and the crew knows it.

3. **The gunlock problem, resolved.** Thomas's piece still has the lock that was malfunctioning at Île aux Noix. He takes it apart in the afternoon watch — carefully, on the deck near his station, with the three small tools he has carried for eleven years. The spring tension is wrong; a worn part, or a tolerance that was never right. He adjusts what can be adjusted, replaces what can be replaced from the armory's supply. By the end of the watch, the lock fires on the first strike. He fits it to the gun and tests it twice. Then sets it aside and takes up the linstock anyway — the lock may fail again in action; the linstock will not.

4. **The French-Canadian carpenter.** Thomas passes the carpenters at their work again — the same carpenter from Chapter 01, or another, working on a last piece of deck planking. They have been aboard for three weeks. They will be put ashore before the engagement. The carpenter is efficient, untroubled, focused on the plank. Thomas watches him for a moment. He thinks about what the carpenter knows about the ship and what he does not.

5. **A thought of Elizabeth.** The evening watch. Thomas below at one point — not his watch, a rare hour off. Elizabeth's letter is in his pocket. He does not take it out. He has had the letter for four months; the ink is worn along the folds where it was opened and closed too many times. The room at Devonport — actually her room, her parents' house while he is away — is a specific place he does not think about for more than a moment. He is not sentimental; he is practical, and the letter is a practical problem: he cannot send a reply from here.

6. **Night watch.** Thomas on the larboard bow in the dark. The lake: flat black, the sky clear, the Vermont mountains visible as shapes against the stars. The American fleet is invisible — less than two miles away, he knows, but the night hides it entirely. The bay holds no light. He tries to hear across the water; there is nothing. The wind has shifted slightly — still predominantly from the southwest, still a headwind. He notes it. The watch passes. The bell marks the half-hours.

---

## Outcome / Turn

Two days of waiting at anchor. The gunlock is fixed; the carpenters are nearly done; Prevost's pressure has reached everyone aboard. The ship is as ready as she will be.

---

## New Question or Pressure Raised

Can a ship be made ready by will and an order? Thomas knows the answer; the chapter does not say it.

## Continuity Notes

- Carpenters from Île aux Noix were still working aboard *Confiance* as she sailed and will be landed before the battle. [cite:14]
- Prevost sent multiple dispatches to Downie demanding the naval attack be synchronized with his land assault. The pressure was significant. [cite:12]
- Some of *Confiance*'s gunlocks were non-functional at the time of the action; this is a documented fact. [continuity #15, cite:02] Thomas fixing one does not resolve the ship's readiness problem — it fixes one lock.
- Thomas carries Elizabeth's letter, folded and read so many times the ink is worn along the creases. He does not reread it in front of others. [continuity #3]
- Thomas does not have an interior monologue about Elizabeth — her presence is in the letter's weight, not in declared longing.
- Downie is never POV. His receipt of the dispatches reaches Thomas through the ship's information channels, not through access to his thinking.
- The wind from the southwest is historically noted as a factor in the British approach.

## Prose Register Note

This is the most interior of Thomas's approach chapters, but "interior" in his register means: the specific physical acts of a man working through his preparations, and the things that are present but not spoken of. The gunlock repair is the chapter's central visible action — a seaman solving a technical problem with the tools he has. Elizabeth's letter is the invisible gravity. The fade ending should arrive through the night watch, the darkness, and the invisible American fleet across the water: silence as Thomas's instrument, not introspection.
