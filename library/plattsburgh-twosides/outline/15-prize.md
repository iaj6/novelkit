# Chapter 15 — Prize

**One-line summary:** September 12: Thomas aboard an American prize tender, the procedural reality of capture; the lake at evening.

**POV character:** Thomas Briggs  
**Tense:** Third-person limited, past tense  
**Setting/time:** An American prize tender on Lake Champlain. September 12, 1814. A full day.

**Chapter shape:** Single scene  
**Ending mode:** Literary fade

**Target words:** ~2,000

---

## Goal / Obstacle

- **Goal:** Get through the day. Keep the routine.
- **Obstacle:** There is no dramatic obstacle. The chapter's texture is the ordinary continuation of life after catastrophe — not the catastrophe itself, but what comes after and is normal again.

---

## Action Beats

1. **Waking aboard the tender.** Thomas wakes on the prize tender — a small schooner or sloop repurposed to transport prisoners from *Confiance* and the other surrendered vessels. He is among men he knows: his messmates, the men from his gun crew, some of the 39th Foot who served as marines. The space is cramped. The ship's motion is different from *Confiance*'s — lighter, smaller, a different creak in the rigging. He is not in irons.

2. **The parole procedure.** An American naval officer or purser takes names and ratings. The terms are stated: no weapons, no attempt to escape, subsistence allowance of one shilling sixpence per day. Pring has already arranged that the prisoners are treated correctly; Macdonough's reputation reached the men before the formal process. The procedure is administrative, not punitive. Thomas gives his name, rating, and ship. The officer writes it down. Thomas receives his allowance.

3. ***Confiance* visible.** At some point during the day, *Confiance* is visible from the tender — at anchor in Plattsburgh Bay, her colors gone, her masts standing or partly down. Thomas looks at her for the duration of a few breaths, then looks away. What he thinks: not stated. What he does: returns to watching the lake.

4. **Men talking.** His shipmates speculate about where they'll be held. Someone mentions Dartmoor. Another says Dartmoor is for ocean-captured men; they're inland, this is different. Parole to a town somewhere in New York or Vermont, more likely. The argument is inconclusive. Thomas listens without joining. He knows they won't know until they're told, and there is no use in the speculation, but the speculation is what men do when they have nothing else to do with their hands and their time.

5. **Elizabeth's letter.** Thomas's hand goes to his pocket at some point in the day. The letter is there. He takes it out — not to read, just to confirm. The paper is damp from the action (perhaps he was wet, or the spray came over the rail, or it is simply the dampness of a captured man on a lake in September). He returns it to his pocket without opening it.

6. **The lake.** The tender moves on the lake through the day — across to the American side, or south toward a landing, or simply at anchor waiting for orders. The lake in September: morning flat, afternoon ruffled by the southwest wind, the mountains on either shore. At the highest elevations, the first maple color has come on — red and orange at the crowns, the lower slopes still green. Thomas watches it. He has seen the Mediterranean and the Channel and the Atlantic. This lake is inland and small by those measures. He watches it anyway.

7. **End.** The light goes off the Vermont mountains. The evening comes. Thomas watches the lake until the light is gone.

---

## Outcome / Turn

Thomas is alive and among his shipmates. The procedural fact of his captivity is established. His ship is gone. He is watching the lake.

---

## New Question or Pressure Raised

None. The book has closed this thread with what is true: he is alive, he is among his shipmates, his ship is gone, the lake is there.

## Continuity Notes

- Prisoner-of-war procedure: names taken, ratings recorded, parole terms given, subsistence at 1s. 6d./day for non-officers. [cite:28]
- Commander Pring praised Macdonough's humane treatment of prisoners in his post-battle testimony. The prisoners are not abused. [characters.md, Pring entry]
- The inland lake context makes Dartmoor unlikely; prisoners from lake engagements were typically paroled to towns in the interior. However, the characters argue about this because they don't know. The book does not resolve this question — the brief's endpoint is September 12, 1814.
- Thomas carries Elizabeth's letter. He has never read it in front of others, and he does not read it now. The check on its presence is physical and private. [continuity #3]
- *Confiance* visible in the bay: the ship still existed as a hulk after surrender; the Americans assessed her as a prize. Her masts were partly damaged in the action.
- The book does not go beyond September 12. Whatever happens to Thomas after this day is not shown.
- The chapter does not philosophize about what the battle meant, what the war meant, or what Thomas's experience signifies. [style.md, continuity #26]
- The first maple color at the crowns: September in the Champlain Valley. [world.md]

## Prose Register Note

This chapter should feel like the morning after something enormous, in the register of the morning after: the ordinary procedures, the ordinary bodies of men, the lake being the lake. Thomas's register does not change. He is not broken; he is not philosophically altered; he is a Royal Navy able seaman on the morning after his ship was taken, doing what a captured seaman does, watching the world he is still in. The literary fade ending is earned by watching: Thomas watching the lake until the light is gone. This is not metaphor for grief or loss — it is a man watching a lake because the light is still on it and there is nothing else required of him in this hour.
