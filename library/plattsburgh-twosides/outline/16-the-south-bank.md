# Chapter 16 — The South Bank

**One-line summary:** September 12: Asa on the south bank of the Saranac, the British retreat road visible to the north, the mill three miles upstream.

**POV character:** Asa Beckwith  
**Tense:** Third-person limited, past tense  
**Setting/time:** The south bank of the Saranac River, Pike's Ford area, three miles upstream of Plattsburgh. September 12, 1814. A full day.

**Chapter shape:** Single scene  
**Ending mode:** Resolved

**Target words:** ~2,000

---

## Goal / Obstacle

- **Goal:** Get through the day. Understand what is visible.
- **Obstacle:** None dramatic. The chapter's texture is what remains after the battle, and what remains is the ordinary world — the river, the ford, the men of his company, and the road to the north where the British army is going.

---

## Action Beats

1. **Morning at the ford.** The south bank is quiet. Asa and the company are still at their positions, but the north bank holds nothing — no soldiers, no fires, no movement. The ford is empty water over the gravel bar. Birds in the trees on both banks. The Saranac running as it always runs.

2. **The retreat road.** Through a gap in the trees to the north — the road to Canada is visible from the south bank at the ford, a quarter mile away. The British army is on it, moving north: wagons, artillery limbers, men on foot in the red coats that were across the ford yesterday. Moving in order but moving away. Asa watches from the south bank. He watches the road the way he watches the river.

3. **The company in the morning.** The men of his company at their ordinary tasks: cleaning muskets, eating, sleeping against the bank in the September sun. One man is fishing in the Saranac, standing knee-deep at the bar. The fish don't know what yesterday was. The sergeant is writing something — a report, probably. Nobody asks Asa to do anything in particular.

4. **Crossing the ford.** Asa wades the ford from south to north — the gravel bar, the channel, the north bank. He has not been on the north bank since before the war started, or near enough. The bank is disturbed: boot prints in the clay, a place where many men stood, branches broken. The passage of a large force is readable in the ground the way a large volume of timber is readable in the millpond — the signs are physical and specific. He reads them without sentimentality. Then he wades back.

5. **News from the lake.** Some point in the morning: a man brings word from downstream. The British fleet surrendered yesterday. *Confiance* struck her colors around noon. The flagship is in American hands; Captain Downie was killed early in the action. The naval details are compressed and probably not entirely accurate in the retelling. But the main fact is there: the fleet is gone.

6. **Asa thinks about the mill.** Sometime in the afternoon: the road to the north is emptying. The wagons and the columns of infantry thinning. The retreat is almost complete. Asa is sitting on the south bank, the river in front of him. He thinks, with a specificity he hasn't allowed himself in the past week, about the mill three miles upstream — Elias, the pitman arm, the log carriage, the sound of the sash, the smell of fresh pine. The work that is waiting there. He doesn't say any of this. He thinks it, the way he thinks about the depth of the ford or the grain of a piece of timber.

7. **End.** Afternoon giving to evening. The road to the north is empty now. The retreat has passed; the valley is returning to its shape. The Saranac runs past the south bank. The September light comes at a low angle over the Vermont mountains. The ford is the ford it has always been. The day closes.

---

## Outcome / Turn

The battle is over. The British are gone. Asa is alive, on the south bank of the Saranac, the mill upstream.

---

## New Question or Pressure Raised

None. The book ends here, on this bank, on this day.

- The mill three miles upstream is Elias Beckwith's sawmill, Asa's home ground. It is present as a specific place Asa is thinking about, not as a symbol of something larger. He will go back to it; the book doesn't show him going.

## Prose Register Note

This is the book's final chapter. The resolved ending mode is appropriate here: the day closes, the road empties, the river runs. Asa's register is the same as it has been throughout — paratactic, observational, the world delivered in sequence. The chapter does not gather itself for a statement about the war, about the battle, about the meaning of Asa's nine days on the south bank of the Saranac. It observes what is there: the ford, the company, the empty road, the mill upstream that Asa has been thinking about without saying so.

The closing image mirrors the ending of Asa's day-before chapter (Chapter 02): the Saranac at dusk, ordinary, specific. This is the structural argument of the day-before and day-after pairing — not that the world is unchanged, but that the world continues in its ordinary modes and those modes are the same world the characters have always inhabited. The prose honors this continuity by treating the end with the same care and restraint it brought to the beginning.
