# Chapter 07 — The Dispatch

**One-line summary:** The squadron arrives near Cumberland Head and anchors; Thomas sees the American line for the first time, and Prevost's order to engage reaches *Confiance*.

**POV character:** Thomas Briggs  
**Tense:** Third-person limited, past tense  
**Setting/time:** H.M.S. *Confiance* on Lake Champlain approaching Cumberland Head, then at anchor outside Plattsburgh Bay. September 7–9, 1814.

**Chapter shape:** Multi-scene (approach to Cumberland Head, first sight of the American fleet, the dispatch arriving)  
**Ending mode:** Mid-action

**Target words:** ~3,000

---

## Goal / Obstacle

- **Goal:** Establish the squadron's position for the coming engagement.
- **Obstacle:** The American fleet's position in the bay is well-chosen — springs on cables, a narrow bay, the wind against the British approach. What Thomas sees across the water is not disorder but preparation.

---

## Action Beats

1. **The approach.** The third day on the lake. Cumberland Head is visible from a distance — the long peninsula that closes off Plattsburgh Bay from the north and east. The squadron closes on it. Thomas on watch during the approach. The lake here is at its northern third; the widest point. The sky clear, a light southwest breeze. He notes the wind with his usual exactness: southwest, which means into the bay they would be beating against it.

2. **Rounding the head.** The squadron rounds Cumberland Head. The bay opens to the south and west. For the first time, Thomas sees what they are sailing against: the American fleet at anchor in a line, roughly one thousand yards from the western shore. Four larger vessels, their colors flying — the Eagle at the north end, the Saratoga (the flag, he's been told) next, then the Ticonderoga and the Preble. Gunboats in the intervals, on the flanks. He counts them. He looks at the angle of the cables and can see, even from this distance, that the ships are rigged with springs — lines back along the cable to the capstan, the ships rotatable at anchor.

3. **Thomas's assessment.** He has seen fleet formations before. He has stood on the deck of *Achilles* at Trafalgar and seen the combined fleet stretched across the horizon — dozens of ships in disorder, spread wide, poorly commanded. The American line is twelve hundred yards long at most, compact, positioned to command the bay's approaches, with the western shore close enough to prevent flanking. The springs are the detail that stay with him. A ship at anchor cannot be outmaneuvered — it can only fire. The springs mean Macdonough has turned the lack of maneuver into a deliberate choice. Thomas does not say any of this. He notes the wind.

4. **Anchoring.** *Confiance* anchors outside the bay, off Cumberland Head. The other squadron vessels take their positions. The carpenters from the yard are still aboard, still completing small works. A boat goes ashore. Thomas stands his watch and sees the boat go and come back. Ordinary procedures.

5. **The dispatch.** A dispatch boat from Prevost's headquarters on shore comes alongside. A letter is taken aboard. Downie receives it aft; Thomas is not privy to its contents. But by the time the watch changes, the word has moved through the ship in the way that word always moves on a lower deck: the army wants the squadron to engage when the land assault goes in. The date mentioned in the dispatch is day after tomorrow, or perhaps tomorrow — the accounts differ in the retelling as they pass. What is consistent: there is a date, there is a plan, the two forces are to move together.

6. **Thomas hears it from a messmate.** They are eating in the evening. A man who was near the midshipman who was near the surgeon who heard something from the wardroom. Thomas listens. The messmate's reaction is the flat equanimity of a seaman who has received many such orders from many quarterdeck windows. Thomas's reaction: he notes the wind. Southwest. A beat to enter the bay. Confiance and her green crew on a headwind in a confined bay against an anchored fleet with springs. He sets down his spoon and finishes his meal.

7. **End.** Thomas on deck after the evening meal. The bay to the south, the American fleet invisible in the dark now but its position known. The last light on the Vermont mountains. The chapter ends on the new fact: there is a date, and the squadron is committed.

---

## Outcome / Turn

The British squadron is in position. A specific timeline for the engagement has been established by Prevost's order. Thomas has seen what they are sailing against.

---

## New Question or Pressure Raised

The approach to the bay will be a beat against the headwind. What does that mean for the timing and the formation when the shooting starts?

## Continuity Notes

- Cumberland Head forms the northern and eastern boundary of Plattsburgh Bay. [continuity #18]
- American line arrangement (north to south): *Eagle*, *Saratoga* (flag, Macdonough), *Ticonderoga*, *Preble*, with ten gunboats in the intervals and on the flanks. [continuity #18]
- The springs on cables: lines run from the capstan back along the anchor cable, allowing the ship to rotate and present a fresh broadside. [continuity #18]
- Prevost's dispatches: he sent multiple letters demanding synchronized naval and land action. He had promised coordinated support; the timing of the naval attack was to coincide with his land assault. [cite:12]
- Downie is never POV — seen at the quarterdeck, receiving letters, giving orders to subordinates.
- The wind from the southwest is historically noted as a factor making the British approach to the bay difficult.
- Thomas's comparison to Trafalgar (the combined fleet) is a professional reference, not a dramatic one — the point is that this fleet is better positioned, not that Thomas is feeling heroic memories.
- Macdonough is never POV [continuity #29]. His flag is visible; he is not.

## Prose Register Note

This is the chapter in which Thomas sees what he will be fighting. The register must stay in his professional vocabulary — the word "springs" on the cables is his noun for what he observes, not a term he explains. The chapter's weight is in what he doesn't say to the messmate at dinner: the whole tactical problem is visible, held silently, rendered in the small gesture of setting down the spoon. The style guide's "when he is afraid he notes the wind" is the operative principle here.
