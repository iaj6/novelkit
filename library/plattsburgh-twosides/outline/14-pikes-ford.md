# Chapter 14 — Pike's Ford

**One-line summary:** September 11: the British assault Pike's Ford in force; Asa holds his position using his knowledge of the river; the naval cannonade runs through the chapter from east-southeast until it stops.

**POV character:** Asa Beckwith  
**Tense:** Third-person limited, past tense  
**Setting/time:** Pike's Ford, Saranac River. September 11, 1814, approximately 8:00 AM into the early afternoon.

**Chapter shape:** Action-heavy  
**Ending mode:** Mid-action

**Target words:** ~4,000

---

## Goal / Obstacle

- **Goal:** Hold the ford against the British crossing attempt.
- **Obstacle:** Peninsular War veterans — disciplined, experienced, numerous — attempting to force a river crossing against a company of New York militia that has been at this position for a week.

---

## Action Beats

1. **First light, the ford occupied.** Picking up directly from Chapter 12's ending: both banks visible in the early light, British soldiers in the tree line on the north bank. Asa at his position. The ford between them. The sergeant's final preparation: verify ammunition, confirm positions, remind the powder carriers. The ordinary detail of having the piece loaded and primed.

2. **The cannonade begins.** From the direction of the bay — to the east-southeast, past the village, past the Saranac's mouth — a sound rolls across the valley. Not thunder; the sky is clear. Rolling, sustained, directionless — the concussion transmitted through the air more than through a specific direction. Asa turns his head toward it without thinking. Then turns back to the ford. It enters his chapter as weather — present, noted, irrelevant to the immediate task. The chapter will not explain it.

3. **The crossing begins.** British soldiers move from the tree line to the north bank. In column, then spreading as they enter the water — red coats, equipment, the sound of men wading the Saranac. They hit the gravel bar and advance across it toward the ford's center. Asa is watching the depth: he knows exactly when the water will change beneath their feet on the far side of the bar, where the channel begins.

4. **First fire.** The sergeant's command: front rank, fire. The company's front rank fires by sections — not quite a volley, something close to one. The smoke immediately in front of them. Asa fires. He reloads: prime and load, the sequence from the drill, executed under the condition the drill prepared for. The rear rank fires while the front rank reloads. The British in the crossing. Some fall; others press.

5. **The channel.** The British advance past the gravel bar and into the channel — the part Asa knew about, the chest-deep current over clay bottom. The column breaks on the depth. Men struggling with the current, their footing gone. The militia fires into the crossing. The assault falters and pulls back to the gravel bar, then the north bank.

6. **The cannonade, ongoing.** The sound from the bay is constant through the morning. It enters in between the shots and reloads — not as a narrative comment, just as a fact of sound present in the morning air. Asa does not know what it means for the battle there. He is at the ford.

7. **Second assault.** The British regroup on the north bank. A different approach this time — a section of soldiers moves upstream along the bank, trying to find a better crossing point. Asa sees them going. He knows this river; he knows there is no better crossing within a quarter mile in either direction at this water level. He tells the sergeant: the bend upstream is worse, deeper, clay bottom all the way. A section of the company is sent upstream to cover it anyway. The British try the upstream approach; the channel holds them. They fall back.

8. **Third and hardest push.** The main body tries the ford again, this time in larger numbers and with more momentum. The British regulars are experienced and they push through the musket fire across the bar. They reach the edge of the channel. This is the closest they come. Asa is firing, reloading, firing. A man beside him takes a ball in the shoulder — not dead, unable to reload — and Asa takes the man's prepared charge to load his own piece with. Another man is hit. The sergeant is shouting. The militia is ragged but it is holding.

9. **A man Asa knows.** Brief moment in the intensity: a man in the company from Asa's home district, who he has known since childhood, says something to Asa in the middle of the crossing — a name, a question, something ordinary in an extraordinary moment. The drafter has latitude here; the point is the presence of a specific person, a civilian in a soldier's moment, the human scale of the thing.

10. **The cannonade diminishes.** Somewhere in the late morning — after the third assault is repulsed and the British are again on the north bank — the sound from the bay changes. It has been continuous for hours; now it is intermittent. Then it becomes scattered shots. Then, at some point that Asa cannot mark exactly, it stops. The morning is not quieter overall — there is still the ford, still the company, still the Saranac — but the rolling percussion from the east is gone.

11. **The British withdrawal.** The north bank: the British are pulling back from the crossing point. Not fleeing — withdrawing in order, a disciplined movement rearward. A rider appears on the north bank road at speed, moving toward the British lines downstream. Something has changed. Asa watches. The ford is clear of men in the water now; the gravel bar is empty.

12. **End.** The ford is held. The British soldiers visible on the north bank are moving north along the road, away from the river. The cannonade from the bay has been silent for some time. Asa looks at what is left on his side of the ford: his company, several men wounded, the sergeant still upright. The Saranac runs past the gravel bar as it always has. Asa doesn't know what happened on the lake. He knows what happened here.

## Outcome / Turn

The ford is held. The British crossing at Pike's Ford failed. The naval cannonade has stopped.

## Prose Register Note

This is Asa's longest chapter and its climax. The prose contracts in action: shorter sentences, specific physical details, Asa's awareness bounded by his position. The cannonade from the bay enters in the spaces between action — in the reloads, in the brief pauses between assaults — never as narration. The drafter must not explain what the cannonade means: Asa hears it, notes it, returns to the ford. When it stops, he notes that it has stopped. That is all. The reader carries the meaning across from Chapter 13.

The ford battle is ground-level, close, wet. The British are not villains — they are soldiers doing their job under fire. Asa is not a hero — he is a nineteen-year-old standing in a position he knows, doing what he's been drilled to do. The prose gives neither side the evaluation. It renders what Asa can see and hear and feel, and the specific facts of the ford's geography as they play out.
