## 1. September 6–12, 1814 Campaign Timeline

A consolidated day-by-day chronology. All dates and movements are taken from multiple overlapping secondary sources with reference to primary dispatches where cited.

**August 31:** Sir George Prevost's army of approximately 10,300–11,000 regulars and Canadian militia departs from the Canadian frontier toward Plattsburgh. [cite:01]

**September 3:** Captain George Downie officially takes command of HMS *Confiance* at Île aux Noix on the Richelieu River. [cite:04]

**September 5:** Confiance's crew — assembled as late as this date — embarks. British post-action records describe them as "totally unknown either to the Officers or to each other." [cite:02]

**September 6:** British advance guard makes first contact with American defenders near Beekmantown, Clinton County, New York. The New York State Militia withdraws "in great disarray" toward Culver Hill under pressure from the British right wing. Major John E. Wool (29th U.S. Infantry) arrives with ~250 regulars, rallies some militia, and makes a fighting withdrawal through Culver Hill and Halsey's Corners before crossing the Saranac into Plattsburgh. [cite:10] American and British casualties at this stage estimated at 45 American and 200–300 British. [cite:10] General Prevost's army reaches the north bank of the Saranac and halts, awaiting the navy. [cite:01]

**September 7–10:** Standoff on the Saranac. American defenders (Macomb's regulars, Mooers's New York militia, and arriving Vermont militia under Strong) hold the south bank. The bridges are planked-up or partially destroyed. [cite:01] Downie's squadron is still fitting out at Île aux Noix.

**September 9:** A night raid led by Captain Ambrose McGlassin (15th U.S. Infantry) — 50 Americans cross the Saranac and destroy a British Congreve rocket battery under construction. [cite:11]

**September 11:** Dawn — Downie's squadron "scales" the guns (fires without shot, a prearranged signal between Downie and Major Coote of the army) to signal the fleet's readiness. [cite:01] The land attack is supposed to begin simultaneously. 

~9:00 AM: British squadron rounds Cumberland Head into Plattsburgh Bay. [cite:01]

~9:00–9:15 AM: *Confiance* fires a devastating opening broadside that kills or wounds approximately one-fifth of *Saratoga*'s crew. [cite:02]

Within 15 minutes: Downie is killed when a cannonball from *Saratoga* strikes the muzzle of one of *Confiance*'s 24-pounders, dismounting it onto him. [cite:04] Lieutenant James Robertson assumes command of *Confiance*.

~10:00 AM: Prevost finally issues orders for the land assault — over an hour after the naval battle began. [cite:01] Brisbane's brigade makes feint attacks at the town bridges. Robinson's brigade, assigned the main flanking attack via Pike's Ford, eventually crosses the Saranac ~3 miles above the American defenses.

~11:00 AM: Macdonough orders *Saratoga* wound: bow anchor cut, kedge lines hauled, ship swings 180°, bringing the undamaged port battery to bear on *Confiance*. [cite:06] Robertson attempts the same maneuver but only exposes *Confiance*'s vulnerable stern.

By noon: *Confiance* surrenders. *Chubb* had already drifted into American lines and been taken. *Finch* had grounded near the American battery on Crab Island. *Linnet* surrenders shortly after *Confiance*. [cite:01]

Upon news of the naval defeat: Prevost orders a halt to Robinson's ground advance (Robinson's men had successfully crossed and were driving the American militia back). Prevost orders retreat. His brigade commanders — Peninsular War veterans Brisbane, Robinson, and Power — are "appalled" by the decision. [cite:01]

**September 12:** British army begins its retreat north toward Canada. 234 soldiers desert during the march. Most desertions are from the Regiment de Meuron (mixed-nationality Swiss mercenaries), the 2/8th (weakened by Walcheren Campaign sickness), and the 1/27th (which had seen limited action before this campaign). Very few Peninsular War veterans desert. [cite:01]

## 2. Geography: Plattsburgh Bay, Lake Champlain, the Saranac River

**Plattsburgh Bay:** A bay on the western shore of Lake Champlain, in the northern third of the lake. Bounded on the north and east by Cumberland Head, a peninsula extending east and then south. The city of Plattsburgh lies on the western (mainland) shore. The bay is sometimes called Cumberland Bay. [cite:16]

**Cumberland Head:** A peninsula projecting eastward into Lake Champlain. The British squadron was required to round this headland before entering Plattsburgh Bay, which meant coming into the Americans' field of fire with limited maneuverability. [cite:16]

**Macdonough's anchor line:** The American ships were anchored approximately 1,000 yards from the beach in a north-to-south line: *Eagle* (northernmost), *Saratoga*, *Ticonderoga*, *Preble* (southernmost). Ten gunboats supported to the south. [cite:06]

**The Saranac River:** Flows roughly east-northeast from the Adirondacks into Lake Champlain at Plattsburgh. The lower portion of the river drops steeply. **Inference (not in sources):** The river in early September likely ran lower and slower than in spring flood, making fords possible upstream that would be impassable in April. The USGS monitoring station at Plattsburgh documents current flow but no 1814 measurements are available.

**Bridge and ford locations:** 
- **Lower Bridge:** In Plattsburgh village proper, at or near the river mouth. Planks were removed by American defenders when they retreated. [cite:01]
- **Upper Bridge:** Within Plattsburgh, some distance upstream from Lower Bridge. Also planked. Mooers's militia was stationed to defend this position. [cite:11]
- **Pike's Ford:** Located approximately 3 miles (4.8 km) above Macomb's defensive line, near what had been Pike's Cantonment (Zebulon Pike's winter camp 1812–13). This was the site of Robinson's main flanking assault on September 11. [cite:01] One source places the successful British crossing "a mile and a half above the upper bridge." [cite:09] **Disputed:** Two figures appear in the sources — 3 miles above "Macomb's defenses" and 1.5 miles above "the upper bridge." These are consistent if Macomb's defenses ran nearly to the upper bridge; the 3-mile figure is the more reliable.

**Saranac mills:** Sawmills and gristmills operated on the Saranac from the 1780s. The first known settler in the Plattsburgh area, Count Charles de Fredenburg, established a sawmill in 1768 "three miles upriver at the point now called Fredenburg Falls." [cite:17] Settlement accelerated from 1785. By 1814, sawmills using the river's waterpower were a routine part of the local economy. [cite:17]

## 3. British Naval Squadron: Ships, Armament, Crew, Manning Crisis

| Ship | Type | Guns | Commander | Crew | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| HMS *Confiance* | 5th-rate frigate | 37 (mounted); 39 (carried) | Captain George Downie | ~270–300 | Flag; largest ship on the lake |
| HMS *Linnet* | Brig | 16 × 12-pounders | Commander Daniel Pring | 99 | [cite:05a] |
| HMS *Chubb* | Sloop | 11 guns | Lt. James McGhie | ~50 | Drifted into American line; captured |
| HMS *Finch* | Sloop | 11 guns | Lt. William Hicks | ~50 | Grounded on Crab Island |
| 12 gunboats | Varied | Varied | — | ~930 total across squadron | [cite:01] |

**HMS *Confiance* armament:** 30 × 24-pounder long guns on the main deck, 6 × 32-pounder carronades, 1 × 24-pounder pivot gun. At time of battle carried 39 guns total but only 37 were mounted. [cite:02]

**Total British squadron:** approximately 930 sailors across all vessels. [cite:01]

**Manning crisis:** *Confiance*'s crew was embarked no earlier than September 5, six days before the battle. They were, per the British post-action court martial record, "totally unknown either to the Officers or to each other." Downie "was acquainted with no officer on board his ship except his first lieutenant." The volunteers recruited from Quebec ships were described as "inferior quality and bad character, several having been in irons." [cite:04] A company of the 39th (Dorsetshire) Regiment of Foot served aboard *Confiance* as marines — soldiers, not trained naval gunners. [cite:02]

**Ordnance crisis:** The ship's magazine was not finished at the time of battle; ordnance had to be brought out to the ship in small boats. The gunlocks (flintlock firing mechanisms) were improvised and poorly fitted, requiring the gun crews to fire with slow-match or linstocks rather than the more precise percussion-style lock. Work continued on *Confiance* up to departure; the last carpenters were dropped off at Cumberland Head before the battle. [cite:02]

**Commander Pring's role:** Daniel Pring commanded *Linnet* and had commanded the Lake Champlain squadron before Downie's appointment. He survived the battle and commanded the naval court martial in August 1815 aboard HMS *Gladiator* at Portsmouth; the court "honorably acquitted all those charged" and "emphatically placed the blame for the defeat on Prevost for forcing the British squadron into action prematurely." [cite:01]

## 4. HMS *Confiance* and Île aux Noix: The Rushed Build

**Île aux Noix Naval Shipyard:** Established as a Royal Navy facility in 1812 on Île aux Noix (Nut Island) in the Richelieu River, Quebec, as the British base and construction facility for the Lake Champlain fleet. HMS *Confiance* was the largest vessel ever constructed there. The yard operated through 1834 before closing. [cite:18]

**Construction timeline:**
- Laid down: spring 1814
- Launched: August 25, 1814 (seventeen days before the battle)
- Commissioned/ready: never formally — she went into battle still incomplete [cite:02]

**Construction deficiencies documented in sources:**
- Magazine incomplete at time of battle; ordnance followed in small boats [cite:02]
- "Portions of the superstructure were missing" [cite:02]
- Gunlocks "ill fitting fastenings hastily improvised by the crew" [cite:02]
- Scantlings (structural timbers) described as "of the very worst timber for building ships" in a yard commander's note [cite:02]
- Workmen still aboard when she left Île aux Noix; the last carpenters were discharged at Cumberland Head before the engagement [cite:02]

**Prevost's pressure on Downie:** Prevost repeatedly urged Downie to accelerate. Downie promised readiness no earlier than September 15, and added "even then, her crew would not have been exercised." [cite:02] The naval court martial of 1815 found that "defeat had been caused principally by Prevost's urging the squadron into premature action." [cite:12]

**The ship's end:** After capture, *Confiance* was taken to Whitehall, New York, where she was used as Macdonough's winter headquarters, 1814–15. She sank in 1820 in the Poultney River mouth and was destroyed by explosives during 1873 dredging. [cite:02]

## 5. George Downie: Biography and Command

**Birth:** January 19, 1778, at Tong near Stornoway, Isle of Lewis, Outer Hebrides, Scotland. Eighth son of Reverend John Downie and Charlotte Mackenzie. [cite:04]

**Age at Plattsburgh:** 36 years, 7 months. *Note to drafter: The brief's research scope mentions "Downie's biography and career to age 33" — this appears to be a brief-writing error, as Downie was definitively 36 when he died. Use the documented age of 36.*

**Career chronology:**
- c. 1790: Joins Royal Navy as midshipman, HMS *Circe* [cite:04]
- October 11, 1797: Participates in the Battle of Camperdown (Dutch fleet defeated off the Texel) [cite:04]
- West Indies service aboard frigates *Melampus* and *Apollo* [cite:04]
- March 23, 1802: Confirmed lieutenant [cite:04]
- 1804: Appointed to 38-gun frigate HMS *Seahorse* under Captain Courtenay Boyle [cite:04]
- May 1805: Serving as first lieutenant of *Seahorse*; leads boarding operation capturing Spanish powder vessels; commended in the London Gazette [cite:04]
- July 1808: Still first lieutenant of *Seahorse* under Captain John Stewart; participates in engagement against Turkish vessels *Badere-Zaffer* and *Alis Fezan*; promoted to commander on Stewart's recommendation [cite:04]
- June 15, 1810 – December 1812: Commands 18-gun HMS *Royalist*; captures multiple French privateers [cite:04]
- January 1, 1813: Promoted to post-captain [cite:04]
- 1813–1814: Commands sloop HMS *Montreal* on Lake Ontario [cite:04]
- August 1814: Appointed to command HMS *Confiance* and the Lake Champlain squadron, replacing Captain Peter Fisher [cite:04]
- September 3, 1814: Officially takes command at Île aux Noix [cite:04]
- September 11, 1814: Killed in action at the Battle of Plattsburgh Bay

**Death:** Within approximately 15 minutes of the battle's opening, Downie was standing behind one of *Confiance*'s 24-pounders, sighting it, when a cannonball from *Saratoga* struck the muzzle. The 24-pounder was dismounted and fell on him. "His skin was not broken, a black mark about the size of a small plate was the only visible injury. His watch was found flattened, with its hands pointing to the very second at which he received the fatal blow." [cite:04]

**Burial:** Riverside Cemetery, Plattsburgh, New York. [cite:04]

**Character note:** Downie arrived at Île aux Noix on September 1 and took command two days later. He had eight days in command before his death. He was an experienced frigate officer and had demonstrated personal bravery on *Seahorse*. He explicitly told Prevost the ship would not be ready before September 15. He had no time to know his officers or work up his crew. **Inference (not in sources):** His private assessment of the ship's and squadron's readiness must have been bleak, but no letters survive expressing this directly. His words to Prevost about the September 15 date are the closest record we have.

## 6. American Naval Squadron: Ships, Armament, Deployment

| Ship | Type | Guns | Commander | Crew |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| USS *Saratoga* | Corvette | 26 (8 × 24-pdr long + 6 × 42-pdr carronade + 12 × 32-pdr carronade) | Master Commandant Thomas Macdonough | 212 |
| USS *Eagle* | Brig | 20 guns | Commander Robert Henley | ~150 |
| USS *Ticonderoga* | Schooner | 14 guns | Lieutenant Stephen Cassin | ~110 |
| USS *Preble* | Sloop | 7 guns | Lt. Charles Budd | ~30 |
| 10 gunboats | Varied | Varied | — | — |

**USS *Saratoga* specifications:** 734 long tons; 143 ft (44 m) long; beam 36 ft 6 in (11.13 m); depth 12 ft 6 in. Laid down March 7, 1814; launched April 11, 1814 at Vergennes, Vermont, by Adam and Noah Brown. Time from keel-laying to launch: 35 days. [cite:06]

**USS *Eagle*:** 20-gun brig, joined the flotilla August 27, 1814 — only 19 days after her own keel was laid, with 15 days of fitting-out. [cite:06]

**American long-gun vs. carronade mix:** Macdonough's flagship relied heavily on short-range carronades. The British *Confiance* had a heavier weight of long-gun fire, giving her theoretical advantage at a distance. Macdonough's anchored-in-a-bay deployment denied the British the range at which their long guns would tell. [cite:01]

**The anchor and spring arrangement:** Before the battle, Macdonough dropped kedge anchors from the quarters of *Saratoga* and rigged spring lines to all his ships' anchor cables. This system allowed each ship to be pivoted through a wide arc using muscle power on the anchor lines, without requiring the use of sails. The crews practiced winding before the battle. [cite:06]

**Macdonough's defensive position:** Anchored ~1,000 yards offshore in a north-south line inside the bay, forcing the British to round Cumberland Head and enter the confined bay under fire. The British long-gun advantage was thus reduced by the necessity of coming to short range in restricted waters. [cite:16]

## 7. Thomas Macdonough: Biography and Tactical Preparation

**Birth:** December 31, 1783 (age 30 at Plattsburgh). Sixth of ten children of Major Thomas Macdonough, Revolutionary War veteran. Orphaned at age 12. [cite:19]

**Naval career:**
- February 5, 1800: Enters Navy as midshipman [cite:19]
- Quasi-War with France: service aboard corvette *Ganges* [cite:19]
- 1804 (First Barbary War): Volunteers for mission to destroy captured frigate *Philadelphia* in Tripoli harbor [cite:19]
- 1807: Promoted to lieutenant [cite:19]
- 1810: Commands gunboats on Connecticut shore; briefly leaves Navy for merchant service [cite:19]
- October 1812: Takes command of naval forces on Lake Champlain [cite:19]

**Battle experience:** Macdonough had "little naval combat experience" before 1814. His service in the Barbary Wars consisted of gunboat actions and demolition work, not ship-to-ship combat. The Battle of Lake Champlain was his first fleet action. [cite:19]

**The winding maneuver (detailed):** During the battle, after *Saratoga*'s starboard battery was disabled or dismounted, Macdonough "dropped a stern anchor; cut his bow cable; and, with the help of tars hauling on lines to kedge anchors, swung the ship around bringing her fresh, port, broadside guns to bear." [cite:06] The undamaged port battery then fired on *Confiance*. Robertson, commanding *Confiance* after Downie's death, attempted the same maneuver but only succeeded in presenting his vulnerable stern to the Americans, forcing surrender. [cite:01]

## 8. The Naval Battle, September 11, 1814

**Opening signal:** The British squadron "scaled the guns" — fired without shot, a prearranged signal between Downie and army liaison Major Coote that the fleet was ready. [cite:01] This signal was supposed to trigger the simultaneous land assault.

**British approach:** The squadron rounded Cumberland Head close-hauled "in line abreast, with the large ships to the north initially in the order Chubb, Linnet, Confiance and Finch, and the gunboats to the south." [cite:01] The wind died as they came around the headland, hampering maneuverability. [cite:01]

**Opening broadside:** *Confiance* positions herself approximately 300–500 yards from *Saratoga* and fires. The broadside "killed or wounded one fifth of Saratoga's crew." [cite:02]

**Downie's death:** Within approximately 15 minutes of the battle's opening, Downie is killed by the dismounted 24-pounder. [cite:04]

**The midphase:** The struggle continues for approximately 2 hours and 5 minutes total. *Confiance* receives an estimated 105–300 hits. A shot "smashing a gaping 7-foot hole in her hull below the waterline" contributes to the decision to surrender. [cite:02]

**HMS *Chubb*:** Shot away from control, drifted west through the American line; taken by a boarding party. [cite:01]

**HMS *Finch*:** After taking damage, grounded on Crab Island (the American hospital island) near the southern end of the bay; captured. [cite:01]

**HMS *Linnet*:** Engaged USS *Eagle* and inflicted heavy damage. After *Confiance* surrendered, *Linnet* continued firing alone for a time before striking her colors. [cite:05a]

**Macdonough's winding:** As described above; decisive moment of the battle. [cite:06]

**Surrender sequence:** *Chubb* captured early; *Finch* grounded; *Confiance* strikes colors (approximately noon, or "by noon" per Wikipedia); *Linnet* strikes colors shortly after. [cite:01]

**Macdonough's dispatch:** Sent to Secretary of the Navy William Jones: "The Almighty has been pleased to Grant us a signal victory on Lake Champlain in the capture of one Frigate, one Brig, and two sloops of war of the enemy." [cite:07] Jones responded: "To view it in abstract, it is not surpassed by any naval victory on record." [cite:07]

**Sound of the battle from shore:** The cannonade was audible from positions along the Saranac. William Apess (Pequot, served with American forces) recalled: "The enemy...commenced a cannonade, which we returned without much ceremony...for six days and nights we did not leave our guns." [cite:11]

## 9. British Land Force: Brigades, Regiments, the Saranac Crossings

**Commander:** Lieutenant General Sir George Prevost, Governor-General and Commander-in-Chief of British North America.

**Total force:** approximately 10,300–11,000 men, organized in three brigades plus artillery, cavalry, and Canadian units. [cite:01]

**Brigade composition:**

*Robinson's Brigade (1st Brigade)* — Major General Frederick Philipse Robinson:
- 3/27th Regiment of Foot (3rd Battalion, 27th Inniskilling)
- 39th Regiment of Foot (Dorsetshire) — *Note: a company of the 39th also served as marines aboard Confiance*
- 76th Regiment of Foot
- 88th Regiment of Foot (Connaught Rangers) — Peninsular War veterans [cite:01]

*Brisbane's Brigade (2nd Brigade)* — Major General Thomas Brisbane:
- 2/8th Regiment of Foot (King's) — weakened by Walcheren Campaign sickness
- 13th Regiment of Foot
- 49th Regiment of Foot (Hertfordshire)
- Regiment de Meuron (Swiss mercenary regiment, mixed nationalities)
- Canadian Voltigeurs
- Canadian Chasseurs [cite:01]

*Power's Brigade (3rd Brigade)* — Major General Manley Power:
- 3rd Regiment of Foot (East Kent, The Buffs)
- 5th Regiment of Foot (Northumberland)
- 1/27th Regiment of Foot (1st Bn., 27th Inniskilling) — had been stationed in Malta, limited recent action
- 58th Regiment of Foot (Rutlandshire) [cite:01]

**The Saranac crossing plan:**
- Brisbane's brigade: feint attacks at the Lower Bridge and Upper Bridge in Plattsburgh [cite:01]
- Robinson's brigade: main flanking assault, crossing Pike's Ford ~3 miles upstream [cite:01]
- Power's brigade: reserve/support role [cite:01]

**Execution on September 11:** Brisbane's bridge attacks were "easily repulsed." Robinson's brigade successfully crossed the Saranac at Pike's Ford, led by "eight companies of light infantry," and "soon drove the defenders back." [cite:01] At this point, orders arrived from Prevost to halt the attack. [cite:01]

**Prevost's delay:** Land assault orders not issued until ~10:00 AM, over an hour after the naval battle began. [cite:01] The court martial record later found this coordination failure was a contributing factor in the defeat.

**The retreat controversy:** Prevost justified withdrawal by arguing the army could not remain in American territory without naval supremacy on the lake for supply and communication. His brigade commanders — Brisbane, Robinson, Power, all Peninsular veterans — were "appalled" and believed victory was within reach when the order came to halt. [cite:12] Wellington, hearing of the retreat, was scathing. Prevost requested a court martial to clear his name; it was scheduled for January 12, 1816, postponed to February 5. Prevost died of dropsy January 5, 1816, one month before the hearing. [cite:12]

**Desertion:** 234 soldiers deserted during the retreat to Canada. Almost none were from the Peninsular War veteran regiments; most were from the Regiment de Meuron, 2/8th, and 1/27th. [cite:01]

## 10. American Land Force: Regulars, Militia, Beekmantown

**Commander:** Brigadier General Alexander Macomb.

**Regular troops:** approximately 1,500, mostly recruits and detachments. [cite:01] Units confirmed present or mentioned in primary and secondary sources:
- 29th U.S. Infantry: Major John E. Wool commanding; conducted Beekmantown delaying action with ~250 men [cite:10]
- 15th U.S. Infantry: Captain Ambrose McGlassin, led the night raid on September 9 [cite:11]
- 6th U.S. Infantry: Present (mentioned in regimental lineage and battle records) [cite:01]

**New York militia (Clinton County levy):** Major General Benjamin Mooers commanded. His jurisdiction included Franklin, Clinton, Essex, Warren, Washington, Rensselaer, and Columbia counties — a broad band of the northern New York frontier. [cite:08] The Clinton County component included men from Champlain Valley towns such as Plattsburgh, Beekmantown, Chazy, and Mooers (the last named for the general). Mooers held 49,400 acres in the region himself. [cite:08] By the time of the battle, approximately 2,000–2,500 New York militia were present. [cite:01]

**Vermont militia:** Major General Samuel Strong commanded approximately 2,000–2,500 Vermont volunteers who traveled to Burlington and then across to Plattsburgh. [cite:20] Strong was chosen unanimously by the Vermonters who had volunteered.

**Beekmantown delaying action, September 6:**
- First contact: New York State Militia and the British right wing, in Beekmantown; militia "withdrew in great disarray" [cite:10]
- Culver Hill: Wool's regulars rally some militia; "short but heated fight" [cite:10]
- Halsey's Corners: Two 6-pound field guns under Captain Leonard; three rounds fired, then Americans forced back [cite:10]
- Americans cross Saranac; British enter north Plattsburgh [cite:10]

**The south bank defense:** Fort Brown overlooked the Saranac from the south. Clear-cutting had opened fields of fire. The American left flank (toward Pike's Ford) was held by militia under Mooers. [cite:11]

## 11. HMS *Achille* at Trafalgar, 1805

*Note to drafter: The brief spells the ship "Achilles" with a final 's'. The ship's actual Royal Navy name was HMS Achille (French spelling, no final 's'), reflecting her capture from the French Navy. Sources are consistent on "Achille."*

**Ship class and armament:** 74-gun third-rate ship of the line, Pompée class. Main battery: 28 × 32-pounders on the gundeck. [cite:03]

**Captain at Trafalgar:** Richard King. [cite:03]

**Position in Collingwood's lee column:** HMS *Achille* was **seventh in Admiral Collingwood's lee (windward) column** — one of the two British columns that broke the Franco-Spanish line on October 21, 1805. She was positioned between HMS *Colossus* and HMS *Revenge*. [cite:03]

**Time of engagement:** *Achille* opened fire on the rear of the combined Franco-Spanish fleet at 12:15 PM. [cite:03]

**Sequence of engagements:**
1. 74-gun Spanish *Montañés*: engaged for approximately 15 minutes [cite:03]
2. 80-gun Spanish *Argonauta*: engaged; Argonauta eventually fell silent and closed her ports, though she could not immediately be accepted in surrender [cite:03]
3. 74-gun French *Achille* (namesake ship): brief exchange of broadsides [cite:03]
4. 74-gun French *Berwick*: ~75-minute engagement alongside; Berwick surrendered with "over 250 casualties" [cite:03]

**British casualties:** 13 killed, 59 wounded. [cite:03]

**Lower-deck experience at Trafalgar — general context:** A first-person lower-deck account from the same battle survives: William Thorpe, seaman aboard HMS *Minotaur*, wrote an account now held at Royal Museums Greenwich. [cite:21] Samuel Leech, author of *Thirty Years from Home* (1844), served in the Navy of this period and provides texture on lower-deck life. Robert Hay's memoir (written 1820–21, covering his service 1803–1811) similarly documents lower-deck conditions during the same era. [cite:22]

**Post-Trafalgar service:** HMS *Achille* continued active duty until decommissioned at Chatham Dockyard in 1815, then laid up at Sheerness until sold for breaking in 1865. [cite:03]

**For the fiction:** A seaman serving on *Achille* at Trafalgar (as Thomas Briggs did) would have entered Collingwood's column in the seventh position, firing on the enemy fleet from approximately 12:15 PM until well into the afternoon. The battle lasted from the first shots (~11:50 AM) until early evening. Casualties were moderate by the standards of the day (13 killed, 59 wounded out of a crew of ~600). The noise of that battle — and the smell, the smoke, the gun-deck atmosphere — would have been a template against which Thomas measures every subsequent action.

## 12. Royal Navy Gunnery: Gun Crew Procedure and Period Vocabulary

**Authority:** The following is drawn from a Navy and Marine Living History Association reconstruction of the circa-1800 Royal Navy gun drill, itself based on period regulations and contemporary sources. [cite:23]

### Crew Composition

For a **24-pounder long gun** (the main battery of *Confiance*):
- 12 men + 1 powder boy
  - No. 1: **Gun Captain** — directs the crew; lays and fires the gun; retreats to the full extent of the trigger lanyard and sights down the barrel [cite:23]
  - No. 2: **Second Captain** — inserts the quoin (elevation wedge), makes final elevation adjustments [cite:23]
  - Nos. 3 & 5: **Loaders** (first and second) — manhandle the shot; insert cartridge and shot; ram [cite:23]
  - Nos. 4 & 6: **Spongers** (first and second) — sponge the barrel after each round; operate the rammer [cite:23]
  - Nos. 7 & 8: **Side tacklemen** — haul the gun out through the port on the "run out" command [cite:23]
  - Nos. 9 & 10: **Train tacklemen** with crowbars — control lateral aim, handle training tackles [cite:23]
  - Nos. 11 & 12: **Handspikemen** — work handspikes (iron levers) to traverse the gun carriage [cite:23]
  - **Powder boy (cartridge carrier)** — carries the powder charge in a passing box from the magazine lobby [cite:23]

For a **12-pounder**: 8 men + 1 boy. For a **6-pounder**: 6 men + 1 boy. [cite:23]

### Gunnery Commands and Actions (Sequence)

1. **"Silence! Man the Starboard [or Port] guns!"** — crew moves to stations [cite:23]
2. **"Cast Loose and Provide!"** — clear the gun for action: open port, unhook breeching rope, remove tompion from muzzle, hook tackle to training bolts, uncover lock, remove quoin covers; powder boy retrieves passing box and stands ready [cite:23]
3. **"Sponge your guns!"** — first sponger rams wet sponge (fleece on a staff) down the bore to the breech; Gun Captain covers the touchhole with his thumb to create a vacuum that smothers any residual sparks from the previous round [cite:23]
4. **"Load cartridge!"** — powder boy passes charge from his passing box; first loader inserts cartridge bag into the muzzle [cite:23]
5. **"Ram cartridge!"** — first sponger/rammer drives the charge home [cite:23]
6. **"Load round!"** — shot-and-wad man passes the iron ball; first loader inserts ball and wad [cite:23]
7. **"Ram round!"** — rammer seats ball and wad against the charge [cite:23]
8. **"Run out!"** — side tacklemen haul the gun forward until the muzzle protrudes from the port; Gun Captain inserts a priming wire through the touchhole to pierce the cartridge bag, then primes with a friction primer or powder from a horn [cite:23]
9. **"Fire as your guns bear!"** — Gun Captain sights, waits for the ship's roll to bring the target into the line of sight, pulls the lanyard. [cite:23]

### Period Terminology at the Guns

- **Tompion:** the muzzle plug, removed before loading [cite:23]
- **Quoin:** a wooden wedge inserted under the breech to elevate or depress the gun; a second captain's responsibility [cite:23]
- **Breeching rope:** a heavy rope securing the gun carriage to the ship's side, absorbing recoil [cite:23]
- **Passing box (cartridge box):** the lidded box carried by the powder boy; protects the powder charge from sparks [cite:23]
- **Priming wire:** a metal rod inserted through the touchhole to pierce the cloth or paper powder cartridge [cite:23]
- **Touchhole (vent):** the small hole in the breech through which the priming powder ignites the charge [cite:23]
- **Linstock:** a staff holding a slow-burning match; used to fire the gun when no lock was available; relevant to *Confiance*, whose gunlocks were poorly fitted [cite:02]
- **Handspike:** an iron lever used to traverse (swing left or right) the gun carriage [cite:23]
- **Training tackle:** rope and pulley system for lateral aim [cite:23]
- **Side tackle:** the pulley system used to run the gun out after loading [cite:23]

### Carronade vs. Long Gun
**Inference (not in sources):** The carronade required a smaller crew (often 4–6 men) and had a simpler loading procedure, but its crew positions were similar in function to those at a long gun. The firing commands were the same.

### British vs. American Gunnery Terminology
The Navy and Marine Living History Association source notes that "U.S. ships of the period were very similar in 'rate' to the British vessels," implying largely standardized procedures. [cite:23] **No source consulted documents a specific vocabulary difference** between British and American gunnery commands in this period. The American practice on Lake Champlain would have been conducted under similar commands to those in the sequence above.

## 13. Royal Navy Life: Watches, Daily Routine, Lower-Deck Culture

### The Watch System

The crew of a Royal Navy ship was divided into two watches: the **larboard watch** and the **starboard watch**. The ship's day was divided into seven periods: [cite:24]

| Watch | Hours |
|---|---|
| First Watch | 8:00 PM – midnight |
| Middle Watch | midnight – 4:00 AM |
| Morning Watch | 4:00 AM – 8:00 AM |
| Forenoon Watch | 8:00 AM – noon |
| Afternoon Watch | noon – 4:00 PM |
| First Dog Watch | 4:00 PM – 6:00 PM (2 hours) |
| Last Dog Watch | 6:00 PM – 8:00 PM (2 hours) |

**The dog watches:** Two-hour watches creating an odd number of periods per day so that each man's schedule rotates — what was the First Watch one day becomes the Middle Watch the next. Also permitted the crew to eat evening meals. [cite:24]

**Bell system:** The ship's bell rang every 30 minutes. Eight bells = end of watch. At one bell, the first half-hour of the watch had passed. Time measured by sand glass. [cite:24]

**Three-watch system:** Sometimes used on larger ships, providing more men on deck and allowing 8 hours off-watch instead of 4. Less common on smaller vessels. [cite:24]

### Lower-Deck Life and Culture

**Messing:** Sailors ate and slept in messes (small groups of 6–8 men) between the guns on the lower deck. "Messmate" was the term for a companion in one's mess. [cite:22]

**Lower deck as social space:** Robert Hay's memoir (1803–1811 service) describes the lower deck as "pretty much crowded day and night," with an informal economy of goods, including contraband liquor. The space functioned simultaneously as sleeping quarters, eating space, and informal marketplace. [cite:22]

**Liberty:** Shore leave; relatively short periods permitted at anchor or in harbour. Prized and sometimes withheld as discipline. [cite:22]

**Pressed vs. volunteered:** Recent research suggests that at most 30% of the Royal Navy in the 1800–1815 period were impressed (press-ganged), contra earlier estimates of 50–80%. Men pressed from inbound merchant ships were preferred because of existing seafaring skill. Volunteers received a signing bonus and two months' advance pay. Deserting volunteers faced execution if caught; pressed men returned to service. [cite:22]

**Pay:** An able seaman earned £1 13s. 6d. per month in 1815. [cite:24]

**Able seaman vs. ordinary seaman:** Rated able seamen were the skilled core of a ship's working crew. The rate signified competency in the full range of seamanship tasks (taking in, making, and reefing sail in any weather; operating ground tackle; and serving at the great guns). Ordinary seaman was the grade below. [cite:22]

**Daily gun drill (peacetime or shakedown):** Gun crews exercised by "running" the guns in and out, performing all loading steps but not actually firing — important since live-fire practice was expensive and damaging to rigging. A ship being rushed into commission would have minimal time even for these exercises. [cite:23]

## 14. Saranac River Sawmill Operations circa 1814

### The Technology: Sash (Up-Down) Sawmill

In 1814, virtually all water-powered sawmills in New York and New England used the **sash sawmill** (also called the "up-down" or "sash-frame" mill). The circular saw and bandsaw did not replace this technology until the mid-to-late 19th century. [cite:25]

**Core mechanism:**
- A straight steel blade, approximately 6 feet long, is mounted vertically in a rectangular wooden **sash** (frame)
- The sash slides up and down on **wooden posts** (slides)
- A **pitman arm** (a wooden connecting rod) links the sash to a **crank** on the waterwheel's shaft
- Water power rotates the wheel; wheel rotation drives the crank; crank drives the pitman; pitman pushes and pulls the sash — thus the saw moves up and down [cite:25]
- The saw **cuts on the downstroke**; on the upstroke, the **log carriage** (a wheeled wooden platform) advances the log a small distance toward the blade [cite:25]

**Log carriage and log dogs:**
- The log rests on the **log carriage**, held in place by **log dogs** — iron staples driven into the log and locked to the carriage
- The carriage advances automatically by a ratchet mechanism (or, in simpler mills, by manual adjustment)
- The mill stops automatically ~3 inches before the cut is complete; the sawyer manually backs the carriage and repositions for the next cut [cite:25]

**Power source:** An **overshot, undershot, or flutter-wheel turbine** waterwheel. On the Saranac, which drops steeply in its lower miles (a significant portion of its total elevation drop occurring in the last third of its length), even a modest stream could power a mill reliably. [cite:25]

### Period Performance Metrics

Thomas Ellicott (1795): "One mill, attended by one good man, if in good order, will saw more than 20 men with whip-saws." [cite:26] Operators were satisfied with approximately **1,000 board feet per day** as a target output. [cite:25]

### Worker Roles and Vocabulary

- **Millwright:** Designs and assembles the mill's wooden framing; coordinates with the blacksmith for the "**sawmill irons**" (the metal components: the millsaw blade, iron crank, log dogs). A millwright apprentice would be learning timber framing, waterwheel construction, and mechanical adjustment. [cite:25]
- **Sawyer (mill operator):** The skilled worker who actually runs the cutting operation; manages log positioning, carriage advance, and saw tension. Often the same person as the mill owner on a small rural mill. [cite:25]

**The "sawmill irons":** the steel saw blade, iron crank, and log dogs — the only metal parts in an otherwise wood-built machine. These required a blacksmith and were obtained from a manufacturer rather than made on-site. [cite:25]

### Sensory Detail

The machine produces a characteristic rhythm: **the saw rasps and rips on the downstroke, the building vibrates with the reciprocating motion**, the millpond carries the sound across still water. The pitman arm's motion is visible as a pendulum-like oscillation connecting waterwheel to sash. [cite:26]

### Saranac River Sawmills in the Historical Record

The first documented sawmill on the Saranac near Plattsburgh dates to 1768 (Count de Fredenburg's mill, ~3 miles upriver). Charles Platt's 1785 settlement party built sawmills along the Saranac as one of their first industrial acts. By 1814, the Saranac was the economic spine of the Champlain Valley timber trade. A sawmill "three miles upstream" from Plattsburgh village would have been in or near the vicinity of these early mills. [cite:17]

## 15. American Infantry Drill circa 1814

**Status of regulations in September 1814:** Winfield Scott's formal codification of American infantry tactics was not completed until December 1814 (officially adopted February 28, 1815). At Plattsburgh in September 1814, American regulars trained under an earlier transitional system derived largely from French drill. [cite:27]

**Scott's contribution before the battle:** Beginning April 1814, Scott ran an intensive camp of instruction at Buffalo, where he drilled two brigades of regular infantry. These same trained troops performed superbly at the Battle of Chippawa (July 5, 1814). The officers and sergeants trained at Buffalo would have served as models and instructors for other regular units. [cite:27]

**Two-rank firing:** The standard American (and British) infantry line formation was a line two ranks deep. Firing by platoon, company, or section rotated the fire — one rank firing while the other reloaded. The commands were: **"Make ready" → "Present" → "Fire" → "Prime and load."** [cite:27]

**For the fiction:** Asa Beckwith is militia, not a regular. Regular sergeants training militia to "load standing in two ranks" would have been teaching the most basic firing drill: stand your ground, load from the cartridge box, bring the musket to "present" and fire on command. This was the minimum competency needed to hold a ford against an infantry assault.

## 16. Prisoners of War: Royal Navy After Surrender

**The parole system:** Officers and non-commissioned officers captured at sea or on the lakes were admitted to parole — a signed promise not to escape. Paroled officers received a subsistence of three shillings sterling per day; other paroled prisoners received one shilling sixpence. Paroled prisoners were assigned to designated inland towns. [cite:28]

**Treatment of the Lake Champlain prisoners:** "Commander Pring and the other surviving British officers later testified that Macdonough showed every consideration to the British wounded and prisoners." [cite:01]

**The prize tenders:** American vessels took *Confiance*, *Linnet*, *Chubb*, and *Finch* as prizes. *Confiance* was taken to Whitehall, New York — the southern terminus of Lake Champlain's navigable waters — and used as Macdonough's winter headquarters. [cite:02]

**Inland routing:** British naval prisoners from Lake Champlain would have been routed south through New York State to inland parole towns or prisoner-of-war depots. **No primary source was located in this research** specifying which parole towns received the Lake Champlain prisoners. The general War of 1812 parole system placed British prisoners in inland American towns at some distance from the coast. For Thomas's day-after chapter, the specific town of final destination need not be named — the procedural reality is: prize tender, south down the lake, transit by road to an inland parole location.

## 17. Casualties, Burials, and the Immediate Aftermath (September 12)

**Total casualties, September 11:**
- American: 220 total (104 killed, 116 wounded) [cite:01]
- British: 939 total (168 killed, 220 wounded, 317 captured, 234 deserted during retreat) [cite:01]
- *Confiance* alone: 41 killed, 83 wounded [cite:02]
- *Linnet*: 10 killed, 15 wounded [cite:05a]
- *Chubb*: ~6 killed, ~16 wounded [cite:05a]

**Medical conditions before the battle:** Dr. James Mann documented that over 500 sick American soldiers were moved to Crab Island (a hospital island at the south end of the bay) before the battle. [cite:11]

**Burials:** Simeon Doty's first-hand account of post-battle burial on Crab Island: "We went to Crab Island...The dead were carried off southward and were buried in trenches without coffins." American and British dead were interred together. [cite:11] Downie was buried at Riverside Cemetery, Plattsburgh. [cite:04]

**More than 30 amputations** were performed by American surgeons following the battle. [cite:11]

**British retreat, September 12:** The army began its withdrawal north toward the Canadian border. The retreat was marked by disarray and 234 desertions. The army that had advanced in close order now retreated in disorder, with the regimental commanders of the Peninsular veterans making their displeasure with Prevost plain. [cite:01]

**Prize procedures:** American prize crews would have been placed aboard *Confiance*, *Linnet*, *Chubb*, and *Finch* immediately after the surrender. British wounded remaining aboard would have been tended by American surgeons. British prisoners (wounded and unwounded) would have begun the process of parole or inland routing within days. [cite:02]

## 18. Race and the Period: Black and Indigenous Presence

### Black Sailors in the US Navy

The official ban on Black naval service (August 1798, by Secretary of the Navy Benjamin Stoddert) was **reversed March 3, 1813**, allowing "persons of color" to serve on "public vessels" of the United States. Despite the 1798 ban, Black sailors had maintained a consistent presence throughout the early Republic period. [cite:29]

**Estimated wartime proportion:** Approximately **15–20% of the US Navy's force during the War of 1812 was made up of Black sailors** — representing roughly one-sixth of naval personnel in the conflict. At the Battle of Lake Erie (Perry's command, 1813), approximately 25% of sailors were Black. [cite:29]

**Black soldiers at Plattsburgh:** Free Black soldiers were organized into **labor parties** by General Izard to construct Forts Brown, Moreau, and Scott on the south bank of the Saranac. Evidence documents that some regular troops refused to work alongside these men, revealing racial tensions within the army. [cite:11]

**Indigenous soldiers:** **William Apess**, a Pequot, served with American forces and fought at Plattsburgh. His autobiography provides one of the few surviving first-person Indigenous accounts of the battle. [cite:11]

### Black Sailors in the Royal Navy

**Inference (not in sources):** The Royal Navy of the period was multi-national and multi-racial by practical necessity. Black sailors served in the Royal Navy throughout the Napoleonic era, and their presence was common if not officially counted. No source specifically documents the racial composition of the Lake Champlain British squadron. **No primary source for this specific theater was located in this research.** The brief notes Black and Indigenous people should not be invisible — this is consistent with documented Black presence in the broader Royal Navy, but specific figures for Lake Champlain are unknown.

### Champlain Valley Demographics

**Inference (not in sources):** The Champlain Valley in 1814 was frontier-transitional. Free Black families had settled in the region. The Indigenous nations who had occupied the valley — particularly the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy) and Abenaki — had been largely displaced by the 1810s but were not absent.
