# Style Guide — *Two Shores*

## Point of View and Tense

Third-person limited, past tense. The narrator is bound tightly to whichever character owns the chapter; no information reaches the prose that the POV character could not observe, know, or plausibly infer. The POV does not shift within a chapter. In action: the character perceives only what is in front of him and within earshot — no omniscient overview of the battle.

---

## Two Registers, One Architecture

The single most important stylistic fact about this book: Thomas Briggs and Asa Beckwith have distinct prose registers, and the difference must be felt in the first paragraph of any chapter — without burlesquing either voice. The narrative voice is not a neutral camera; it is colored throughout by the POV character's vocabulary, rhythm, and habits of attention.

**Thomas's register** is spare, technical, the prose of a man in his eleventh year of Royal Navy service. Naval vocabulary enters in stride — no italics, no explanatory appositions, no glossing on first use. The forenoon watch, the larboard bow, the breeching rope, the quoin, the handspike: used as a working seaman uses them, as names for real things. His interior life surfaces not as declared emotion but as observation and omission — he fears the ship's unreadiness; this never becomes a sentence that says so. When he is afraid he notes the wind. His Englishness is in word choice (larboard not port; making sail not hoisting sail; "he should think" rather than "he thought") and in the deliberate weight of short declarative sentences, not in phonetic dialect. Primary exemplars: Shaara's restrained third-person interiority; O'Brian's trust-the-reader naval texture.

**Asa's register** is closer to the surface — the nineteen-year-old consciousness is less armored. He thinks in the river: flow, fall, shoals, the reading of water in motion. His images are drawn from the sawmill and the Saranac; his syntax is more paratactic, delivering the world in sequence rather than in subordinated analysis. Sentence lengths are shorter than Thomas's on average. His Americanness is in idiom and tempo — the Champlain Valley vernacular of 1814, not 2024 colloquial. The sound of the naval cannonade from the bay enters his chapters as weather: a rolling percussion coming from no direction, not a narrative signal. Primary exemplar: Frazier's landscape-as-vocabulary register.

---

## Sentence Rhythm

- **Thomas's chapters:** average 14–20 words. Weight arrives at the end of the sentence. Subordination used sparingly and purposefully — a long clause earns its place. Three or four short declaratives can be laid in sequence to build tension or reproduce the rhythms of naval procedure.
- **Asa's chapters:** average 10–16 words. More paratactic. Shorter sentences more frequent. The world delivered in fast sequence rather than built up through syntax.
- **Maximum before a warning:** 35 words. Sentences exceeding this should be audited for whether the length earns its keep.
- **Action chapters (September 11):** sentence lengths shorten further. Details — specific objects, specific sounds, specific directions of force — become the unit of perception. No time for subordinate clauses.

---

## Period Vocabulary

Period vocabulary is rendered in stride without italics and without explanatory tags on first use. The reader is trusted to absorb the context. This applies to:
- Royal Navy terms: watches, gun crew commands and positions, sail and rigging nomenclature, lower-deck social structure
- American military terms: two-rank infantry drill commands, fort and earthwork vocabulary
- Sawmill vocabulary: sash, pitman arm, log carriage, log dogs, millpond
- Period American and British idiom: the brief's constraint is that differences show in idiom and rhythm, not in phonetic spelling

When a technical term is used in a scene, the prose should make its function evident through context — through what the character does with it or around it — not through a parenthetical definition.

## What the Prose Does Not Do

**No fate-of-the-nation narration.** Neither character knows what the day will mean historically. The prose does not know either. No sentence in the book carries historical retrospect. The significance of the battle to the Ghent negotiations, to British invasion plans, to the shape of the eventual treaty — none of this enters the prose, directly or by implication.

**No jingoism in either direction.** Thomas is not a villain because he is British. Asa is not a hero because he is American. Each chapter sees only what its POV character sees. Where the historical record is contested — Prevost's retreat, *Confiance*'s readiness — the prose renders what the character observes and leaves the contest in the silence.

**No invention beyond the brief's constraints.** No intelligence subplot, no spy narrative, no secret messages. The war is in the ships at anchor, the regiments on the road, the militia on the river.

**The day-before chapters observe without foreshadowing.** The reader knows the book is set before a battle; the prose does not remind them. No ominous skies, no loaded final glances, no italicized silences that signal the storm coming.

**The day-after chapters observe without philosophizing.** What has changed is visible; the prose shows it without weighing it. Thomas's captivity on the prize tender is procedural reality, not tragic theme. Asa on the south bank of the Saranac has the river and the visible road to the north. The prose handles this with the same restraint as the rest of the book.

---

## Historical Figures

Named historical officers appear from below. They are glimpsed across a deck or a tree line, heard giving an order, seen in the consequence of their decisions. They are never interior. This is not the story of Downie or Macdonough or Prevost; it is the story of what it felt like to receive their commands.

---

## Dialogue

Restrained. Period in idiom. Neither character speaks at length about internal states. Dialogue tends to be operational: tasks, observations, orders, requests. Free indirect discourse is the preferred instrument for rendering interior reaction without explicit thought-tags. Dialogue between characters should carry the register difference between Thomas's economical formality and Asa's more immediate vernacular.

---

## Failure Modes

The AI-author patterns most tempting in this story's register. The drafter should actively avoid these; the compression pass should cut them.

- **Thesis restatement** — the prose must not tell the reader what the battle meant, what the war meant, what each man's experience means against the sweep of history. Any sentence that sounds like the opening of a Wikipedia article is this failure mode. The brief is explicit: "No fate of the nation narration." Watch for sentences beginning with "This was," "It would later be," "Neither could then have known," or any clause that steps outside the character's moment into retrospective evaluation.

- **Post-scene subtext naming** — after a charged scene (Thomas at the gun, Asa at the ford, the surrender, the retreat road), the prose must not step back and explain what the scene was "really" about. The day-after chapters in particular are vulnerable: the brief explicitly forbids eulogizing or philosophizing. The prose observes; it does not explain what the reader should feel.

- **Sentence-after-the-sentence** — an image lands (the gun spinning off its carriage, the ford in morning light, smoke moving across the water), and the next sentence explains what the reader just felt. Cut the second sentence. The image must do the work alone.

- **Emotional structural analysis** — Thomas does not explain his suppressed fear; he notes the wind. Asa does not explain that his caution is the reasonable assessment of a nineteen-year-old who knows he can be killed; he watches the tree line. A sentence such as "He feared not the battle itself but what the battle would mean for men who were not ready" is the failure mode. The analysis belongs to the reader, not the prose.

- **Meta-commentary on cognition** — "He noted this." "It occurred to him that—" "She registered the fact." These are labels for thinking the prose already shows. If the observation is present, the label is redundant. If the observation is not present, adding a label does not supply it.

---

## Lean-Into Patterns

Patterns the drafter and editors should **not** treat as failures, because this register depends on them.

- **Free indirect discourse for interiority** — the character's own vocabulary and rhythm suffusing the narration without explicit "he thought" or "he felt" tags. For Thomas especially, this is where his voice lives. His unspoken assessments of *Confiance*'s readiness, his memory of *Achilles* at Trafalgar, his thoughts of Elizabeth: these arrive through the prose's texture, not through declared cognition.

- **Period vocabulary without gloss** — using "the forenoon watch," "the larboard gun," "the sash," "the pitman arm," "the quoin" as if the reader already knows them. This is not showing off; it is what Shaara and O'Brian do, and it creates the world. If a reader can absorb context without a definition, the prose should trust that.

- **Sensory and landscape as emotional register** — the state of the wind and sea as Thomas's unspoken gauge of the situation; the river as Asa's primary instrument of understanding. These are not metaphors bolted on — they are the characters' native languages and must be treated as such throughout.

- **Restrained accumulation** — both characters' chapters build meaning through the accumulation of precise observed detail, not through the declaration of meaning. Trust the cumulative weight of specific things.
