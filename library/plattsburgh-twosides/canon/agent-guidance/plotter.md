# Plotter Guidance — *Two Shores*

## POV Count and Rotation

Two POV characters only: Thomas Briggs (Royal Navy) and Asa Beckwith (American militia). **Strict chapter alternation throughout all 16 chapters.** The plotter chooses which POV opens the book; once chosen, the alternation is non-negotiable — no two consecutive chapters may share a POV. No ensemble, no third POV, no chapter in which both characters appear.

## Chapter-Shape Preferences

Each character's eight chapters are typed by the brief. The plotter's primary structural task is to assign specific events and beats to specific chapters within these types:

**Day-before chapters (1 per character):** Single-scene or slow-accumulation. These are quiet, observational, present-tense-of-the-world chapters. Thomas: aboard *Confiance* during fitting-out at Île aux Noix or the southward passage — a specific watch, a specific set of duties, the ship as she is in her unreadiness. Asa: a working day at the Saranac sawmill, before the muster call. Both chapters should feel complete in themselves — not obviously preamble, not obviously weighted. **They do not foreshadow.**

**Approach-and-standoff chapters (5 per character):** These carry the bulk of narrative time, from roughly September 3 to the early hours of September 11. The characters move toward the battle: *Confiance* sailing south and anchoring at Cumberland Head; the British army arriving at Beekmantown (September 6) and establishing the Saranac standoff (September 7–10); Asa mustered, posted, drilled, waiting. These chapters may elide time within their boundaries ("that evening" / "three days passed in the same way"). They build from the ordinary toward the tightening — but resist manufactured tension. Action is permitted within these chapters (Beekmantown, the September 9 night raid by McGlassin's company, early British probing of the Saranac line). The approach chapters should vary in shape: some single-scene, some intercut with forward-time movement, some built around a specific incident.

**Climactic action chapters (1 per character):** September 11, 1814. Thomas's chapter covers the naval engagement from approximately 8:00 AM to noon — Downie's death within the first fifteen minutes, the pounding of *Confiance*, the eventual surrender. Asa's chapter covers the British infantry attempt on Pike's Ford and what holds the line. Both are the longest chapters in the book (target 3,500–4,500 words). Both are tight, fast, observational — the character perceives only his own immediate field. The naval battle and the ford action are simultaneous; the only sensory crossover between threads is the sound of the cannonade audible from the Saranac.

**Day-after chapters (1 per character):** September 12. Quiet, procedural, observational. Thomas: aboard an American prize tender on Lake Champlain, the practical realities of captured men (parole, subsistence, the company of his shipmates). Asa: on the south bank of the Saranac, the British retreat road visible to the north. Neither chapter philosophizes. Both observe what has changed.

## Episode-vs-Arc Structure

The five approach-and-standoff chapters per character are **a continuous arc within their thread**, not episodic. Each chapter should build on the previous Thomas chapter (or the previous Asa chapter) — advancing the character through real time, not resetting between chapters. The threads do not connect at the character level; they connect at the temporal level, converging on September 11.

The day-before and day-after chapters are more self-contained — each is a bounded day, beginning and ending with the physical world.

## Ending-Mode Distribution

Target across 16 chapters:

- **5 resolved beats** — the day ends, a decision is made, a task is completed. Appropriate for day-before and day-after chapters, and for some of the quieter approach chapters.
- **6 mid-action or end-on-a-new-fact** — the chapter ends with the situation changed: a new order given, a new threat visible, the night before the battle. Appropriate for the middle approach-and-standoff chapters as the timeline tightens.
- **4 literary fades on image or observation** — the chapter ends on a specific sensory detail that carries the chapter's weight without stating it. Appropriate for the day-before and day-after chapters especially.
- **1 turn** — the evening of September 10 or the early hours of September 11, when the chapter ends on the fact that the morning will be different. This is the closest to a cliffhanger the book gets, and it should earn it through the accumulated weight of the approach, not through a manufactured reveal.

Avoid clustering the same ending mode in consecutive chapters of the same POV. Do not assign literary fades to both Thomas's and Asa's versions of the same narrative moment — vary the ending shapes even when the calendar position is the same.

## Chapter Target Words

- **Day-before chapters:** 1,800–2,400 words. Quiet and complete.
- **Approach-and-standoff chapters:** 2,000–3,200 words. Vary within this range; not all five should be the same length.
- **Climactic action chapters:** 3,500–4,500 words. The longest chapters in the book.
- **Day-after chapters:** 1,800–2,400 words. Quiet and complete.
- **Overall manuscript target:** ~40,000 words across 16 chapters.

## Genre-Specific Structural Conventions

**Literary historical dual-narrative with battle climax.** The structure the brief imposes is close to *The Killer Angels*: each chapter is one consciousness during a bounded period; both consciousnesses are moving toward the same day; the battle is the structural apex, not the conclusion. What distinguishes *Two Shores* from that model is the absence of interaction between the two POVs — Thomas and Asa share a day, not a space.

Structural musts:
- The day-before chapters must feel genuinely ordinary — not dramatized as "the last ordinary day." The ordinary registers as ordinary because the prose treats it as such.
- The climactic action chapters must be simultaneous — the plotter should mark their temporal overlap explicitly, so the drafter can use the cannonade as the acoustic thread.
- The day-after chapters close the frame. They are not epilogues; they are the final chapter-type, and they carry the same prose register as everything else. Do not ask the drafter to "land the theme" in these chapters; the brief is explicit that they observe, not philosophize.

**Additional structural consideration:** The British side and American side should receive roughly equal chapter weight in terms of the event density assigned to their approach-and-standoff phase. Beekmantown (September 6), the standoff days (September 7–10), and the British night raids supply material for Asa's approach; the southward passage of *Confiance*, the arrival off Cumberland Head, and the pre-battle night at anchor supply material for Thomas's approach. The plotter should not front-load one thread with drama and leave the other with routine — the symmetry of equal interiority is a structural requirement of the brief.
