# Reader Guidance — *Two Shores*

## Intended Audience

Adult readers of literary historical fiction — specifically readers who bring patience for period texture, can absorb unfamiliar vocabulary from context, and value restrained interiority over announced emotion. Comparable readers would come to this book from *The Killer Angels*, *Cold Mountain*, or *Master and Commander*. Not genre thriller readers; not readers who need propulsive hooks to continue. College-level reading grade. The audience will notice and value the formal symmetry of the two-POV alternation; they will also notice immediately if one voice drifts toward the other's register, or if the prose breaks its own discipline and editorializes.

---

## Brief-Adherence Checks to Perform

The Reader must answer each of these questions explicitly in the developmental review, in addition to general craft assessment:

1. **Is the register difference between Thomas and Asa audible in the first paragraph of every chapter?** Pull the opening paragraph of any chapter and ask: does the vocabulary, sentence rhythm, and quality of observation identify the POV character without the chapter header? If not, name the specific chapter and what drifted.

2. **Does the prose ever carry historical retrospect?** Flag any sentence that reads like it knows what September 11, 1814 will eventually mean — to the peace negotiations, to the British invasion plan, to history. The brief's prohibition is explicit and absolute. "No fate of the nation narration" means zero tolerance.

3. **Do the day-before chapters foreshadow?** Any sentence in the day-before chapters that signals the coming catastrophe — ominous weather, weighted pauses, italicized small moments — is a brief violation. Flag every instance.

4. **Do the day-after chapters philosophize?** Any sentence in the day-after chapters that eulogizes, weighs significance, or explains what the battle meant is a brief violation. Flag every instance.

5. **Do any named historical officers become POV?** Downie, Pring, Macdonough, Mooers, Macomb, Prevost, Brisbane — all must remain exterior: glimpsed, heard, seen in their effects, never interior. Flag any chapter section where the narrator reports what a historical officer thought, felt, or intended.

6. **Does either thread claim knowledge the character cannot have?** Thomas cannot know what is happening at Pike's Ford; Asa cannot know what is happening on *Confiance*. The only crossover is the sound of the cannonade. Flag any narration that steps outside the character's perceptual range.

7. **Is the jingoism constraint maintained?** Neither the British nor the American side should be framed as more righteous, more competent, or more sympathetically human in the prose. If Thomas's chapters feel like a story about British tragedy and Asa's feel like a story about American heroism — or vice versa — that is a failure. The balance must hold.

8. **Is the strict POV alternation maintained?** Count the chapters: Thomas and Asa must alternate without exception. Any chapter that breaks the alternation is a structural violation.

9. **Does the climactic action chapter for each POV stay within the character's field of perception?** Thomas sees the gun positions, the mast, the men around him, the deck; he does not see the whole battle. Asa sees the ford, the tree line, the men in his two companies; he does not see the whole engagement. Flag any section that becomes omniscient.

10. **Is period vocabulary used correctly and in stride?** Spot-check the naval vocabulary in Thomas's chapters and the sawmill/river vocabulary in Asa's chapters against the research dossier (`canon/research.md`) and glossary. Flag any anachronisms, misused terms, or terms explained with parenthetical definitions (which violate the "in stride" convention).

## What "Good" Looks Like for This Book

A good chapter in this book reads like a good Shaara chapter: one consciousness filtering the world through its specific vocabulary and history, the prose never stepping outside that consciousness to comment, the events landing with weight earned through specificity rather than through announcement. A reader finishing a chapter should feel they have inhabited a person, not followed a camera.

A good Thomas chapter smells like a lower deck and thinks in the practical assessments of an eleven-year naval veteran. His unspoken fear about *Confiance*'s readiness is present — it shapes every observation — but is never declared. The reader must carry it.

A good Asa chapter moves the way a young man moves when he is watching something he does not entirely understand — alert, quick to observe, the river's logic applied to an unfamiliar world. The cannonade arriving from the bay should feel like weather he has no name for.

A good day-before chapter is genuinely ordinary. It does not feel like a last meal before execution. It earns its place by making the reader care about the work — the mill, the watch — before the world interrupts.

A good September 11 chapter is dense, precise, and short in its sentences — and contains nothing the character could not see from where he stands.

## What to Flag Explicitly if the Manuscript Drifts

These are the drifts that must be named even when the individual prose is competent:

**Flag: Historical retrospect smuggled into charged scenes.** Any moment when the prose pauses on September 11 in a way that implies the author knows how important the day is. This is the most insidious drift because it often arrives as a beautiful sentence. It must be cut anyway.

**Flag: Register collapse between threads.** If Thomas's chapter 7 and Asa's chapter 8 read in the same prose rhythm — same sentence length, same quality of observation, same interior distance — the voices have collapsed. The formal requirement is that the two registers remain distinct throughout.

**Flag: Philosophizing in the final two chapters.** Any day-after chapter that begins to carry the weight of theme, meaning, or retrospect is violating the book's own aesthetic. The brief is explicit: "The prose handles this with the same restraint as the rest of the book."

**Flag: Invented plot mechanics.** Any intelligence plot, spy narrative, or secret-message thread is a categorical brief violation. Name it as such.

**Flag: Dialect performing.** Any moment where Thomas's Englishness or Asa's American frontier-ness is rendered through phonetic spelling or heavy dialect tags is a brief violation. "The difference in their speech is in idiom and rhythm, not in dialect spelling."

---

## What to Applaud

If the manuscript achieves these things, the Reader should specifically defend them in the "what to leave alone" section:

1. **The register distinction held throughout.** If a reader can identify the POV character from the prose texture alone — if the voices are genuinely two different consciousnesses and not one voice wearing different costumes — this is the book's central formal achievement and must be named and protected.

2. **The day-before chapters' genuine ordinariness.** If the mill and the fitting-out feel like real working days without the pressure of what the reader knows is coming, the drafter has done something difficult. Do not ask to "heighten" these chapters. Their refusal to heighten is the point.

3. **Precise technical vocabulary used without apology.** If the naval and sawmill terms land in stride and the world builds up through them without any authorial hand-holding, protect that. The instinct to italicize or gloss is always wrong for this book.

4. **Restraint in the day-after chapters.** If the final chapters observe without explaining, resist the editorial instinct to ask for more "emotional resolution." The restraint is the resolution.
