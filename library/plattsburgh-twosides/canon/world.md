# World — *Two Shores*

## The Champlain Valley, September 1814

First week of September. Nights already cold enough for a coat; days in the low sixties Fahrenheit. The hardwood ridges above the Saranac still mostly green but with the first maple color at the crowns. The lake flat at dawn and ruffled by afternoon. Smells of the valley: pine resin, lake mud, wood smoke, the iron tang of an artillery park.

The war is in its third year. The Atlantic seaboard has been raided from the sea; Washington burned in August. The northern front — the Champlain corridor — has been quiet through most of 1814 while Prevost's army gathered strength from the Peninsular veterans arriving after Napoleon's abdication. Now it moves. The British army crossing the border into New York is the largest, best-equipped force ever to invade from Canada. The lake is the supply line and the strategic hinge. Whoever controls it controls the campaign.

## The British World: Île aux Noix and H.M.S. *Confiance*

Île aux Noix is a fortified island in the Richelieu River, a few miles above where it drains out of the northern end of Lake Champlain. The Royal Navy established a shipyard there in 1812; it operates until 1834. In the summer of 1814 the yard is racing to complete *Confiance* — thirty-seven guns, the largest warship ever to be launched on this lake — on a deadline that cannot slip.

*Confiance* is fifth-rate frigate construction, laid down June 1814, launched in early August, sent south before she is finished. Her planks are green timber from the local forest. Her magazine was incomplete when she departed; some of her guns lack functioning gun locks; her crew were strangers to one another and to the ship the morning they boarded. Soldiers of the 39th Foot serve as her marines. Carpenters from the yard were still completing work aboard her as she sailed and were dropped off at Cumberland Head before the engagement.

Aboard ship, Thomas's world: the cramped lower deck, mess tables folded against the deckhead beams when not in use, hammock hooks in rows fourteen inches apart, the smell of lamp-black and salt pork and damp wool, the bell every half-hour marking the watch, the constant ritual of pumping bilges that will never be entirely dry in green timber. The ship is organized into larboard and starboard watches; each able seaman knows his gun station, his sail station, his fire-and-collision-quarters assignment. What he does not know — because no one does — is how the rest of his crew will perform in action.

The southward passage down Lake Champlain from Île aux Noix to Plattsburgh Bay: flat farm country on the west shore, the Green Mountains rising on the east, the lake narrowing and widening as it runs south. The ship still being fitted out under way. Drill run on an unfamiliar crew.

## The American World: The Saranac Valley

The Saranac River runs east out of the Adirondack foothills and enters Lake Champlain at Plattsburgh. Along its lower course, the land is mixed hardwood and pine with cleared fields and occasional mill sites. The river runs cold and fast in spring flood; by September the water is lower but still moving, the color of tea over limestone where it shallows over gravel bars.

Three miles upstream of the village, at a place where the river narrows between clay banks and there is a ford that adults can cross in September low water without wetting their chests, Asa's uncle runs a sash sawmill. The mill uses waterwheel power to drive a pitman arm connected to a reciprocating sash — a vertically moving frame that holds the millsaw blade. Logs come down the river from the upper valley, are caught in the millpond, are winched onto the log carriage, and are advanced by hand through the blade stroke by stroke. One good sawyer with a sash mill can cut more than a thousand board feet a day — more than twenty men with a pitsaw. The mill building vibrates in rhythm with the saw's stroke; the rasping carries across the millpond.

Plattsburgh village, at the river's mouth, is a small lake town — a courthouse, taverns, wharves, a landing for schooners out of Burlington. In late August and early September 1814 it has been remade into a fortified position. Fort Brown and earthwork companions sit on the bluffs above the south bank of the Saranac. The Lower Bridge in the village has been destroyed. The Upper Bridge, a mile and a half above the Lower, has been destroyed. Pike's Ford, three miles upstream from the Lower Bridge, is where the militia stands — two companies of Clinton County men and a regular sergeant running two-rank drill. That is where the British infantry will try to cross.

## Plattsburgh Bay

A roughly oval bay on the western shore of Lake Champlain, at the lake's northern third, bounded on the north and east by Cumberland Head. Roughly a mile and a half wide at its widest. The American squadron anchored in a north-to-south line approximately one thousand yards from the western shore: *Eagle* at the north end, then *Saratoga*, then *Ticonderoga*, then *Preble*, with gunboats in the intervals and on the flanks. The squadron lies at anchor with springs on the cables — lines run from the capstan back to the anchor cable, allowing the ship to be rotated at anchor, presenting a fresh broadside.

The British squadron enters the bay from the north, rounding Cumberland Head. They must beat in against a headwind. The cannonade, when it begins, is audible from the upper fords on the Saranac — a sustained, rolling percussion, directionless when the wind shifts, nothing like thunder.

## Technology Level

**Naval:** Long guns (iron shot, powder cartridge, flintlock or linstock ignition, rope breeching, wooden gun carriage), carronades (short-range, heavier shot), anchor and spring arrangement for positional fighting, kedge anchors for winding a ship at anchor. Signals by flag hoist. No steam. Paper dispatches by boat.

**Land:** Smoothbore flintlock muskets, two-rank infantry fire by company (Scott's French-derived drill: "Make ready — Present — Fire — Prime and load"). Wheeled artillery on the fixed defensive works. The Saranac River as the defensive line; stone walls and tree lines as the cover.

**Communication:** Dispatch riders, flag signals, the postal system for personal letters. Thomas's letter from Elizabeth took months to arrive; when he reads it the ink is faded along the folds.
