# Brief

## Premise

Lake Champlain, the first week of September 1814. Two lives — one on each side of the war — move toward the same Sunday morning from opposite shores of Plattsburgh Bay.

**Thomas Briggs**, thirty-two, is an able seaman on H.M.S. *Confiance* — Captain George Downie's thirty-seven-gun ship and the British flagship on Lake Champlain. Plymouth-born, he was at Trafalgar at twenty-three aboard the *Achilles*, served the Channel and the Mediterranean for ten years after that, and was reassigned to the Lake Champlain expedition in early summer 1814 — pulled from a ship of the line and sent inland to a flotilla being scratch-built in the timber yards of Île aux Noix. He has never said aloud what he thinks of *Confiance*'s readiness, but he thinks it constantly. He has a wife in Devonport he has not seen in three years and a son he has never seen, born after he sailed.

**Asa Beckwith**, nineteen, is a militiaman in Major-General Benjamin Mooers's Clinton County levy. A millwright's apprentice at his uncle's sawmill on the Saranac, three miles upstream of Plattsburgh village, he was called up the last week of August when Sir George Prevost's army crossed the border from Lower Canada. He knows the river — every ford, every bend, every place a man can or cannot cross in September water — better than most of the regulars now on the south bank. His father served at Sackett's Harbor and came home with a fever that did not leave. Asa has been the man of the house since he was fifteen.

The threads do not cross at the level of people. Thomas and Asa never meet, never see each other, never know the other exists. They cross at the level of the day: on Sunday, the eleventh of September, 1814, between half past eight and noon, Captain Downie's squadron engages Master Commandant Thomas Macdonough's at anchor in Plattsburgh Bay while Brigadier-General Brisbane's brigade attempts to force the Saranac at three points to break the American land line. The cannonade is audible from the upper fords. The British squadron is shot to pieces; Downie is dead inside fifteen minutes; *Confiance* surrenders by noon; the army's retreat begins by evening. Thomas spends the morning at his gun on a ship he has known from the keel up was not ready. Asa spends the morning at Pike's Ford with two companies of Clinton County men and a regular sergeant who has been showing them how to load standing in two ranks.

The story unfolds across roughly nine days — opening with each of them in his world as it was before the squadrons moved south, and closing on the evening of the day after the battle. Neither thread is subordinate to the other. The book ends with Thomas on a prize tender bound for an American port and Asa back on the south bank of the Saranac, the smoke long since cleared.

## Length and shape

- Target length: ~40,000 words
- Number of chapters: 16

The chapters strictly alternate POV — Thomas, Asa, Thomas, Asa, and so on — across narrative time. The plotter chooses which POV opens the book; once chosen, the alternation holds. Chapter lengths vary with the demands of the beat (roughly 1,800–4,500 words; the average is around 2,500). Day-frame chapters tend to be shorter and quieter; the September 11 action chapters run longer.

**Structural frame.** Each POV gets eight chapters arranged around the September 11 axis:

- **1 day-before chapter** — Thomas and Asa each in his world as it was before the battle. For Thomas: routine watch aboard *Confiance* in the final days of her fitting-out at Île aux Noix, or on the southward passage — a ship being rushed into commission, drills run on a green crew, the lake passing on either side. For Asa: a day at his uncle's sawmill on the Saranac, before the militia muster turned from rumor into orders. The prose observes; it does not foreshadow.
- **5 approach-and-standoff chapters** — *Confiance*'s commissioning and southward sail (Thomas) and the British arrival / Beekmantown / Saranac standoff days (Asa, with internal time elision permitted within a chapter), through the early hours of September 11.
- **1 climactic action chapter** — the main beat of the September 11 engagement in their thread. For Thomas, the gunfight at anchor: Downie dead within fifteen minutes, the ship pounded, the eventual surrender. For Asa, the British attempt on Pike's Ford and what holds the line.
- **1 day-after chapter** — September 12. What is left. For Thomas, captivity routine aboard an American prize tender on Lake Champlain. For Asa, the south bank of the Saranac with the British retreat road visible to the north. The prose observes what has changed; it does not philosophize about what it meant.

The plotter has discretion within this frame; the day-before opening and day-after close are non-negotiable.

## Voice

Third-person limited past, bound tightly to whichever POV the chapter belongs to. Each voice has its own register; the difference between the two should be felt within the first paragraph of any chapter — and the English speech registers should be felt as distinct without being burlesqued.

- **Thomas's chapters**: spare, technical, the prose of a Royal Navy able seaman in his eleventh year of service. Naval vocabulary in stride; the British usage where it differs from the American. He has seen battle and knows what a working gun crew sounds like and what *Confiance* does not. The interior lives in what he does not say, not in what he announces. When he is afraid he notes the wind. His Englishness is in word choice and rhythm, not in dialect spelling — never "yer" or "guv'nor" or anything of the kind.
- **Asa's chapters**: closer to the noise of his nineteen-year-old consciousness — the farmboy still half-present in the militiaman. Dialect is rare and never burlesque. He thinks in the river: in flow, in fall, in shoals — because that is what he has known. The sound of cannon fire from the bay enters his prose as weather, not narration. His Americanness is in idiom and tempo, not in dialect spelling.

Across both: restrained period prose. Action when action calls. Reflection clipped. The story trusts the reader to feel what is not said.

## Audience and register

- **Target audience age range**: adult literary
- **Voice pace**: medium — sentences breathe but do not sprawl; action scenes tighten without becoming clipped
- **Reading-grade target**: college
- **POV count intent**: two co-leads, strict chapter alternation
- **Chapter-ending convention**: mixed — some resolved beats, some mid-action, no manufactured cliffhangers. Literary fade at the close of the book.

## Constraints

- Real-world historical accuracy. Order of battle, timeline, named officers, river fords, ship classifications, militia units, British regiments — all real, all rendered correctly. Specific facts come from the researcher's dossier in `canon/research.md`; the drafter and editors must adhere to it.
- Macdonough, Macomb, Mooers, Pring, Downie, Prevost, Brisbane, Robertson, and other named historical officers may appear but are NEVER POV. They are seen from below — sometimes spoken to, sometimes glimpsed across a deck or a tree line — never interior. The story is told at the deck-and-trench level, not the command level.
- **No jingoism in either direction.** Thomas is not a villain because he is British. Asa is not a hero because he is American. Each is a person in a war neither chose. The prose does not weight one side. Where the historical record is contested — the controversy around Prevost's retreat, the readiness of *Confiance*, the conduct of the militia — render what each character sees and let the contest live in the silences. Do not editorialize.
- No invented intelligence subplot. No spies. No secret messages. No coded signals. The war is in the ships at anchor, the regiments on the road, the militia on the river — not in plot mechanics.
- No "fate of the nation" narration. The historical significance of the battle (it effectively ended the British plan to invade from Canada and stiffened the American position at Ghent) emerges from the action, never announced. The characters do not know what the day will turn out to mean. The prose does not tell the reader either.
- No anachronistic dialogue. Period vocabulary is captured in `canon/research.md`; the drafter must use it. British and American speech registers must differ — but in idiom, rhythm, and word choice, not in phonetic dialect.
- The two POVs never meet. They share a single day. The naval cannonade is audible from the upper fords; that is the only direct sensory crossover the prose may use.
- The book ends in the evening of September 12, 1814 — the day after the battle. Thomas is on a prize tender; Asa is on the south bank of the Saranac. No coda jumping forward beyond that. Whatever each character carries from those two bracketing days is what the reader gets.
- The day-before chapters observe normal life — or what passes for normal in each man's world. They do not foreshadow, do not signal the storm coming. The reader knows the title of the book; the prose does not need to remind them. Thomas's day-before is the working life of a ship being rushed into commission; Asa's is a day at his uncle's sawmill. Both are ordinary in the way only fiction set days before catastrophe can render ordinary.
- The day-after chapters observe what has changed. They do not philosophize about what the battle meant, do not weigh the day's significance, do not eulogize the dead. Thomas's captivity in particular is rendered as the procedural reality it was — the documented routing of Royal Navy prisoners after a surrender — not as a tragic theme to be milked. He is alive, he is among his shipmates, his ship is gone. The prose handles this with the same restraint as the rest of the book.
- Race and class are facts of the period, not subject lectures. Thomas is white; so is Asa. Black and Indigenous people are present in the Champlain Valley in 1814 (and present in the Royal Navy of the period) and should not be invisible, but their treatment in the prose avoids both erasure and centering.
- Period military and naval vocabulary in stride, not in italics. The reader is trusted to follow.
- The wartime period is real history; Thomas Briggs and Asa Beckwith are invented. Names of real places may appear; names of real people may appear; no other real people should be invented into the narrative.

## Research scope

The researcher should produce `canon/research.md` covering at minimum:

- **September 6–11, 1814 timeline.** Beekmantown (Sept 6, Wool's delaying action), Saranac standoff (Sept 7–10), naval and land battle (Sept 11). Hour-by-hour for Sept 11 where the record allows.
- **British naval squadron.** *Confiance* (Downie, flag), *Linnet* (Pring), *Chubb*, *Finch*, and the twelve gunboats: armament, crew composition, manning shortcomings, the rushed construction of *Confiance* at Île aux Noix. Downie's biography and career to age 33. Royal Navy gun crew procedure on a frigate-built sloop or fifth-rate of the period — specifically how an able seaman functioned during action.
- **The Battle of Trafalgar — *Achilles* specifically.** Thomas's earlier service. The ship's role at Trafalgar (the lee column, Collingwood's squadron), her captain, her casualties, the day from a lower-deck perspective. Used to ground Thomas's interior comparisons during the Plattsburgh action.
- **British inland naval establishment.** Île aux Noix shipyard, the rushed *Confiance* build, the manning expedients (drafts from ships of the line, soldiers of the 39th Foot serving as marines, pressed Canadian boatmen).
- **American naval squadron.** *Saratoga* (Macdonough, flag), *Eagle*, *Ticonderoga*, *Preble*, and the ten gunboats: armament, crew composition, where built. Macdonough's biography to age 30. The anchor and spring arrangement, the kedge anchors astern, the maneuver that allowed *Saratoga* to wind 180° mid-action.
- **British land force.** Prevost's army composition (which Peninsular War veteran regiments specifically — the 3rd, 5th, 27th, 39th, 76th, 88th, etc. as documented). Brisbane's brigade. The three Saranac crossing attempts and how each was repulsed. The controversy around Prevost's retreat and the planned (never held) court-martial that was preempted by his death.
- **American land force.** Macomb's regulars (which regiments), the New York militia under Mooers (which units, from which Champlain Valley towns), Wool's delaying action at Beekmantown.
- **The Saranac River line.** Lower Bridge (in town), Upper Bridge, Pike's Ford. Approximate distances, terrain features, what was destroyed and what was defended.
- **Period vocabulary.** Royal Navy gunnery commands and gun crew nomenclature. American naval gunnery commands and gun crew nomenclature (where it diverges from RN usage). British and American infantry drill terminology. Period names for terrain features in the Champlain Valley. Royal Navy lower-deck idiom of the early 19th century — but for register, not for dialect spelling.
- **Contemporary accounts.** Macdonough's after-action letter to Secretary Jones. Macomb's reports. The British court-martial transcripts (Captain Robertson's trial of *Confiance*). Prevost's dispatches and the responses they drew. Period newspaper coverage from both sides of the border. Surviving militia memoirs and surviving RN lower-deck memoirs of the period (e.g., Robert Hay, Samuel Leech) for texture on able-seaman daily life.
- **Race and the period.** The Champlain Valley demographics in 1814. Black and Indigenous presence in the New York militia. Black sailors in the Royal Navy of the period (a meaningful population — render the period correctly, neither erasing nor over-centering).
- **Saranac River sawmills and milling work of the early 19th century.** How a small water-powered sawmill operated, the labor and vocabulary, the rhythm of a working day. Used to ground Asa's day-before chapter.
- **Routine duty aboard a Royal Navy ship being rushed into commission, 1814.** The working life of an able seaman on a ship like *Confiance* in her final days of fitting-out and her shakedown sail — drills, watch organization, the small textures of an ordinary day on a ship that is not yet ready. Used to ground Thomas's day-before chapter.
- **The immediate aftermath of September 11.** Casualty handling and burial practices on both sides, prisoner-of-war routing for surrendered Royal Navy crews (the documented procedure — paroles, prize tenders, eventual inland holding), the British army's retreat march of September 12, the American squadron's repairs and prize procedures. Period documents covering September 12–13. Used to ground both day-after chapters.

Tool budget is 30 `WebSearch` and 30 `WebFetch` calls. Prioritize primary sources: after-action reports, court-martial transcripts, period newspapers, surviving memoirs from both sides. Modern secondary sources only to triangulate.
