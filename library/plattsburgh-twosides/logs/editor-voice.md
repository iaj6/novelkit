## 01-green-timber.md

**Register overall:** Clean. Thomas's voice is correctly calibrated throughout — naval vocabulary in stride, interior life rendered as observation and omission, FID used without thought-tags, ending on the wind as his unspoken gauge. No exclamation marks, no emotion declarations with "felt," no meta-commentary on cognition, no fate-of-the-nation narration.

**One item requiring writer judgment — potential foreshadowing (style guide: day-before chapters observe without foreshadowing):**

> *"She still did. Three months of drills on the river and three months of the Richelieu's damp had not changed that.  
> He did not know what would."*

The fragment "He did not know what would" cannot be fixed at the word level without becoming a sentence-level intervention (compression's domain). The question for the writer: does the fragment invite the reader to supply "battle / shot / fire" as the implied answer? If so, it violates the rule against day-before foreshadowing. If the intended reading is simply Thomas's professional uncertainty about whether the ship will ever smell like a working vessel rather than a carpenter's yard, it is fine. The fragment's antecedent ("what would change the smell") is narrow enough to support either reading — the writer should decide whether the dramatic-irony reading is being courted or not.

## 05-running-out.md

**Register slip — Asa's water/flow metaphor in a Thomas passage**

In the Trafalgar memory section, the simile describing automatized knowledge reads:

> "The knowledge had passed into the body and stopped requiring attention — the way water finds a channel and stops needing to discover it —"

`canon/style.md` assigns water/flow/channel imagery explicitly to Asa's register: "He thinks in the river: flow, fall, shoals, the reading of water in motion." Thomas's metaphors should come from the nautical/mechanical world — rigging, ground tackle, the action of tools. "Water finds a channel" belongs to Asa's vocabulary, not Thomas's.

This can't be fixed at the word-or-clause level without introducing new prose. Suggested direction: replace with a nautical or mechanical image — something from the action of a block finding its lead, a line settling to its correct tension, or a handspike finding purchase. The substituted image should convey automaticity (a system settling into its correct state without searching) through Thomas's technical register rather than Asa's river register.

## 07-the-dispatch.md

**Singular "acoustic" as a count noun — writer judgment needed**

Line: `"a wardroom servant, the captain's clerk, the acoustic of this ship in a quiet anchorage with the hatches open"`

"The acoustic" (singular, meaning the acoustic properties/character of a space) reads as 21st-century register. Period usage favored "acoustics" (plural, scientific) or plain constructions at Thomas's level — e.g., "the way sound carried in this ship." The sentence is doing elegant structural work as the third item in a list, so a simple substitution of "acoustics" alone is insufficient; the rhythm depends on the singular article. Suggest: "the way sound moved in this ship" or "the carry of sound in this ship" — but this is a writer call on whether the modern-feeling singular was intentional.

**Two items flagged for writer's judgment (unfixable at word/clause level):**

**A. Analytical subordination in Asa's POV — Dunbar passage:**
> "what he was looking for now was the hesitation between steps — the small interval where thought was still filling a space that reflex had not yet claimed"

This is free indirect rendering of Dunbar's attention as Asa perceives it. The construction "thought was still filling a space that reflex had not yet claimed" is subordinated analytical prose — closer to Thomas's register than Asa's paratactic, deliver-the-world-in-sequence style. Style guide: "His syntax is more paratactic, delivering the world in sequence rather than in subordinated analysis." A possible Asa-register rendering: "the small hesitation where a man's hands still waited on his head." Writer should decide whether Asa has absorbed enough drill vocabulary to think in these terms, or whether the phrase should be compressed.

**B. Abstract qualifier — "It had stopped seeming like a particular thing":**
> "The valley had been full of that sound since the company left Plattsburgh. It had stopped seeming like a particular thing."

"Seeming like a particular thing" is slightly more literary-abstract than Asa's usual concrete idiom. It's not a clear violation, but it's the kind of elevated generalisation that accumulates across a chapter. A more Asa-weighted alternative: "It had stopped being particular" or simply leaving the preceding sentence to carry the meaning alone.

## 11-first-light.md

**Sentence-level meta-commentary on Thomas's cognitive state — needs writer judgment**

Opening section, paragraph 4:

> He had not particularly meant to watch it that long; it was the kind of watching a man did when a thing he had stopped noticing was no longer there to stop noticing.

The first clause ("He had not particularly meant to watch it that long") is clean Thomas register. The second clause after the semicolon names the *mechanism* of his watching in a recursive, literary way that belongs to the author's voice rather than Thomas's. Thomas observes physical things; he doesn't analyse the phenomenology of his own attention. The style guide identifies "emotional structural analysis" as a failure mode — this is the cognitive variant of it: explaining what kind of mental event just occurred rather than letting the watching stand.

The fix would require either cutting the second clause (compression's territory, since it means halving the sentence) or replacing it with something in Thomas's register. Suggested replacement register: a physical observation that redirects attention outward, e.g., noting what the bay looks like now that the jolly boat is gone, or simply ending the sentence at "that long." Either option requires the writer's call on what, if anything, Thomas turns back to.

**Fixes applied in this pass:**
- "cold and without equivocation" → "cold and hard" (abstract/figurative characterisation replaced with physical weather-speak in Thomas's register)
- "thirty-three, perhaps thirty-four, the same age as the war" → "thirty-three, perhaps thirty-four" (removed portentous lyrical comparison that doesn't resolve as a Thomas observation and nudges toward literary-narrator register)

## 14-pikes-ford.md

**Fixed:** `ambient, constant, telling` → `steady, constant, telling` (opening paragraph). "Ambient" is Latinate/educated-register; a millwright's apprentice's free indirect narration should not carry it. "Steady" is period-neutral and more grounded in Asa's vocabulary.

**Unfixable (writer judgment required):** The thunder simile in the second section —

> "But the sound was what thunder wanted to be if it could persist: rolling and sustained, a concussion that arrived in the chest a heartbeat before the ears caught it…"

"What thunder *wanted to be*" gives thunder volition and desire. The style guide says Asa's images "are drawn from the sawmill and the Saranac"; this is a literary/elevated personification of weather rather than a sawmill-or-river-grounded comparison. The clause works atmospherically but sits slightly above Asa's register. A fix here would require rewriting the clause, which falls outside word-level scope. Suggest: describe the sound through its physical properties alone ("heavier than thunder, without its shape: rolling and sustained…") or through a river-current analogy, removing the personification.

## 16-the-south-bank.md

**Cognition tag requiring writer's judgment — penultimate section**

The sentence **"He heard it now."** sits between two passages describing the ford's riffle sound:

> The riffle at the bar was constant from the rise — that low, continuous sound that was part of the air of this place the way the sound of the sash in the frame was part of the air of the mill. **He heard it now.** It was the same sound it made in the dark before the muster notice came…

Per the style guide's failure mode on meta-commentary on cognition, this sentence labels a perception the surrounding prose already enacts. In free indirect discourse the transition would move straight from the description of the sound to its character ("The same sound it made in the dark before the muster notice came…"). However, removing "He heard it now." entirely is a compression move, not a word/clause fix, so it is outside the voice pass's scope. The writer should decide whether the sentence serves as a deliberate rhythm marker or should be cut.
