# Reader Assessment — Act 3
### Chapters 12–16 (*Both Banks*, *Action*, *Pike's Ford*, *Prize*, *The South Bank*)

---

## Overall Impression

Act 3 is the manuscript's strongest act. Chapters 13 and 14 are its formal and emotional center — the simultaneous September 11 chapters running through the same morning from opposite shores, connected only by the cannonade that moves through Asa's pauses between shots while Thomas is at the gun. The structural seam works. The pacing is appropriate: a compressed center (12–14) followed by two quieter day-after chapters that decompress without explaining. All five threads reach their proper ends. The brief's most demanding constraints — restricted POV, no historical officers interior, no historical retrospect — are honored throughout.

---

## What Works

**The night probe sequence (ch. 12).** Asa reads the ford entirely through sound — limestone gravel scraping, the change in scrape as depth increases, the breath-quality of men listening in the water — and the company holds fire while the probe finds the bar but not the channel. This is Thread 4's penultimate beat delivered through the exact sensory language the book has been building for Asa's chapters. The restraint of the company is shown the same way: Dunbar's "hold gesture, the same shape it had in the morning drills" is the right precision.

**Restricted POV in ch. 13.** Thomas never knows the whole battle. He marks time by "third cycle, or the fourth." Downie's death arrives "by the accounting that reached the lower deck in the way such things did." The unnamed surrender order comes from "a voice Thomas did not recognize." These are the "seen from below" constraint executing correctly at every critical moment. The Trafalgar comparison lands with particular force: "not as memory but the way a man arrives at the name of a tool when a different tool is doing the same work." That is the book's register doing what it should — professional analogy, not declared emotion.

**The cannonade as connective tissue (ch. 14).** The rolling percussion from the east runs through ch. 14 from first assault to final retreat, heard in Asa's pauses between shots. Its cessation is handled exactly right: "The intervals had simply grown until there was nothing between them." Asa cannot know what it means; the reader, holding ch. 13, does. This structural argument — two men on the same morning, neither knowing the other's story — is the book's central formal claim, and here it pays off.

**The parole procedure (ch. 15).** The American officer with the purser's mate and the ledger is everything the brief asked for: bureaucratic, humanely competent, and undramatized. The men speculating about Dartmoor versus inland posting is good period texture. Thomas's hand going to his pocket — the letter there, paper gone soft, set back without opening — completes Thread 2 in a single act of omission. "He had nothing required of him before morning" is the right close.

**Ground-reading in ch. 16.** Asa wades to the north bank and reads the churned mud, the skinned bark, the cold ash — "the way he read the millpond bank after the spring freshet, looking for the measure of how much had come through and how fast." Thread 4 inverts itself cleanly: the knowledge he used to hold the ford now reads what the ford cost the army that tried it.

## What Isn't Working (or Is at Risk)

**Ending-mode uniformity.** Chapters 13 through 16 — four consecutive chapters — all close on the same shape: a quiet image of water or darkness, fading to nothing. Ch. 13: "The colors were down. The lake was still." Ch. 14: "The water ran." Ch. 15: "The lake went dark. He stayed at the rail until it was." Ch. 16: "The September dark came down." These are each individually earned, but the repetition of the mode across all four closing chapters risks monotony, particularly for a reader coming in sequence. The act-level pattern is more visible than any single chapter makes it.

**Verbatim repetition of "the ford was the ford it had always been."** The sentence appears near the close of both ch. 14 and ch. 16 in identical form. If this is a deliberate callback, it needs the act-level rhythm to carry it; if it isn't, it reads as a tic. It arrives so soon in the sequence (two chapters apart) that its second appearance may deflate rather than deepen.

**"He would go back to it" (ch. 16).** Thread 3's resolution is the mill memories — the headgate, the pitman arm, the smell of pine sap — which do the work cleanly and exactly as designed. The sentence that follows — "He would go back to it" — names what the memories have already established. The brief says "He does not announce that he will go back"; this sentence is almost an announcement. The mill memories alone are sufficient. The explicit declaration is a small over-articulation in a chapter that otherwise trusts the reader.

**"It was what it was" (ch. 15).** The Trafalgar comparison is strong. The close of that passage — "He watched it, as he had watched it, and it was what it was" — lands a colloquial resignation phrase in Thomas's interior that reads as contemporary rather than period. It is a register slip, mild but audible in a chapter where everything else is calibrated. Thomas's habituation to difficult realities is already present in the observation itself; the phrase doesn't add to it.

**Final inventory, ch. 13.** "The gun had been at the ship to the end. It had fired for as long as there was an order to fire, and the sequence had held, and the men had held their sequence, and the cartridges had come up from below at their half-step lateness, reliably, through whatever the morning had been." This is Thomas's own assessment, not authorial commentary, so it doesn't strictly violate the style guide. But it explains what the reader has just witnessed — the gun firing to the last, the sequence holding, Weir at the hatch — in a way that approaches the "sentence-after-the-sentence" failure mode. The final two lines ("The colors were down. The lake was still.") are perfect; the three-sentence inventory preceding them blunts the arrival.

## Thread Development

All five threads reach correct resolution in Act 3. Thread 1 (The Unready Ship) closes in ch. 13 as fact, not vindication — the headwind, the delayed approach, the battery thinning. Thread 2 (The Unreachable Home) closes in ch. 15's deliberate non-resolution: letter in pocket, son still unknown, distance unchanged. Thread 3 (The Household Man) closes in ch. 16's mill memories. Thread 4 (Knowledge of Ground as Weapon) resolves in ch. 14: the channel breaks three attempts. Thread 5 (Prevost's Clock) resolves across both ch. 13 and ch. 14 with the structural crossover the book requires. No threads are dropped. The weave holds.

## Brief-Adherence Notes

- **Register distinction**: Audible in the first paragraph of all five chapters. Thomas's opening in ch. 13 ("the tramp of feet, the cry of orders, the sound of anchor work beginning") and Asa's in ch. 12 ("The river ran its hiss at the gravel bar") are immediately identifiable without the chapter header.
- **Historical retrospect**: None detected across all five chapters. The historical significance of the battle never enters the prose.
- **Historical officers as POV**: Clean. Downie dies through rumor; the surrender order comes from an unnamed voice; the rider at the ford delivers a wordless message.
- **Cross-POV knowledge**: Both characters stay within their perceptual limits. Asa learns of *Confiance*'s surrender through a militiaman in ch. 16, appropriately hedged.
- **Jingoism**: The British soldiers in ch. 14 are rendered as professionals — "they moved like men who had done this before and expected the other bank to be defended and came anyway." The American parole procedure in ch. 15 is competent and fair. The balance holds.
- **Day-after philosophizing**: Ch. 15 and ch. 16 observe without weighing, with the one exception noted above ("He would go back to it").
