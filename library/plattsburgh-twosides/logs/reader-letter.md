# Developmental Editor's Letter — *Two Shores*

---

## Overall Impression

At its best, *Two Shores* does something genuinely difficult: it holds two men in two registers across sixteen chapters — the same nine days, opposite shores — without ever letting the prose tip its hand about which side deserved to win or what the day would eventually mean. The two-voice architecture is not a gimmick; it is the book's moral argument made structural. Thomas Briggs and Asa Beckwith are given the same proportion of the page, the same quality of interior attention, and the same refusal of sentimentality at their close. The formal symmetry earns what it promises. A reader finishing this draft will feel they have inhabited two distinct people, not followed a camera across a battle.

The manuscript is closer to publishable than most first drafts of historical literary fiction. The core material is strong. The problems that remain are real and need specific attention — but they are problems of execution at the sentence and chapter-ending level, not problems of conception. The book knows what it is.

---

## What's Working

**1. The two-register architecture holds throughout.** Drop any paragraph from any chapter into a blind test and the POV character is identifiable from the prose texture alone — before the chapter header, before a single proper noun. Thomas's chapters open in the vocabulary of eleven years at sea (forenoon watch, larboard number three, quoin, breeching rope, tompion) and think in the practical assessments of a man who has learned to suppress what he cannot say aloud. Asa's chapters open in the river and the mill (headgate, pitman arm, millpond bark-water, the reading of current) and move paratactically, the world delivered in fast sequence rather than subordinated analysis. This distinction does not slip across sixteen chapters. That is the book's central formal achievement and it must be protected in revision.

**2. The day-before chapters.** "Green Timber" (Ch01) and "The Sash" (Ch02) achieve the near-impossible: they render genuine working lives without loading them with the foreboding the reader carries in from the title page. Thomas's deficient gunlock, the carpenter at the checked plank, the drill that finishes in reasonable time — none of it is preamble. Asa's crook in the first log, the hemlock slowed through by hand, the feed ratchet — the mill feels like a place someone might go back to because it has been made worth going to. The structural argument of these two chapters — that there was a life before the war worth inhabiting — is what makes the September 11 chapters matter. Do not let any revision "heighten" the day-before chapters. Their refusal to heighten is the point.

**3. The ford-wading scene and Thread 4's payoff.** The single strongest scene in Act 1 is Asa wading the channel in Ch06 — counting paces across the gravel bar, feeling the clay give under him, reading the ford in reverse from the north bank, the knowledge made physical rather than explained. The payoff in Ch14, when the channel breaks three successive British assault attempts ("they moved like men who had done this before and expected the other bank to be defended and came anyway"), delivers everything the thread promised. The river does not care about the Peninsular veterans' credentials. The argument is hydraulic and correct.

**4. The springs-on-cables moment (Ch07).** Thomas rounds Cumberland Head and counts the American fleet. He does not declare what he feels. He counts ships, identifies springs on cables, works through the tactical geometry, and concludes by setting down his spoon — picking it up after a moment and finishing the salt pork. This is the book's method at its purest: full professional assessment, zero declared emotion. The Trafalgar comparison earns its length because it arrives as Thomas's professional memory, not the author's historical gloss. This scene is the act-2 peak of Thread 1 and Thread 5 together, and it is handled with exactly the precision the style guide calls for.

**5. The structural crossover of Ch13 and Ch14.** The cannonade running through Ch14 from first assault to silence — "The intervals had simply grown until there was nothing between them" — is the book's formal argument paid in full. Asa cannot know what the silence means. The reader, holding Ch13, does. Thomas's restricted POV in Ch13 is clean throughout: he marks time by "third cycle, or the fourth"; Downie's death arrives "by the accounting that reached the lower deck in the way such things did"; the surrender comes from "a voice Thomas did not recognize." These are the "seen from below" constraints executing correctly at every critical moment. The structural seam holds.

**6. Thread 2's deliberate non-resolution.** The damp letter in Thomas's pocket in Ch15 — the paper gone soft, the hand that goes there and returns without opening — closes the Unreachable Home thread with exactly the restraint the brief demands. The thread does not resolve because the situation does not offer resolution. Thomas is alive, watching a lake, three years from Devonport and no closer. The prose does not mourn this. It simply notes it, and stops. This is the right ending for this thread, and it is the right kind of ending for this book.

## What Isn't Working

**1. Ending-mode uniformity — the book's most significant structural problem.** The brief specifies "mixed — some resolved beats, some mid-action, no manufactured cliffhangers." The manuscript delivers something closer to: quiet literary fade, sixteen chapters running. Act 2 alone has five consecutive fades in the same mode — water, wind, the night being what the night was. Act 3 compounds the problem: Ch13 ("The colors were down. The lake was still."), Ch14 ("The water ran."), Ch15 ("The lake went dark. He stayed at the rail until it was."), Ch16 ("The September dark came down."). Each ending is individually earned. Taken in sequence, they become interchangeable. By Ch15, a reader in full stride through this manuscript has received the same emotional signal — quiet image, fade to dark or water — for nine of the final ten chapters. The accumulation flattens what should be the book's most varied terrain. Revision needs to introduce at least two or three endings in a different mode — a mid-action cut, a resolved beat, a turn on new information — before the final literary fades of Ch15 and Ch16.

**2. Over-articulation — the "sentence-after-the-sentence" failure mode.** The style guide names this explicitly: an image lands, and the next sentence explains what the reader just felt. The problem appears in at least five chapters and is the manuscript's most pervasive craft failure:

- *Ch05 ("Running Out"):* The "working gun crew, when it was right, made a particular sound" passage runs three paragraphs of phenomenological analysis on expert performance — including "the load cycle occurred. Through them." This is Thomas analyzing his own professional cognition in a mode the rest of his register never allows. It breaks his voice and arrives before the Trafalgar comparison, which would have carried the thought without the setup.
- *Ch09 ("Riding at Anchor"):* The night-watch passage restates its central image through the moon's descent to the New York shore and closes on "the night was what the night was" — three sentences doing the work one image had already done. Ch11 found the compression Ch09 needed: "Same as it was." The contrast proves the principle.
- *Ch13 ("Action"):* "The gun had been at the ship to the end. It had fired for as long as there was an order to fire, and the sequence had held, and the men had held their sequence, and the cartridges had come up from below at their half-step lateness, reliably, through whatever the morning had been." This inventory explains what the reader has just witnessed across the full chapter. The next two lines ("The colors were down. The lake was still.") are perfect; the three-sentence explanation preceding them is not.
- *Ch15 ("Prize"):* "He watched it, as he had watched it, and it was what it was." The phrase "it was what it was" is also a mild register slip — colloquial contemporary idiom in a chapter otherwise precisely calibrated.
- *Ch16 ("The South Bank"):* "He would go back to it" names what the mill memories — the headgate, the pitman arm, the smell of pine sap — have already established. The brief is explicit: "He does not announce that he will go back." The mill memories alone are sufficient; the declarative sentence is redundant and nearly a brief violation.

**3. Verbatim repetition in Ch14 and Ch16.** The sentence "the ford was the ford it had always been" appears near the close of both chapters, two chapters apart. If this is an intentional callback, the reader needs the interval and the act-level rhythm to carry it; arriving two chapters apart, it reads as a drafting tic rather than a resonance. Either consolidate the formulation to one appearance or create enough distance and variation that the second instance registers as echo rather than repetition.

**4. The analytical passage in Ch05 breaks Thomas's register.** This is the single most consequential voice slip in the manuscript. Thomas's interior life surfaces through omission, professional reflex, and physical sensation — never through declared cognition about the phenomenology of expertise. The three-paragraph analysis in "Running Out" belongs to an authorial voice stepping in to explain what the Trafalgar comparison would have conveyed on its own. Additionally, the simile within that passage — "the way water finds a channel and stops needing to discover it" — is Asa's vocabulary, not Thomas's. Asa thinks in the river; Thomas thinks in wind and sea. One instance is a small thing; the detection matters because the accumulation of such crossings, if they appear in later chapters, would begin to collapse the register distinction that is the book's central achievement.

**5. Dunbar's function without texture.** Sergeant Dunbar appears across Chs 06, 08, 10, 12, and 14 as the primary secondary human presence at the ford. He issues orders, posts watch, holds fire. He functions correctly as mechanism. But given the sustained page time he occupies, his absence of observed texture is noticeable — he is present in enough scenes that his thinness begins to read as a choice the manuscript hasn't committed to. Not a POV character, and the book wisely never slips into his interiority, but a few more instances of observed exterior behavior would make him a person rather than a role. Compare the unnamed young British prisoner in Ch10, rendered as fully human in three sentences of external observation, with Dunbar across six chapters.

## Pacing Assessment

The book's pacing is largely sound. Act 1 builds with appropriate deliberateness — the two working days, then the departures into the war, then the settling into position — and ends on the right note: Asa at the midnight watch, hearing the ford in the dark, the north bank empty. Act 3 moves with compressed authority through the simultaneous September 11 chapters and decompresses correctly into the two quiet day-after chapters.

The one sustained pacing concern is Ch05 ("Running Out"), which is the longest Thomas chapter in the manuscript and contains four full drill cycles plus the analytical passage before the Trafalgar comparison arrives. By the third cycle, the pattern of correction-and-advance has become predictable, and the chapter's momentum slows before the Trafalgar memory restores it. The chapter runs roughly as long as the analytical passage. Cutting the phenomenological setup (see above) while keeping the Trafalgar comparison would solve both the pacing and the register problem simultaneously.

Act 2's middle — Chs 09 and 10, the standoff days — is the book's quietest sustained passage, and quiet is appropriate there. The risk is that five consecutive fades render the approach to battle imperceptibly instead of building toward it. A single mid-action cut or resolved beat in this stretch (Ch08 or Ch10 is the most natural candidate) would give the sequence a moment of differentiation before the final approach.

## Character Assessment

**Thomas Briggs** is fully alive in his register and fully realized in what he does not say. His relationship with *Confiance* — the man who has known from the keel up that the ship was wrong, yet stands his post — is the book's cleanest dramatic argument. His interior life in Ch15, the damp letter handled and set back, is perfect. The one threat to his voice is the analytical mode of Ch05's "working gun crew" passage; resolve that and Thomas holds without fault.

**Asa Beckwith** is alive and earned. The nineteen-year-old consciousness closer to the surface, the river-reading applied to new situations, the fear named as caution — all present throughout. The ford-wading scene and the ground-reading in Ch16 are his strongest moments, and they are among the book's. The mill-memory resolution of Thread 3 in Ch16 is exactly right; only the subsequent "He would go back to it" needs to come out.

**Dunbar** is the book's most significant secondary character and the one most in need of a single additional observation to become a person. He is present enough to deserve it.

**Downie and other historical officers** are handled correctly throughout — seen from below, without interior access. The inference Thomas draws from Downie's face during the Ch11 inspection ("the quality of a man assessing something he was responsible for and could not change in its essential nature") walks close to the interior access line but stays within POV correctly: it is Thomas reading a face, not the narrator entering Downie's head.

---

## Thread Assessment

All five threads reach their designated resolution. The weave check passes: no dead chapters, correct entries and advances throughout. Some specific notes:

- **Thread 1 (The Unready Ship):** Resolves in Ch13 as fact, not vindication. The headwind, the delayed approach, the battery thinning — the unreadiness is determinative without being editorialized. This is the thread's designed close and it lands correctly.
- **Thread 2 (The Unreachable Home):** Deliberately unresolved, correctly. The letter is the same letter at the end as at the beginning. This thread is the book's most interior achievement and it is handled with complete restraint.
- **Thread 3 (The Household Man):** Resolves correctly in the mill memories of Ch16. Remove "He would go back to it" and the resolution is clean.
- **Thread 4 (Knowledge of Ground as Weapon):** The strongest payoff in the book. Every entry and advance earns the climactic scene. The inversion in Ch16 — the same knowledge now reading what the ford cost — is a graceful structural touch.
- **Thread 5 (Prevost's Clock):** The lower-deck rendering of dispatches that never appear on the page is the right mechanism throughout. The land-side resolution in Ch14 — the British withdrawal, Asa watching without knowing why — is exactly the structural crossover the thread requires.

## Voice Assessment

The prose holds its register with one consistent exception (the analytical mode in Ch05, detailed above) and several smaller over-articulations that share the same root failure: the prose explains what it has already shown. The impulse to add the clarifying sentence after the image is the manuscript's one persistent habit. It appears at the chapter level (Ch09's night-watch), the scene level (Ch13's final inventory), and the line level (Ch15's "it was what it was"; Ch16's "He would go back to it"). None of these are failures of vision; they are failures of last-sentence restraint.

The British-American register distinction is honored without dialect spelling or phonetic performance throughout. Thomas's Englishness is in word choice and rhythm; Asa's Americanness is in idiom and tempo. Both are sustained.

## Suggested Revisions

**1. Vary at least three chapter endings before the final act.** The ending-mode uniformity is the structural problem a second draft must solve first. The book has earned its two literary fades at Ch15 and Ch16. The question is: what did the preceding fourteen chapters earn? Select two or three chapters in Acts 1 and 2 — the most natural candidates are Ch05 (end on the drill itself rather than the moon over the mountains), Ch08 (end on the Beekmantown sound rather than a fading fire), and Ch10 (end on Asa hearing the prisoners rather than "the standoff was four days old") — and close them in a different mode. This does not require new material; it requires cutting the current fade and finding the right earlier image to stop on.

**2. Cut the "working gun crew" analytical passage in Ch05.** Remove the three paragraphs of phenomenological analysis beginning "A working gun crew, when it was right, made a particular sound" and move directly from the drill sequence to the Trafalgar comparison. The comparison carries the professional gap without the setup. This also removes the water/channel simile, repairing the register bleed. The chapter will be shorter and better.

**3. Remove the final explanation in each of the four identified over-articulations.** In Ch09, cut from "the night was what the night was" back to the last concrete image. In Ch13, cut the three-sentence inventory ending "through whatever the morning had been" and let "The colors were down. The lake was still." arrive directly after the surrender. In Ch15, cut "it was what it was" and replace with a precise observation already present in the passage. In Ch16, cut "He would go back to it" and let the mill memories stand.

**4. Add two or three moments of observed texture to Dunbar across Chs 06–14.** These need not be scenes; they can be single external observations of posture, word choice, or physical habit — the same quality of attention Asa gives to the young British prisoner in Ch10. Dunbar's function in the narrative is correct; his thinness as a person is the one gap in Act 2's human texture.

## What to Leave Alone

**The register distinction.** If revision introduces any instinct to make Thomas's chapters more "emotional" or Asa's chapters more "analytical" — to soften the difference between the two voices so they feel more unified — resist it entirely. The formal distance between the two registers is not a stylistic choice that can be revised by degree. It is the book's argument. The two consciousnesses are distinct because the book claims that the two men's experience of the same nine days was genuinely different. A revision that moves the voices toward each other would undo the book's deepest work.

**The day-before chapters.** Do not heighten Ch01 or Ch02. Do not add a weighted image at the end of either. Do not let the soldier who reads the notice too carefully know he is reading it for the last time. Do not let Thomas at the bow understand what the changed wind will mean. Both chapters are correctly restrained. The ordinariness is the point, and it is very hard to write. It is already done.

**The parole procedure in Ch15.** The American officer with the purser's mate and the ledger is exactly what the brief asked for: bureaucratic, competent, and undramatized. The temptation in revision will be to give this scene more emotional weight — perhaps a moment of exchange between Thomas and the American officer, perhaps a more elegiac quality in Thomas's observation of *Confiance* under American colors. Resist both. The chapter's restraint is the chapter's strength. Thomas is alive; his ship is gone; the procedure is the procedure. That is enough.
