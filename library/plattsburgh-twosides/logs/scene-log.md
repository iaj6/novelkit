
## 01-green-timber

## 02-the-sash

In mid-afternoon, clearing debris from the dam, Asa sees Pike's Ford from the millpond edge — reads the gravel bar, the clay banks, the low September water — without emphasis or portent. ## 03-the-lake-opens

Weir arrives half a step late (same as before). Rowan takes the rammer from the wrong shoulder; Thomas repositions him with one hand on his forearm, no words. ## 16-the-south-bank
**Summary:** September 12, 1814. Asa is on the south bank rise at Pike's Ford as the morning comes up — the north bank empty, the gravel bar clear, birds returned to the alder and birch. Through a gap in the pines to the north the retreat road is visible: artillery, wagons, infantry in red coats moving north in discipline. By mid-morning the columns are thinning. The company is at their ordinary tasks: Holt cleaning his musket, Covell fishing from the bar, three men asleep in the sun, Dunbar writing in his ledger. Marsh sits ten feet from Asa with his dressed shoulder and cloth sling, watching the road. A brief exchange — 'They're going' — and nothing more is said about it. In the mid-morning Asa wades the ford: south bank clay into the channel (chest-deep, clay bottom), onto the bar (knee-deep limestone gravel), and up to the north bank. The ground keeps a record: churned boot prints in the clay, pine duff stripped, bark scraped at pack and musket height, a cold fire pit, a tin canteen in the moss. He reads it as he would read the millpond bank after a spring freshet, and wades back. Before noon a militiaman from downstream brings word: the British fleet came into Plattsburgh Bay the previous morning; the flagship Confiance struck her colors before noon; the British captain was killed before the morning was a quarter old. The account is secondhand (militia officer → sailor), some details uncertain, but those three facts are certain. Asa registers the certain things and sets the uncertain ones aside. The fleet is gone. The road to the north empties by mid-afternoon; the last rear guard element passes before one o'clock and after that nothing moves on the road. Asa sits on the south bank and thinks about the headgate, the wheel, the pitman arm, the sash in the frame, the sound and smell of fresh-cut pine, the log carriage, the log dogs, Elias at the adjustment stake — the specific sensory memory of the mill's work, present without being said aloud. The Saranac runs past: the same river, the same level, the same riffle sound it made before the muster notice came. Evening: the retreat road empty, the Vermont mountains dark against a lighter sky with the first maple red-orange at the crowns, the riffle at the bar constant from the rise. The ford was the ford it had always been. Asa sits on the south bank. The mill is three miles upstream. He will go back to it. The light goes off the mountains; the river runs; the September dark comes down.
