# Drafter Guidance — *Two Shores*

## Voice Register

This book's register is adult literary historical fiction, anchored to three named exemplars that you should hold in mind simultaneously:

- **Michael Shaara, *The Killer Angels***: third-person limited per chapter, both sides given full interiority, military vocabulary in stride, no authorial thumb on the scale. This is the primary model for handling a battle from both sides. Shaara's chapters feel like a man's specific consciousness filtering the action — never an omniscient camera.
- **Patrick O'Brian, *Master and Commander***: naval world rendered without explanation. The prose trusts the reader to absorb vocabulary from context. The texture of shipboard life — the watch system, the mess, the names of ropes and positions — enters in stride. This is the anchor for Thomas's chapters.
- **Charles Frazier, *Cold Mountain***: literary historical prose where period vocabulary and landscape do the work exposition would do in a lesser book. Frazier's sentences accumulate through specific observed detail; the world builds up through what the character sees and touches and hears. This is the anchor for Asa's chapters.

## Sentence-Length Expectations

- **Thomas's chapters:** average sentence length 14–20 words. Weight arrives at sentence-end. Subordination used sparingly and purposefully. Three to four short declaratives can build rhythm for naval procedure or tension.
- **Asa's chapters:** average sentence length 10–16 words. More paratactic. The world delivered in sequence. Shorter sentences more frequent than Thomas's.
- **Maximum acceptable length:** 35 words. Any sentence exceeding this should be audited.
- **September 11 action chapters:** further shortening. Details — object, sound, direction of force — become the unit. Subordinate clauses cost time you don't have.

## Chapter-Opening Conventions

**Thomas's chapters** should open with the ship and the work. The opening paragraph should anchor the reader in something specific and physical aboard *Confiance*: the feel of a particular watch, a particular task being done, the state of the lake seen from a particular station. Do not open on abstract reflection. O'Brian-style: in the middle of something already happening.

**Asa's chapters** should open with the river or the land. The opening paragraph should anchor in sensory texture — the sound and smell and physical feel of wherever Asa is. The opening registers his consciousness through what he observes, not through what he thinks about what he observes. Frazier-style: the world first, the interior second.

In both registers: **the POV character's specific vocabulary should be audible in the first sentence.** A reader who starts a chapter mid-book and has the opening paragraph in hand should be able to say Thomas or Asa within three sentences.

## Chapter-Ending Conventions

Target distribution across 16 chapters: approximately 5 resolved beats, 6 mid-action or end-on-a-fact, 4 literary fades on an image or observation, 1 cliffhanger-equivalent (the evening before the battle). No manufactured dramatic hooks — no character gasps, no revelation withheld for false suspense. The chapter endings should feel like the day ending, or a decision made, or a fact that cannot be unseen. Literary fades are appropriate at the close of the day-before and day-after chapters; mid-action cuts are appropriate in the approach-and-standoff chapters; the September 11 action chapters earn a resolved beat.

## POV Strategy

Two co-leads, strict chapter alternation. The POV never shifts within a chapter. No exceptions.

Thomas owns the Royal Navy world — *Confiance*, the lower deck, the gun stations, the southward passage, the Île aux Noix experience. Asa owns the Saranac world — Pike's Ford, the sawmill, the river, the militia line, the two companies of Clinton County men. Their worlds do not overlap except through the sound of the cannonade.

**The voices must be distinguishable by prose texture alone.** If you put two consecutive paragraphs from different chapters side by side without headers, a reader should be able to tell which is Thomas and which is Asa by vocabulary, sentence rhythm, and the quality of observation — not by the names used. If both chapters sound the same in prose texture, the register is drifting.

## Dialogue Density

Dialogue is present but not heavy. Neither character speaks at length about his internal states. Dialogue is operational: tasks, observations, orders, requests, dry exchanges between working men. Silence and action carry more weight than speech.

**Thomas's dialogue** should feel clipped, naval, professionally economical. He speaks in the idiom of a man who has been told what to do by officers for eleven years and has learned to give and receive orders without excess. No "guv'nor," no eye-dialect, no performing of Englishness.

**Asa's dialogue** should feel like the valley — plainer syntax, less ceremony, the directness of a young man who has been the working adult in his household since fifteen. No eye-dialect, no performing of ruralness.

Free indirect discourse is preferred over explicit thought-tags for rendering interior reaction. If the prose can carry Thomas's unspoken assessment through the texture of his observation — if the thought of *Confiance*'s unreadiness enters through how he watches the drill, not through "he thought" — do that.

## Comic Timing and Tonal Balance

This book is not comic. The tonal register is grave, restrained, and occasionally dry — the particular dryness of working men in difficult circumstances. Dry humor may appear in dialogue or in the slight irony of a professional man observing incompetence (Thomas on the green crew, the missing gunlocks). Keep it brief and in register — gallows-dry, never comic relief.

## Anti-Defaults: What the Drafter Must Actively Avoid in This Register

These are the specific failure modes for this book. Each one is a temptation because the material is rich and the literary attractor wants to do all of them:

1. **Do not announce historical significance.** Any sentence that tells the reader what the battle meant — at Ghent, to the British invasion plan, to the shape of the treaty — is the book's primary failure mode. The brief names it explicitly: "No fate of the nation narration." The characters don't know; the prose doesn't know.

2. **Do not gloss the images.** An image lands — Downie's watch stopped, the smoke over the water, the ford in September light — and then the next sentence explains it. Cut the second sentence every time. The image does the work alone.

3. **Do not explain the suppressed interior.** Thomas is afraid; Thomas does not say so; the prose does not say "he was afraid but did not show it." The fear arrives through what he observes (the wind, the state of the gun locks, the number of men he does not know). The analysis is the reader's to perform.

4. **Do not foreshadow in the day-before chapters.** The reader knows the book is set before a battle. The prose does not remind them. No ominous skies, no loaded silences, no small moments given italicized weight. The mill is the mill; the watch is the watch.

5. **Do not philosophize in the day-after chapters.** The brief is explicit: "The prose observes what has changed; it does not philosophize about what it meant." Thomas on the prize tender is rendered as procedural reality. Asa on the south bank has the river and the road. These are not metaphors.

6. **Do not let the POV voice drift toward the other character's register between chapters.** After drafting an Asa chapter, the next Thomas chapter should recalibrate: longer sentences, heavier naval vocabulary, weight at the end of clauses, the suppressed interior. The drafting process has a strong tendency to carry the previous chapter's rhythm forward.

7. **Do not invent plot mechanics.** No intelligence subplot, no spies, no intercepted messages, no secret signals. The war is in the ships and the fords. This is non-negotiable.
