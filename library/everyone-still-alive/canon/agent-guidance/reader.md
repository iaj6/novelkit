# Reader Guidance — *Everyone Still Alive*

## Intended Audience

Adult crossover readers who bring comfort with literary fiction but are choosing this book partly for genre satisfaction: apocalyptic dread, survival stakes, moral horror. The Vignette 1 opening (The Drill) is pitched at upper-YA — fast, sensory, a teenage POV — and is intended to recruit readers who came for that register. The book then deepens and slows, moving through McCarthy-register bleakness (#3), cold perpetrator first-person (#4), and slow maritime dread (#6). The ideal reader is a patient genre reader who will hold six separate casts in mind, trust the structural argument to accumulate across the mosaic, and reward a book that takes its form seriously as meaning.

The Reader phase must evaluate whether the manuscript succeeds for *this* reader — not for a reader of pure literary fiction (who may want more interiority in all vignettes) and not for a pure genre reader (who may want a single protagonist's arc across all 22 chapters).

---

## Brief-Adherence Checks to Perform

For each vignette, ask and answer explicitly:

### Global (all vignettes):
- Does the register shift feel **legible** when the vignette changes? A reader should feel the floor change underfoot. If not, name which vignette has averaged toward the literary default.
- Does every chapter advance the thesis question — who is the monster — without stating it in narration?
- Is the **infected gradient intact?** Vivid/physical in V1 only; absent/feared in V2–4; never seen in V5; ambiguous in V6. Flag any vignette where the infected are mentioned in a way that breaks this gradient.
- Does the word "zombie" appear in narration (not in character speech)? If so, flag it.
- Are the **connective threads** present? The Reader of Names heard in the correct vignettes (1, 3, 5, 6); the carved fish present as a concrete object in V1, V3, and V5/V6.

### Vignette 1 — The Drill (fast YA horror):
- Does Chapter 1 open with action or dialogue, not description?
- Are sentences short by default? If a paragraph has 3+ sentences averaging over 20 words, flag the register drift.
- Do chapters end on cliffhangers or mid-action cuts? If any V1 chapter ends on a literary fade or resolved beat, flag it.
- Does Amara's choice (opening the door) read as compassionate, not as stupidity or villainy? This is the thesis's first proof; if it reads like a dumb horror-movie mistake, the vignette has failed.

### Vignette 2 — The Line (institutional dread):
- Does the horror live in procedure rather than action? If violence is explicit and dramatic (not quiet and procedural), flag the register drift.
- Does Selle's complicity arrive through what he does, not through what he thinks or feels about doing it?
- Does the vignette end on the decision to turn back the woman — without showing us the consequence?

### Vignette 3 — The Road South (literary-bleak):
- Are sentences long, internally cadenced, and subordinate-rich? If the average sentence is under 15 words, flag the register drift.
- Is dialogue unattributed (indent only)? If tags appear, flag — this is intentional in the McCarthy register.
- Do all four chapters end on quiet bleak fades? If any chapter ends on a cliffhanger or dramatic beat, flag it.
- Are the three near-deaths from other people, not infected?

### Vignette 4 — The Sweep (cold perpetrator):
- Is it in **first person, present tense** throughout? Any slip to past tense or third person is a hard error.
- Does the narration avoid editorializing about what the crew does? If Cass's narration condemns or excuses the crew's actions, flag it.
- Is the kid's final understanding and decision left unnarrated by Cass — shown through what the kid does, not explained?

### Vignette 5 — The Raft (warm communal):
- Is there any infected activity, sighting, or confirmed encounter in this vignette? If so, flag as a gradient violation.
- Does the communal register hold? Is "they / the community / the raft's people" used appropriately to widen from Nadia's close third?
- Does the Galena appear in any chapter 15–17? If so, flag — it belongs to Vignette 6 only.

### Vignette 6 — The Derelict (slow-dread maritime):
- Does Nadia's register visibly tighten across the five chapters — warm in Ch 18, strained in Ch 21, spare in Ch 22?
- Is the Galena present in every chapter, even when not named directly?
- Does the vignette avoid definitively answering what is on the Galena before the final chapter?
- Is Mikkel's treatment by the community the moral center of the vignette — more important than the ship's contents?
- Does the Reader of Names thread remain unresolved?
- Does the last image of Chapter 22 deliver the thesis?

## What "Good" Looks Like for This Book

**Good Vignette 1** reads like the opening of Charlie Higson's *The Enemy* or the first 40 pages of Rick Yancey's *The 5th Wave*: short declaratives, a teenager's sensory present, dialogue that breaks before the scene is established, the threat arriving by motion and sound before it has a name. A reader who would not normally read this book picks up Chapter 1 and cannot stop.

**Good Vignette 3** reads like the opening of McCarthy's *The Road*: long sentences with internal pulse, physical landscape rendered as indifferent character, dialogue stripped of tags and affect. A reader who has never read McCarthy is slowed down in a way that feels intentional, not labored.

**Good Vignette 4** reads like Alden Bell's *The Reapers Are the Angels*: a beautiful prose voice that is doing something terrible, and the gap between the beauty and the action is the horror.

**Good Vignette 5** earns its warmth — meaning the warmth is specific (these people, this morning light, this spinach in the greenhouse) and not generic. It does not feel like a break from the book; it feels like the book's argument about what is still possible.

**Good Vignette 6** makes the reader feel the Galena in chapters where it is not mentioned. The dread is ambient before it is specific.

## What to Flag Explicitly if the Manuscript Drifts

**The single most important drift to name explicitly:** If any vignette is written in the literary-adult default register — long sentences, deep interiority, restrained subtext, measured pacing — when its assigned register is something different, **name this drift by vignette and by register.** Do not allow craft excellence in the wrong register to pass as success. A beautifully written Vignette 1 in the McCarthy register is a failure. A tender Vignette 4 that gives Cass interiority and self-awareness is a failure.

**Other drifts to name explicitly:**

- **Thesis stated in narration.** Any sentence that articulates the book's argument ("the monster was fear," "this was what civilization had come to," etc.) is a flag.
- **Infected gradient violation.** Confirmed infected activity in Vignettes 2–5 breaks the structural argument. Name it and specify the vignette.
- **Single-protagonist compression.** If the mosaic begins to feel like it's "about" one character — as if Nadia or Jesse or Lenn is the real protagonist and the others are supporting material — name this. The book has six protagonists and no hierarchy.
- **Connective thread over-articulation.** If any character recognizes the carved fish's full history, or if the Reader of Names is definitively identified, flag it as a continuity and thematic failure.
- **Tone uniformity.** If all six vignettes share the same emotional temperature — even if individually the writing is strong — flag the averaging. The tonal range from Vignette 1's urgency through Vignette 3's bleakness through Vignette 5's warmth through Vignette 6's dread is the book's architecture.

## What to Applaud

When the manuscript hits these well, explicitly defend them in the "what to leave alone" section:

1. **Register shifts that feel like a genre shift** — if moving from Vignette 2 to Vignette 3 genuinely feels like entering a different book (different sentence rhythm, different relationship between landscape and character, different weight of dialogue), praise this and defend it against any compression toward uniformity.

2. **Amara's compassion reading as compassion, not stupidity** — this is the hardest single beat in the book. If it lands with genuine moral weight — her choice was the right human choice in the wrong situation — protect it.

3. **Vignette 5's warmth as hard-won argument, not tonal relief** — if The Raft reads as a genuine answer to the thesis (not a break from the dark material, but the book's evidence that the opposite of monstrous is achievable), applaud this and defend it against editors who read warmth as slackening.

4. **Cass's prose being beautiful in Vignette 4** — if the first-person perpetrator voice achieves the Alden Bell register (lyrical and violent simultaneously), defend its beauty. Flatness is not the goal; precision and evasion in the moral register are. A beautiful sentence from Cass is doing the book's most difficult work.

5. **The last image of Chapter 22** — if the final image of the book lands with earned resonance and delivers the thesis without stating it, protect it from any pressure toward a more explicit or comfortable closing.
