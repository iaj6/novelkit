# Themes

## 1. Distance from catastrophe makes monsters

The book's structural argument — embedded in its chronology and enforced by the infected gradient. The closer a vignette is to the actual infected, in time and physical proximity, the less monstrous the people in it are to each other. In Vignette 1 (Week One), the infected are real and present and terrifying, and the worst act is a fifteen-year-old's friend opening a door because a person was crying on the other side. By Vignette 4 (Year One), there are no infected for hundreds of miles, and the narrator is one of the monsters and knows it and continues. The gradient is the proof. The monster is whatever distance from the actual catastrophe lets people pretend they are still good.

This theme must never be stated in the narration. It lives in the structure itself. Any vignette that states it explicitly has failed.

## 2. The question of the good person — asked and answered differently by each vignette

Each vignette's POV character is being tested by the same implicit question: are you still a good person? The answers range across the book's six movements. Jesse: yes, in the way a fifteen-year-old can be, and the yes costs him. Daan Selle: he is trying, and he has not yet understood that trying is no longer sufficient. Lenn: he is no longer certain, and the uncertainty is the right answer. Cass: no, and they know, and they continue. Nadia (V5): yes — and she is the evidence that goodness is not only possible but buildable. Nadia (V6): yes, but for how much longer, and at what cost. The question is never settled globally; it is settled freshly and differently by each vignette. The sequence from Jesse's yes through Cass's no to Nadia's fragile yes is the book's emotional arc.

## 3. What we leave behind — small things persist

The carved wooden fish travels hand to hand across two years and six casts, and no one knows where it has been. No heroic act is responsible for its survival; no plan sent it forward. It moves because people — a fifteen-year-old who carves when he is afraid, a boy who finds things, a stranger on a road — carry small made objects for a while and set them down. What survives the collapse is not the large decisions; it is the small making. Nadia's logbook is the same gesture at greater length: someone needs to write down that this happened, that people were here. The connective threads of the book (the fish, the radio, the names being read) are all gestures of insisting on record against erasure.

## 4. The form is the argument

*Everyone Still Alive* could not exist as a single-protagonist survival novel without abandoning its thesis. The mosaic form — six separate casts, hard cuts, no shared characters, register shifts that the reader feels underfoot — is itself the argument that all six casts are one humanity. Each vignette is a facet of the same catastrophe viewed from a different angle, in different light, at a different depth. The reader assembles the argument from the fragments the same way survivors assemble knowledge of the world from what the radio says, what the road shows, what the horizon holds. The connective threads (the fish, the Reader of Names, the thesis question itself) are not connective-tissue plot devices; they are the seams that make the mosaic visible as a single object when you step back.
