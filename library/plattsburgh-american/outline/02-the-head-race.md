# Chapter 02 — The Head Race

**One-line summary:** Asa works a full day at the sash sawmill; the river and the work are ordinary; on the walk home he thinks briefly about his father.

**POV:** Asa Beckwith  
**Tense:** Past  
**Function:** Day-before

---

## Setting / Time

The uncle's sash sawmill on the Saranac River, approximately three miles upstream of Plattsburgh village. ~August 29, 1814 — a day before or alongside ch 1; the day is ordinary, chosen because it has not yet been interrupted.

Time span: dawn to dusk, and a short walk home after. A full working day, compressed to its essential textures.

---

## Chapter Shape

**Bottle** — one location (the mill and its immediate environs: the head race, the log yard, the mill floor, the tail race, the river bank). Near real-time compression of a single day's work. The mill is a working place, rendered with specificity; it is not a stage set. No dramatic event. No foreshadowing.

## Ending Mode

**Literary fade** — Asa walks home upstream along the river. The mill's sound fades behind him. He thinks of his father, briefly, and then the chapter closes on the sound of the river going dark.

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

*The goal is the day's work. The obstacle is the work itself — logs, water, saw. The beats are the beats of making boards.*

1. **Dawn — the head race:** Asa arrives at the mill before the uncle, checks the head race and the water gate. The Saranac in late August: clear over gravel and stone, cold even at the margin. He gauges the water level by a mark on a specific flat rock — a nick he cut himself at the waterline two summers ago when the uncle told him to record the August level. The water is right: a half-inch above the nick. He opens the sluice gate to fill the head race. The water begins to move.

2. **Opening the mill:** The uncle arrives (name: **Eli Beckwith**, approximately 50, the mother's brother, a laconic man who teaches by demonstration rather than instruction). He nods at Asa. They open the wheel gate; the millwheel catches and begins to turn; the pitman arm takes up its lurch; the sash blade begins its reciprocating stroke. The sound is the first entry of the chapter's register: the groan of the pitman arm, the hiss and thump of the blade. Sawdust drifts down to bank in the still water at the tail race.

3. **Morning — a run of pine boards:** They are working through a contract for Plattsburgh — framing lumber for a new storage building on the south side of the village. The logs have been floated down from upstream and are stacked in the log yard. Asa sets the first log with the cant hook (rolling it onto the carriage, the iron hook biting the wood), sets the bail dogs to grip it, and opens the gate a fraction to advance the carriage on the upstroke. A **laborer in the log yard** — briefly characterized: a man of mixed Abenaki descent, perhaps forty, who has worked the log yard seasonally for three years, known to Asa since he was a boy — handles the finished boards as they come off the back of the carriage, stacking them in the drying shed. He and Asa exchange a few words about the log quality; the pines are second-growth, the rings tight, but one log has a hidden knot that may cause trouble.

4. **Noon stop:** The mill stops for the noon meal. The blade swings to rest. The sudden quiet is always startling; after an hour of the sash sound, the silence has a physical quality. Asa walks to the river bank and washes sawdust off his hands and forearms. He eats his dinner sitting on the bank with his feet over the water. The Saranac here is wider and slower than at Pike's Ford downstream; the current is gentler. He knows where the channel runs deep (a hole in the gravel below the millpond dam) and where it runs shallow (a bar at the bend fifty yards downstream). He knows the river the way he knows his own hands.

5. **Afternoon — the hidden knot:** The log the laborer warned about turns out to be worse than expected. The saw blade catches in the knot and hops; the carriage jerks sideways. No one is hurt; the blade doesn't break. But they lose time clearing the jam and re-setting the log. The uncle examines the knot without irritation and explains, very briefly, what Asa should have looked for when gauging the log in the yard: a discoloration in the bark, a slight swelling. Asa looks at the bark and sees it now. He stores this.

6. **End of the working day:** The last board cut. The uncle closes the gate; the wheel slows; the pitman arm stutters to a stop. They rack the tools, cover the saw blade, bank sawdust away from the wheel housing where it might catch. Asa checks the head race water level one more time (lower now — the afternoon's work has drawn it down; he will need to note whether it recovers overnight). He notes it without writing it down; he will know in the morning whether the Saranac is right.

7. **The walk home:** Asa walks upstream along the south bank of the Saranac. The mill behind him, the sound of the river his company. It is dusk; the water is catching the last light and the alders are dark. He thinks of his father: a fact he holds. His father is upstream at the house, in the fever that did not leave after Sackett's Harbor. Asa has been the wage earner since he was fifteen. He does not think about this at length; it is simply the weight of the walk.
