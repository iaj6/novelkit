# Chapter 09 — The Kedge

**One-line summary:** Silas overhears the winding maneuver described; understands for the first time what the spring-and-kedge arrangement is for; visits the port battery; the chapter ends with a fact stored in its right place.

**POV:** Silas Ware  
**Tense:** Past  
**Function:** Approach 4

---

## Setting / Time

USS *Saratoga*, Plattsburgh Bay. September 9–10, 1814 — two days before the battle.

Time span: Portions of two days; the chapter's substance is a single realization and its aftermath.

---

## Chapter Shape

**Interiority-heavy** — the main action of the chapter is Silas's mental processing. The physical events (the overheard conversation, the visit to the port battery) are brief; the chapter's weight is in what Silas does with a piece of information. His interiority does not announce itself — it surfaces as a sequence of observations and stored facts — but the chapter's shape is the shape of a mind working.

---

## Ending Mode

**Turn** — the chapter ends with Silas at his starboard guns after returning from the port battery. A fact has arrived (the winding plan) and shifted the chapter's register. The chapter ends without elaborating the shift — the fact is stored, the guns are right, the chapter closes.

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

1. **Scene 1 — The overheard conversation (Sept 9, morning):** Silas is working on the breech of gun #2 — cleaning fouling from the vent — when the ship's carpenter and his mate pass along the main deck above. Their voices come down through the grating. They are confirming something about the stern-anchor cable, speaking in the shorthand of men who already understand each other: *"…let go the stern, cut the bower, haul on the kedge, and she comes around…"* The carpenter says something Silas can't hear; the mate answers: *"…long as the kedge cable holds…"*
   
   They pass on. Silas is still for a moment. He parses the words: *let go the stern anchor, cut the bower cable, haul on the kedge.* This is a description of winding a ship — turning her 180° at anchor, without headway, without room to sail. He has heard the maneuver described once, years ago, in a seamanship lecture at the receiving ship in Boston Harbor, before his enlistment. He had not thought of it since.

2. **Scene 2 — The implications, worked through:** Silas does not stop working; he continues cleaning the vent, and while his hands do the familiar work his mind does the unfamiliar work of tracing the geometry.
   
   *The starboard battery is the battery that will bear on the British fleet as they round Cumberland Head and enter the bay.* They will come from the northeast; the *Saratoga* is anchored to bring the starboard side toward that approach. The engagement will begin on the starboard side. The engagement will continue on the starboard side until — until the starboard battery is shot to pieces, or until the ship winds.
   
   *There are four guns on the port battery.* He has never been responsible for them; they belong to another quarter-gunner. But they are there. If the ship winds, they will bear.
   
   He does not say this to Fry. He does not say it to the gunner's mate. He finishes cleaning the vent, moves to gun #3.

3. **Scene 3 — The port battery (Sept 9, afternoon):** Silas walks to the port side of the gun deck on his off-watch and looks at the port battery. Four long twenty-fours, identical to his own. The quarter-gunner of the port battery is a man named **Gideon Marsh** — approximately 28, from Massachusetts, the kind of man who doesn't volunteer information but answers questions precisely. Silas looks at Marsh's guns without saying anything for a moment. Then he says: *"Your number-two gun's flint is cracked."*
   
   Marsh looks at him. He says: *"I know."* He has been meaning to replace it this afternoon. Silas nods. He looks at the garlands on the port guns — the shot is right; seventeen in one, eighteen in the others. He does not count out loud. He does not explain why he is looking.
   
   Marsh says: *"Looking for something?"* Silas says: *"Just looking."* He returns to the starboard battery.

4. **Scene 4 — Evening, the fact stored:** Silas eats his supper. He is not preoccupied; he is quiet, which is how he is most of the time. The cracked flint on Marsh's gun nags at him in a minor, habitual way — not his gun, but a wrong thing — until, between the hardtack and the coffee, he hears from the mess table that Marsh has replaced the flint. The gunner's mate mentioned it in passing.
   
   After supper Silas goes back to the starboard battery. He checks his four guns in order: flints, vents, cartridge allocation, garland count. Everything is right. He does not think about the port battery or the winding maneuver; those are facts he has stored. His four guns are in front of him. He attends to them.

---

## Outcome / Turn

Silas now understands — in general terms, not in tactical detail — that the *Saratoga*'s starboard battery is not the whole of the battle. If it is destroyed, the ship can wind and bring the port battery to bear. He has stored this fact in the right place. He has also introduced himself, obliquely, to Marsh and the port guns. The chapter ends with this shift in Silas's understanding settled and set aside — not acted on, not announced, simply carried.

---

## New Question or Pressure Raised

What will happen to the starboard battery? The question is now implicit in everything Silas does at his four guns. But the chapter does not raise it aloud. Silas checks the flints and goes below.
