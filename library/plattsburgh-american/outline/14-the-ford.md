# Chapter 14 — The Ford

**One-line summary:** The British assault Pike's Ford; the two-rank fire holds the line; Asa's knowledge of the ford saves the flank; the naval cannonade stops; the British pull back.

**POV:** Asa Beckwith  
**Tense:** Past  
**Function:** Climactic action

---

## Setting / Time

Pike's Ford, south bank of the Saranac River. September 11, 1814 — approximately 11:00 AM to early afternoon (approximately 1–2 PM).

Time span: Approximately two to three hours of action, with some compression in the middle; the chapter renders the assault in close detail at the beginning and end, with the sustained middle exchange compressed.

---

## Chapter Shape

**Action-heavy** — the chapter's substance is the ford defense. Like ch 13, the prose stays in the POV character's immediate sensory world: what Asa can see, hear, feel from the south bank. No tactical overview. The action is comprehensible because the reader knows the ford from chs 8, 10, and 12.

---

## Ending Mode

**Resolved beat** — the British troops are back on the north bank; the ford is clear; Asa is standing on the south bank with a hot musket and ringing ears. A unit of action is complete. He is alive. The chapter ends without reaching for further meaning.

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

### PHASE 1: The British Arrive (~11:00 AM)

1. **First movement on the north bank:** The north bank comes alive. Not a rush — a deliberate movement through the brush on the far side, the sound of boots and equipment in undergrowth. Then the first British soldier appears at the tree line: red coat, white cross-belts, musket at the trail. Then another. Then the brush releases a column of them at the ford's edge.
   
   They are well turned out. They are professional soldiers — the kind that Veeder meant when he said *as good as people say.* Asa has never seen a British soldier at this distance before. They look like soldiers from a book illustration, except that they are breathing and their boots are muddy from the road.

2. **The British enter the water:** The column dresses its front rank and steps into the ford. The first men are thigh-deep at the center; then knee-deep on the limestone shelf. Asa is watching them from the main position, twenty-five yards away, behind the cut brush. He knows exactly where their feet are. He knows where the gravel trough is, twelve feet ahead of them. He knows that when the front rank steps off the limestone shelf into the gravel, they will lose three inches of height and the bottom will shift.
   
   He does not fire. The sergeant has not given the order.

3. **The sergeant's command:** *"First rank, make ready."* Asa is in the first rank. He brings his musket up, cocks it, settles the stock against his shoulder. The British column is mid-ford. The front rank is into the gravel trough; their step has changed, hesitant for a moment. Twenty yards.

### PHASE 2: The Exchange (~11:00–12:30 PM)

4. **First volley:** *"Fire."* The south bank fires — not in unison (militia rarely achieve true unison), but close enough: a ragged-simultaneous crash from the entire line. At twenty to twenty-five yards into a column of men in shallow water, the volley is effective. Some of the British soldiers go down; others stop. The column's forward momentum breaks for a moment.
   
   Asa is already reloading. Bite the cartridge, pour, prime, powder down, ball, patch, ram. Nine steps. The sequence. His hands do it.

5. **The British response:** The British are not routed. They are professional soldiers and they have taken fire before. The front rank kneels in the water and fires back; the rear rank advances over them. The British volley hits the south bank. Asa hears the individual shots going past him — a specific sound he has no prior experience of: a sound between a whistle and a snap, close and personal, coming from the wrong direction. He does not stop reloading.
   
   *"Second rank, fire."* The second rank fires while the first rank reloads. The exchange is continuous now.

6. **The sustained middle exchange:** The chapter compresses here — the back-and-forth of the exchange rendered as accumulated sensory experience rather than shot by shot. Asa's world for the next thirty to forty minutes:
   - The sequence: load, fire, load, fire. The sequence holds.
   - The smoke: thickening; the far bank becomes an impression, a direction.
   - The sound: the naval cannonade from the east is a low continuous presence, felt in the chest, beneath the nearer sound of the muskets.
   - Holt, to Asa's right, loading and firing steadily.
   - Veeder, downstream, not visible through the smoke but audible — he shouts *"Ford's right flank — move up"* at one point and someone from further down the line moves.
   - The British are in the water. Some are down. Others are pressing. They have not broken through.

### PHASE 3: The Flank (~Midday)

7. **The flanking attempt:** At some point in the middle of the exchange, Asa is aware of movement to his right — upstream, in the willows. Not coming from the ford: coming from the north bank downstream of the willows, where the brush is thick. Someone is looking for another way across. He thinks: the submerged log under the alders. He told the sergeant about this in ch 8. He looks for the sergeant.
   
   He finds Cole twenty feet to his left, watching the main ford. Asa calls: *"Sergeant. The willows, north side."* Cole looks. A moment. Then: *"Webb, Holloway — willows, right flank."* Two men peel off to cover the willows.
   
   A moment later: a crash from the willows — branches, water, confusion. The flanking party has stepped on the submerged log that Asa described. The log rolled. Men down. Asa doesn't see this; he hears it — the particular sound of men losing their footing in fast water. He turns back to the main ford.

8. **The flanking party withdraws:** The flankers don't press through the willows. The fallen men are hauled back; the British column at the main ford is already beginning to hesitate. The flanking attempt has cost time and men and the main thrust is losing force.

### PHASE 4: The Silence (~10:30–11 AM in the bay; ~11:30–12 PM perceived at the ford)

9. **The naval cannonade stops:** Asa is loading when he notices it. The pressure in his chest that has been there since half past eight — the low sustained percussion from the east — is gone. He has been hearing it for so long without thinking about it that its absence is startling. He does not look east. He keeps loading.
   
   He notices the absence and then the ford demands his attention. He will not think about what the absence means until later.

10. **The British hesitate:** After the cannonade stops — whether because of the ford's stiffening resistance, or orders received from somewhere beyond what Asa can see, or both — the British column's pressure at the ford lessens. The front rank has stopped advancing; it has moved from mid-ford to the north-bank shelf. The British are still firing, but they are firing from a position that is not advancing.
    
    Sergeant Cole: *"First rank. Deliberate. Don't rush the shot."* The sequence continues, but slower now.

### PHASE 5: The Withdrawal (~1:00–2:00 PM)

11. **The British pull back:** The column at the ford withdraws. Not in a rout — the British rear ranks cover the front ranks as they step back toward the north bank. They move with the same deliberateness they came with. They are professional soldiers pulling back in order, and the thing that is frightening about it is that it looks almost the same as their advance.
    
    Sergeant Cole holds the south bank in position. He does not order pursuit. The militia does not pursue. They stand at their posts and watch the red coats move back through the north-bank brush and disappear.

12. **The ford empty again:** The British troops are gone from the ford. The north bank is empty — empty in a different way than this morning's empty, because now there are three or four bodies in the shallows and the ford is running a slightly different color downstream of them, which Asa notices and then stops looking at.
    
    Sergeant Cole: *"Stand easy."* 
    
    Asa uncocks his musket and lowers it. His hands are shaking slightly; he is surprised by this, because his hands did not shake during the fight. He notes: the shaking is after, not during. He holds his musket at the trail and looks at the ford.

---

## Outcome / Turn

The ford held. The British attacked at Pike's Ford and withdrew. Asa's knowledge of the ford — the gravel trough, the submerged log under the alders — contributed to holding the line. The naval cannonade has stopped; he does not know what this means. He is alive; the ford is his; the day, at least this part of it, is over.

---

## New Question or Pressure Raised

What did the stopping of the naval cannonade mean? Asa does not ask this in the chapter. He notes it and returns his attention to the ford. The answer comes in ch 16.

## Continuity Notes

- The British land attack historically began approximately 11 AM (continuity #12). Three crossing attempts were made: lower bridge (destroyed), upper bridge (contested), Pike's Ford. All were repulsed. This chapter renders the Pike's Ford attempt.
- Brisbane's brigade: the British force at Pike's Ford is part of Brisbane's brigade (see `canon/characters.md`). Brisbane himself is not seen; the British are a column, a presence, not individuated officers.
- The flank attempt via the willows: not an explicitly documented historical event; it is plausible given the described terrain and the general British pattern of probing. The drafter should treat it as fictional but consistent with the period military tactics.
- The submerged log: planted in ch 8 (Scene 1, Asa's ford knowledge conveyed to Cole). Paid off here.
- **Isaac Webb** appears briefly in the flank-covering moment (Cole sends him to the willows). He has been present but not central; consistent with his earlier characterization.
- **Solomon Veeder** is active during the exchange. He does not die here; the chapter does not require a named character death. If the drafter adds casualty detail among the unnamed militia, it should be brief and unornamented.
- The naval cannonade stopping: Asa perceives this absence. He does not know whether this is American victory or American defeat. He cannot know. Consistent with the rule that Asa has no information about the naval engagement except sound.
- The battle's historical end: the British began their retreat to Canada the following morning, September 12. Asa does not yet know this; it belongs to ch 16.
