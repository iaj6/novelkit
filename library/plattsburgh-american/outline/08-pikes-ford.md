# Chapter 08 — Pike's Ford

**One-line summary:** Asa arrives at the ford and walks the sergeant through every stone of it; the two-rank loading drill begins; by the end of the second day, Asa's musket shoots where he points it.

**POV:** Asa Beckwith  
**Tense:** Past  
**Function:** Approach 3

---

## Setting / Time

Pike's Ford, south bank of the Saranac River — approximately 1–3 miles upstream of Plattsburgh village. September 7–8, 1814.

Time span: Two days rendered in scene; the chapter stays at the ford for its entire duration.

---

## Chapter Shape

**Multi-scene** — two days at one location, rendered in four scenes: the arrival and assessment, the brush-cutting, the first night, the two-rank drill and its results. The location is the container.

---

## Ending Mode

**Mid-action / hard cut** — the chapter ends mid-drill: the sergeant has just called *"First rank, load"* for the fourth time, and the chapter cuts. The drill is not finished; the afternoon is not finished.

---

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

1. **Scene 1 — Arrival and assessment (Sept 7, midday):** Sergeant Cole marches the two companies up the south bank road from Plattsburgh, two hours' walk. Asa knows the road; he has walked it dozens of times to and from the mill. When they reach the ford he steps to the bank without being told and looks at the water. Late August water: clear over the gravel, running fast at the center, the current bending around the limestone shelf where the ford is shallowest. The north bank is brush and alder; the south bank is lower, with good ground for a firing line.
   
   The sergeant surveys the ford and then asks: *"Beckwith. You said you know this ford."* Asa says yes. The sergeant: *"Show me."* He walks Asa into the ford — they cross together, the water at knee height at the center, thigh-deep in one brief trough. The sergeant asks questions as they cross: where does the bottom shift? Where does the current pull a man sideways? Where would a loaded man — cartridge box, musket — lose his balance? Asa answers every question with precision: the limestone shelf is solid to the middle; then three yards of loose gravel that shifts underfoot; the current pulls to the left (south); at the right-hand edge of the ford, under the alders, there is a sinkhole — the bank is three feet deep there and silty. The sergeant nods at each answer. They cross and come back.
   
   On the south bank, the sergeant looks at the ford once more, then looks at Asa. He says: *"Anything else?"* Asa thinks. Then: *"In the willows, on the north side, twenty yards east. There's a submerged log. A man looking for another crossing would try there. He'd step on the log and it would roll."* The sergeant looks at the willows. *"Good,"* he says, and turns to the companies.

2. **Scene 2 — Cutting the firing lanes (Sept 7, afternoon):** The companies spend the afternoon cutting brush from the south bank, opening sight lines across the ford. Holt works efficiently with a camp axe; he is good in the woods. Asa works alongside him. The work is hard and quiet and smells of cut alder and green willow. By evening the south bank has three cleared positions: the main position at the ford's center, one to the right at the downstream edge, one to the left at the upstream edge.
   
   Asa looks at the ford from each position and gauges the shot distance. The center of the ford is perhaps thirty yards away. At thirty yards, a smoothbore musket is reliable. He notes this the way he would note a saw cut depth.

3. **Scene 3 — First night at the ford:** The cook fire is fifty yards upstream, sheltered behind a rise. The two companies eat and talk. Asa sits with Holt and a third man — introduce **Solomon Veeder**, about 40, a farmer from south of Plattsburgh, taciturn, a veteran of the '08 campaign (never said directly; it's in how he sits and how he drinks from a canteen). Holt and Veeder talk; Asa mostly listens. The talk is about the ford, about what the British will try, about whether the British are as good as people say. Veeder says, without drama: *"They're as good as people say."* Holt laughs; Veeder does not.
   
   Asa's watch begins at midnight. The ford at night: different sounds, different cold. He walks the south bank from the right-hand position to the left without a light, counting his steps. He already knows the distance without counting — he has walked this bank in the dark going to and from the mill his whole life — but he counts anyway, because it is useful to have the number fixed and certain.

4. **Scene 4 — Two-rank drill, second day (Sept 8):** The sergeant begins the loading drill after breakfast. The regular soldiers demonstrate first: two ranks, first rank fires, first rank falls back to reload while second rank advances one step and fires. The sequence is mechanical and fast; the regulars make it look like breathing.
   
   Then the militia tries it. It is bad. Webb (who has returned after Beekmantown and been reassigned here; brief reappearance) fumbles the sequence and fires while first rank is still in front of him. The sergeant stops the drill, says nothing to Webb, and repeats the demonstration.
   
   Asa pays attention to the sergeant's hands during the demonstration — specifically, the reload: bite the cartridge, pour the powder, prime the pan, close the frizzen, pour the powder down the barrel, ball in, patch, ram. Nine steps. *The same nine steps each time.* He thinks: it's like the ragwheel on the carriage — the same advance each time, one tooth, every upstroke. You don't think about whether to advance; you just advance.
   
   He drills the sequence until it is automatic, or nearly. When the sergeant corrects his musket cant — the stock is leaning left, the shot will go left — he adjusts it. Second shot: on. Third shot: on. The sergeant watches without expression.

---

## Outcome / Turn

Asa has given the sergeant every piece of ford knowledge he has. The sergeant has used it and found it worth having. The two-rank drill is becoming, if not reliable, at least possible. Asa's musket shoots where he points it.

---

## New Question or Pressure Raised

What will it be like when the British are in the water? The ford is known; the sequence is (mostly) learned; the question is what happens when the sequence must hold under fire, with men in the water across from them. This chapter doesn't reach for that question. It ends mid-drill, in the present.
