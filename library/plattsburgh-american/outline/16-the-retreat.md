# Chapter 16 — The Retreat

**One-line summary:** The British army retreats north on September 12; the ford is empty; Asa walks upstream along the river; the chapter ends in the sound of the river and no more.

**POV:** Asa Beckwith  
**Tense:** Past  
**Function:** Day-after

---

## Setting / Time

Pike's Ford and the south bank of the Saranac upstream, toward the mill. September 12, 1814 — from dawn to late morning.

Time span: A morning; approximately three to four hours.

---

## Chapter Shape

**Bottle** — one location (the ford and its immediate upstream stretch), a compressed morning. The chapter observes without commenting. It renders what is there and what is not there; it does not weigh what the previous day meant.

---

## Ending Mode

**Literary fade** — the chapter ends with the sound of the river. The British army has gone; the ford is empty; Asa is upstream, on familiar ground, and the river sounds the way it always sounds. The chapter fades on that sound.

---

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

*There is no dramatic goal. The beats are the beats of the morning after: what remains, what is quiet, what is changed and what is not.*

1. **Dawn at the ford, September 12:** Asa is at the ford at first light. He did not sleep — he was on watch and then the watch ended and there seemed no reason to sleep. The north bank is empty. The ford is empty.
   
   In the shallows downstream of the ford's center, there are things he does not look at directly. He notes them with peripheral attention and does not go there.

2. **The British encampment:** The abandoned British encampment is visible on the north bank — or its remnants. The fires are out. Equipment is scattered: a canteen here, a section of broken cartridge box, a blanket folded oddly against a root as if put there with care and then left. The army is gone; only its leavings remain. Asa does not cross the ford to look at them.

3. **The sound of the retreat:** From a mile or more to the west, then north, then northwest — the sound of the British army on the road. At first just a low, indistinct rumble. Then, for a few minutes, clearer: the beat of a drum, slow and plain (keeping step, not playing a march), then muffled again by distance and the sound of the river. An army walking north.
   
   Sergeant Cole is at the bank beside Asa, as he has been every morning this week. He watches the empty north bank. He says: *"They're going home."* He says it the way he would say: the river's running high. A fact. He turns and walks back toward the cook fire.

4. **The company disperses:** The two companies at the ford have no orders beyond holding the position. They hold it. Some men go back to sleep; they spread their blankets on the south bank behind the firing positions and sleep in the morning sun. Holt sits against a tree and sharpens his knife — not because it needs sharpening, but because it is something to do with his hands. Webb is at the fire, eating.
   
   Asa stands at the bank for a while. Then he walks upstream.

5. **The walk upstream:** He follows the south bank of the Saranac upstream. Not toward the mill — not consciously heading there, or not yet. Just walking, keeping the river on his left. The morning is clear; the September light is good on the water; the alders are beginning to go yellow at the tips.
   
   After a few hundred yards, he reaches a bend in the river where the bank rises and he can look back downstream and see the ford from above — from the angle he has never been, the soldier's angle, the angle that shows the ford's whole shape at once. He stops and looks at it.
   
   He can see why it was defensible. He can see the shelf and the trough, the alders on the north bank. He can see the cleared south bank where the firing positions are. He can see, from this angle, what he could not see from below: the way the willows make a false cover on the north side, the way the limestone shelf tilts slightly, making the crossing longer than it looks. He files this away. He does not know why.

6. **The thought of his father:** He stands at the bend and thinks of his father. Briefly, and obliquely: his father served at Sackett's Harbor and came home with a fever that did not leave. Asa stood at Pike's Ford. He does not compare these two things, or if he does, he does it without knowing the result. He turns south and begins walking back.

7. **Return to camp:** The cook fire; the sound of the company settling into the strange quiet of a morning with nothing to do. Holt has made coffee. Asa takes a cup and stands at the bank again and drinks it. The ford is quiet. The river is running.
   
   The sound of the British army on the road is gone. The river is the sound that remains, the same as it always was, the same as it was last week when he walked upstream to the mill in the evening and heard it going dark.

---

## Outcome / Turn

The British army is retreating. The ford held. Asa is here, on his river, with both hands and a cup of coffee. The morning is September 12, 1814, and that is all it is.

## New Question or Pressure Raised

None. The chapter does not reach forward. The book ends here.

## Continuity Notes

- The British retreat began September 12 (continuity #13). The army retreated to Canada; the retreat was reportedly rapid and somewhat disorderly (Prevost's reputation was badly damaged). From the ford, it would be audible as a retreating army on the road to the north and west.
- The things in the shallows: bodies from the previous day's action. The chapter does not describe them directly; Asa does not look at them directly. The prose uses peripheral acknowledgment — consistent with the style guide's instruction to describe without editorializing.
- Sergeant Cole's line ("They're going home"): four words, delivered as a fact. He does not add anything. This is the chapter's only gesture toward what the day before meant; the prose does not amplify it.
- The thought of Asa's father: this is the one permitted oblique surface of the theme (a man who served and came home to sickness; Asa who served and is standing here). It is brief, unanalyzed, and does not announce its weight. Consistent with `canon/style.md` on brief earned interiority.
- The ford seen from above, from the bend: this is Asa's final look at the place that defined these ten days. He stores it the way he stores the water-level mark, the saw-cut depth, the sound of the current at night. The prose does not say what it means.
- The book ends with the sound of the river. Not a manufactured image of significance — the river is the river, the sound is the sound he has known his whole life. The ending is in the register: this sound continues; it was here before and will be after; Asa is in it without being diminished by it. The chapter fades.
