# Chapter 06 — Beekmantown Road

**One-line summary:** The Clinton County militia breaks at first contact; Asa stays with Wool's rearguard through the fighting withdrawal; after it ends, the sergeant assigns him to Pike's Ford — his own river.

**POV:** Asa Beckwith  
**Tense:** Past  
**Function:** Approach 2

---

## Setting / Time

The Beekmantown road, north of Plattsburgh — from Beekmantown Corners through Culver Hill to Halsey's Corners — then the road back south to the Saranac line. September 6, 1814.

Time span: Dawn to late afternoon on a single day; the fighting withdrawal covers perhaps four or five hours.

---

## Chapter Shape

**Action-heavy** — the chapter's substance is physical movement and controlled fear; the fighting withdrawal is rendered moment-by-moment for its middle section. The day is one continuous unit of action. Some interiority is present but brief; the chapter moves.

---

## Ending Mode

**Turn** — after the withdrawal ends, the sergeant assigns Asa and two others to Pike's Ford. In the moment of receiving the assignment, Asa registers: that ford is three hundred yards downstream from his uncle's millpond. He knows every stone of it. The chapter ends on this fact, stated without elaboration. A fact has shifted the chapter's register.

---

## Goal, Obstacle, Action Beats

1. **Pre-dawn march north:** Asa's company — part of the combined Clinton County and Essex County levy under Mooers — marches north on the Beekmantown road in the dark before dawn. The road is flat, running between farms; the tree line is black against a dark sky. Holt is behind Asa in the column; Webb is two men ahead. The men talk in low voices. Nobody knows what is on the other end of the road. The sergeant — here first encountered directly; his name to be fixed in drafting, a regular-army noncommissioned officer from Wool's 29th Infantry, perhaps Sergeant **Elias Cole** — walks the column's right side without speaking. He has been with the levy since they arrived three days ago; this is the first time they have marched in column rather than drilled on the south bank.

2. **First contact north of Beekmantown Corners:** Before they reach the Corners, shots come from the front of the column — a scatter of musket fire, then a pause, then more. Then from the left flank, a sound Asa has never heard: a volley, many guns at once, a single crack that is many cracks pressed together. Then shouting from the front. Then the column's front begins to move backward — not marching, running. The men around Asa begin to go. His feet are already moving; he has taken two or three steps back before he is aware of it.

3. **The staying:** The sergeant materializes from the column's edge, standing still while the militia runs past him on both sides. He is not shouting. He is holding his musket at order arms and speaking in a conversational tone that somehow carries over the running feet: *"Those who are stopping, face north."* He says it twice, not louder the second time. Asa stops. Holt stops. Five or six others stop. Webb does not stop; Asa will not see Webb again until the camp at Plattsburgh that evening. The sergeant: *"Good. Follow the column through the Corners."*

4. **The fighting withdrawal:** For the next three hours, Asa exists in a series of specific actions that the sergeant dictates: *move to that fence, fire, reload, fall back to the oak, fire, reload, fall back.* The sequence is the structure; the structure is survival. Through Beekmantown Corners — where Asa fires his first shot at another human being (he sees a British soldier in the road, red coat, and shoots at him and does not see the result because he is already reloading) — to Culver Hill, where Captain Leonard's two 3-pounders are firing and the noise is larger and more physical than anything Asa has prepared for. The cannon shakes his chest. He loads behind a stone wall and tries to keep the cartridge from shaking in his hands.
   
   The British do not break. They keep coming — not at a run, but in column, steadily. The withdrawal stays in order because the sergeant says when to move and where. Asa does what he is told. He is afraid in a particular way: not of dying but of losing the sequence. If he loses the sequence (fire, reload, fall back), he will not know what to do next.

5. **Halsey's Corners:** The withdrawal stops. The British advance checks — partly because of the 3-pounders (which have been firing grape at the column), partly because they are far enough south now that pushing further costs them. The American position firms. Silas is at a fence rail; his hands are shaking. He counts his remaining cartridges: three. The sergeant walks behind the line and says, once, without inflection: *"Stand easy."*
   
   Asa looks at the men around him. Holt is there, loading slowly and carefully. Five or six of the original thirty or forty who started at the Corners. The rest are south of here, on the road to Plattsburgh, running or walked.

6. **Return south:** The rearguard falls back in order to the Saranac line; the British do not follow to the river. The march back is mostly silent. The sergeant walks with them. Asa's legs are steady now; the shaking passed about a mile back. He is aware that he is hungry.

7. **Back in camp — the assignment:** In camp south of the Saranac, the sergeant speaks with an officer (seen from Asa's perspective as a blue coat and epaulettes; not identified by name). Then the sergeant comes to Asa, and to Holt, and to one other man from the levy — a name Asa doesn't know yet, from a farm south of Beekmantown — and tells them: *"You three are going to Pike's Ford. Upstream. You'll have two companies."*
   
   Asa asks where Pike's Ford is. The sergeant says: *"Three miles upstream, west of the road. Shallow ford, bad ground on the north bank for a formed column."* 
   
   Asa hears this and says: *"I know that ford."* He says it without drama because it is a fact. His uncle's millpond is upstream of it by perhaps three hundred yards. He has crossed that ford forty times, in high water and low, in September and in April. He knows the exact stone that marks the shallowest crossing. He knows where the bottom shifts underfoot.
   
   The sergeant looks at him for a moment. Then: *"Good."*

---

## Outcome / Turn

The fighting withdrawal at Beekmantown has ended. Asa stayed when most of his county ran. He has been assigned to Pike's Ford — the exact ford that is his home water. The knowledge he has always had about that place has become, suddenly, the thing the war needs from him.

---

## New Question or Pressure Raised

What will the British do at the ford, and when? Not yet — that question belongs to later chapters. This chapter's turn is simply: the right man in the right place, arrived at by the accident of a fighting withdrawal.

## Continuity Notes

- Introduces **Sergeant Elias Cole** (name provisional; may be established in drafting; should be a noncommissioned officer from Wool's 29th U.S. Infantry or a similar regular regiment). He is not a POV character; he is seen entirely through Asa's observation. He will appear in chs 8, 10, 12, 14. His characterization: methodical, economical with language, demands competence, does not waste emotion.
- **Isaac Webb** breaks and runs; this is not judgment — most of the militia did. He returns to camp. His reappearance in ch 8 is brief; he is reassigned elsewhere in the levy.
- **Rufus Holt** stays; he is assigned to the ford with Asa. Consistent through subsequent chapters.
- The Beekmantown action is historically accurate: approximately 700 militia and 250 regulars vs. approximately 4,000 British; most of the militia broke; Wool's rearguard held and fought back to Halsey's Corners. See `canon/research.md` (cite:06, cite:17).
- Captain Leonard's 3-pounders at Culver Hill: historically accurate; the two guns were a meaningful factor in the fighting withdrawal.
- Pike's Ford: identified in `canon/glossary.md` as approximately 1–3 miles upstream of Plattsburgh village. The uncle's mill is 3 miles upstream, so the ford is between the mill and the village, making Asa's knowledge of it entirely plausible.
- The sergeant's word "good" at the end of Scene 7: the same word he will use in ch 10 ("you waited. Good."). This is not announced; it is a recurring tic of a man who praises by understatement.
