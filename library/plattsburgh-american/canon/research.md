## Section 1: September 6–11, 1814 — Day-by-Day Timeline

### August 31 – September 5: The March South

Sir George Prevost's army departed Montreal approximately August 31 and marched south through Lower Canada and into New York State. The force numbered roughly 10,000–11,000 effectives in the van, with the full army estimated at 14,000 [cite:01]. The advance elements reached Champlain, NY, and engaged American outposts in skirmishing actions at Chazy on approximately September 4 [cite:05]. American forces under Brigadier General Alexander Macomb conducted delaying actions before falling back toward Plattsburgh.

### September 6: Beekmantown

This is the day Wool's delaying action occurs.

Macomb ordered Major John E. Wool (29th U.S. Infantry) north with 250 regulars to join the militia on the Beekmantown road. The combined American force included approximately 700 militia from Clinton and Essex Counties under Major General Benjamin Mooers [cite:17]. British strength in the right column was approximately 4,000 troops.

The first skirmish occurred at dawn north of Beekmantown Corners. Most of the militia broke at first contact, but a portion remained with Wool and conducted a "fighting retreat" — turning, firing, and falling back — through Beekmantown Corners to Culver Hill, and ultimately to Halsey's Corners [cite:06]. At 8 AM, Captain Leonard arrived with two 3-pounder guns; when a half-mile-long column of British troops approached Halsey's Corners, Wool ordered the guns fired into the column, "cutting down whole ranks." A subsequent British bayonet charge forced the Americans to complete their retreat across the Saranac River bridge into Plattsburgh village [cite:06].

**American casualties (September 6):** approximately 45 killed and wounded, including men killed while removing bridge planks under fire [cite:06]. **British casualties (September 6):** approximately 240 killed and wounded [cite:06]. These figures are from a secondary/commemorative source and should be treated with caution — see "Disputed Points" below.

Upon crossing, Macomb ordered the planking removed from the bridge (at today's Bridge Street crossing) and formed into a breastwork to defend the south bank [cite:01].

### September 7: First Saranac Standoff

Prevost deployed his army on the north bank of the Saranac and began constructing batteries and artillery positions. Tentative attempts to force the river were made but repulsed by Macomb's regulars [cite:01]. The British occupied houses along the north bank near the lower bridge and used them as firing positions; the Americans responded with hot shot, eventually burning approximately 15 buildings including private homes, stores, the courthouse, and the jail [cite:05].

### September 9: Night Raid

Captain George McGlassin led approximately 50 Americans in a night raid that destroyed a British Congreve rocket battery approximately 500 yards from the American fortifications, without American casualties [cite:01]. This is the clearest datum for offensive American action during the standoff days.

### September 10: Wind Delay

The northeast wind prevented the British naval squadron from moving. Prevost held his army in place. The prevailing northeast wind would shift to a direction favorable to Downie's approach the following morning [cite:01].

### September 11: The Battle

**Naval engagement, ~9:00 AM:** The British squadron rounded Cumberland Head into Plattsburgh Bay at approximately 9 AM [cite:01]. Macdonough's account says the British fleet "anchored in a line ahead, at about three hundred yards distance" from his ships [cite:04]. The British began the firing. One source gives the naval action start as 9 AM; others suggest closer to 8:30 AM [cite:05]. The engagement lasted approximately two and a half hours [cite:08] — ending between 11 and 11:30 AM.

**Land attack ordered, ~10 AM:** Prevost ordered the land assault approximately one hour after the naval battle had begun — a coordination failure noted by British commanders who felt the army should have attacked simultaneously [cite:01]. By then the naval battle's outcome was already being decided.

**Brisbane's feint at the lower bridge:** Brigadier General Thomas Brisbane's brigade made a feint attack at the Saranac bridges in town. The feint was "easily repulsed" [cite:01].

**Robinson's brigade and Pike's Ford:** Major General Frederick Robinson's brigade was supposed to cross the Saranac at Pike's Ford, approximately 1.5–3 miles above Plattsburgh (sources vary in measurement — see Section 7), to attack Macomb's left flank. Robinson's column was "misdirected by some British staff officers and missed the ford which was their objective" [cite:01]. After relocating the ford, Robinson's light infantry drove the American defenders back and crossed to the south bank, forming in battle order. At this moment a messenger arrived from Prevost ordering the attack cancelled upon news of the naval defeat [cite:05]. Robinson's brigade had actually formed on the south bank in battle order before being called back — a fact that shocked the Peninsular veterans, who believed they could have taken the American works [cite:01].

**Naval battle resolution:** After approximately 2 hours and 5 minutes of fighting [cite:03], *Confiance* was reduced to a wreck — some 105 to 300 round shot in her hull [cite:03]. Captain Downie had been killed in the opening minutes (see Section 3). Lieutenant Robertson could not replicate Macdonough's winding maneuver (see Section 4) and surrendered. *Linnet* (Pring) surrendered shortly after. *Chubb* and *Finch* had already been taken earlier in the action. The British gunboats largely escaped [cite:01].

**British retreat:** After dark on September 11, Prevost ordered the army's withdrawal northward toward Canada [cite:01]. The retreat was rapid and disorderly; large quantities of ammunition and stores were abandoned in Plattsburgh [cite:05].

### September 12: Aftermath

The British army marched north. During the retreat at least 234 soldiers deserted — the majority from the Régiment de Meuron, the 2/8th Foot, and the 1/27th Foot [cite:01]. British land battle casualties for the entire siege period (September 6–11): 37 killed, 150 wounded, 57 missing [cite:01]. American regular army casualties: 37 killed, 62 wounded, 20 missing; overall land losses including militia estimated at 115 killed, 130 wounded [cite:01].

Burial of naval dead took place on Crab Island. Two plank hospitals had been established there before the battle; Dr. James Mann amputated more than 30 limbs following the engagement and worked without adequate medical support [cite:09]. Simeon Doty (a civilian teenager, not a soldier) helped bury the dead on Crab Island: "The dead were carried off southward and were buried in trenches without coffins. Redcoats and bluecoats were put in together" [cite:09]. Some 150 British, Canadian, and American men are buried in unmarked graves on Crab Island [cite:01]. Dead from the battle were described as "broken up and smashed up a great deal" by artillery [cite:09].

Naval prisoners — including Pring, Robertson, and the surviving officers of the squadron — were held by the Americans pending formal exchange or parole, as was standard War of 1812 practice. The British naval court martial for Pring and the surviving officers was held August 18–21, 1815 aboard HMS *Gladiator* at Portsmouth; the officers were fully acquitted, the standard finding when defeat arose from circumstances beyond officers' control [cite:01]. British army prisoners: three officers and 31 other ranks of the 76th Foot were captured during the siege [cite:01].

## Section 2: American Naval Squadron

### *Saratoga* (flag, Master Commandant Thomas Macdonough)

- **Type:** Corvette (also termed "small frigate" by some sources)
- **Dimensions:** 143 ft long, 36 ft 6 in beam, 12 ft 6 in draft, 734 tons displacement [cite:02]
- **Crew:** 212 officers and men [cite:02]
- **Armament:** 8 long 24-pounder guns; 6 × 42-pounder carronades; 12 × 32-pounder carronades [cite:02]
- **Keel laid:** March 7, 1814 [cite:02]
- **Launched:** April 11, 1814 — "the thirty-fifth day after her Keel was laid, and all her Timber taken from the Stump" (Macdonough to Secretary Jones, April 11, 1814) [cite:07]
- **Builder:** Adam and Noah Brown of New York City [cite:02]
- **Location:** Otter Creek (Vergennes, Vermont), below the falls [cite:07]
- **September 11 casualties:** 28 killed, 29 wounded (per Macdonough's after-action letter) [cite:04]
- **Damage received:** approximately 55 round shot in the hull [cite:04]

The *Saratoga*'s eight long 24-pounders were distributed along the gun deck, four to a broadside. **Inference (not in sources):** The long 24-pounders formed the primary long-range battery, while the carronades were reserved for close action — consistent with standard American practice of the period.

### *Eagle* (Lieutenant Robert Henley)

- **Type:** Brig
- **Tonnage:** ~500 tons; crew 150 [cite:01]
- **Armament:** 8 × 18-pounder long guns; 12 × 32-pounder carronades [cite:01]
- **Launched:** August 11, 1814 — approximately 19 days after keel-laying [cite:07]
- **Position in line:** Northernmost of the four major vessels (i.e., first to encounter the British squadron rounding Cumberland Head) [cite:01]

### *Ticonderoga* (Lieutenant Stephen Cassin)

- **Type:** Schooner (converted from a merchant steamboat hull; steam power deemed unreliable) [cite:07]
- **Tonnage:** ~350 tons; crew 112 [cite:01]
- **Armament:** 4 × 18-pounder long guns; 8 × 12-pounder long guns; 5 × 32-pounder carronades [cite:01]
- **Position in line:** Third, south of *Saratoga*

### *Preble* (sloop)

- **Tonnage:** ~80 tons; crew 30 [cite:01]
- **Armament:** 7 × 9-pounder long guns [cite:01]
- **Position in line:** Southernmost, at the shallow end near Crab Island [cite:01]

### Ten Gunboats (Row Galleys)

Named vessels: *Borer*, *Centipede*, *Nettle*, *Allen*, *Viper*, *Burrows*, *Wilmer*, *Ludlow*, *Aylwin*, *Ballard* [cite:01]

Six of the ten were built at Vergennes by Noah Brown under a February 1814 Navy Department contract; each approximately 75 ft long, mounting 2 large cannons [cite:07][cite:08]. Crew per gunboat: 26–40 [cite:01].

### Vergennes as Shipyard

Vergennes was chosen for its industrial infrastructure: "eight forges, two furnaces, a wire factory, a rolling mill, gristmills, and sawmills" plus access to bog iron ore from Monkton, Vermont [cite:08]. Noah Brown arrived with more than 100 shipwrights; a second team under Adam Brown brought approximately 200 more for the later work [cite:07]. The shipyard was defended by Fort Cassin at the mouth of Otter Creek, which repelled a British raid in May 1814 [cite:07].

### Total American Squadron

14 warships, 2,264 tons total, 882 crew [cite:01].

## Section 3: British Naval Squadron

### *Confiance* (Captain George Downie, flag)

- **Type:** Fifth-rate frigate (36 guns as rated; 37–39 guns actually mounted)
- **Dimensions:** 147 ft 5 in long, 37 ft 2 in beam; largest warship ever to sail on Lake Champlain [cite:03]
- **Crew:** approximately 270–325 (sources conflict; see "Disputed Points") [cite:01][cite:03]
- **Armament:** Sources conflict — Admiralty records vs. prize accounts disagree significantly. Wikipedia gives: 30 × 24-pounder guns + 6 × 32-pounder carronades + 1 × 24-pounder pivot. As captured: 27 × 24-pounder guns, 2 × 18-pounder guns, 4 × 32-pounder carronades, 6 × 24-pounder carronades [cite:03].
- **Built:** Île aux Noix naval yard on the Richelieu River, Quebec [cite:03]
- **Launched:** August 25, 1814 — less than three weeks before the battle [cite:03]
- **State at battle:** Still unfinished; workers still completing rigging and carpentry tasks when she sailed [cite:03]
- **Manning:** Crew assembled largely from untrained provincial militia; a company of the 39th Foot was embarked as marines. Crew was "totally unknown either to the Officers or to each other" (British post-action report, cited in secondary source) [cite:03]. Captain Downie had written to Commodore James Lucas Yeo on Lake Ontario that Confiance could not be ready with an exercised crew before September 15 [cite:03].
- **Downie's death:** Killed approximately 15 minutes into the action. He was standing behind a long 24-pounder sighting it when an American round shot struck the gun's muzzle, dismounting the 2,000-pound cannon from its carriage; the gun fell on top of him and crushed him to the deck. He died instantly. An eyewitness noted: "His skin was not broken, a black mark about the size of a small plate was the only visible injury. His watch was found flattened, with its hands pointing to the very second at which he received the fatal blow" [cite:03].
- **Robertson's command:** After Downie's death and the death of the first lieutenant, Lt. James Robertson took command. He attempted to wind ship but "succeeded only in presenting the vulnerable stern to the American fire" [cite:03]. Surrendered after approximately 2 hours and 5 minutes of battle [cite:03].
- **Damage:** Approximately 105 round shot (Macdonough's estimate) to 250–300 (prize master Daniel Records' count) in the hull [cite:03]. Casualties: 41 killed (including Downie), 83 wounded [cite:03].

### *Linnet* (Captain Daniel Pring)

- **Type:** Brig; ~350 tons; crew 125 [cite:01]
- **Armament:** 16 × 18-pounder long guns (per Wikipedia; sources disagree on exact figure) [cite:01]
- **Action:** Surrendered to Macdonough after *Confiance* struck. Pring was captured; the court martial finding was full acquittal [cite:01].

### *Chubb* (sloop)

- **Tonnage:** ~112 tons; crew 50 [cite:01]
- **Armament:** 1 × 6-pounder long gun, 10 × 18-pounder carronades [cite:01]
- **Action:** Captured early in the engagement (details in Section 4).

### *Finch* (sloop)

- **Tonnage:** ~110 tons; crew 50 [cite:01]
- **Armament:** 4 × 6-pounder long guns, 7 × 18-pounder carronades [cite:01]
- **Action:** Captured; ran aground near Crab Island and was taken by the invalid battery there [cite:01].

### Twelve Gunboats

Most escaped after the squadron's defeat. Armament: various (1 × 24-pounder to 1 × 18-pounder long guns, carronades) [cite:01].

### Total British Squadron

16 warships, 2,402 tons, ~937 crew [cite:01]. On paper heavier and larger than the American squadron; in practice the British were hampered by the untrained crew of *Confiance* and the lack of exercised gun crews.

## Section 4: Macdonough's Preparation and the Winding Maneuver

### Anchoring Position

Macdonough moored his vessels in a north-south line across the entrance to Plattsburgh Bay (Cumberland Bay), running from near Cumberland Head to the shallows off Crab Island. The position was close to the Vermont shore and Crab Island, so the line could not be flanked [cite:01]. Order from north to south: *Eagle*, *Saratoga*, *Ticonderoga*, *Preble* [cite:01].

### Spring and Kedge System

Each vessel was fitted with both bow and stern anchors, with "springs" — hawsers attached to the anchor cables — that allowed the ship to be rotated in a wide arc without making sail. The *Saratoga* additionally had kedge anchors laid out from the quarters (the after corners of the ship), astern and to the sides [cite:01]. Macdonough had the crews practice turning their ships so that alternately the starboard and port guns would face south toward the expected approach of the British [cite:02].

The purpose: if one broadside's guns were disabled, the ship could be "wound" (rotated 180°) to bring the undamaged broadside into action — without needing wind or way. This was a deliberate preparation, not an improvisation [cite:07].

### The Winding Maneuver (September 11)

After nearly two hours of fighting, *Saratoga*'s starboard battery was largely destroyed. Macdonough's own after-action account: "a stern anchor was let go, the bower cable cut, and the ship winded, with a fresh broadside on the enemy's ship" [cite:04]. In practice: the stern kedge anchor was let go, the bow cable was cut, and by hauling in on the kedge anchors Macdonough's crew spun the *Saratoga* approximately 180° to bring the undamaged port broadside to bear on *Confiance* [cite:02][cite:07].

The last serviceable starboard gun — a carronade — had broken loose from its carriage during the action [cite:02]. The winding was completed successfully.

*Confiance* attempted to replicate the maneuver but Lieutenant Robertson "succeeded only in presenting the vulnerable stern to the American fire" [cite:03]. This sealed the battle's outcome.

### Position and Distance

Macdonough's account says the British anchored "at about three hundred yards distance" from the American line [cite:04]. This placed the two flagships at a range well within effective carronade range (500 yards effective maximum) as well as long-gun range.

## Section 5: American Land Forces

### Regular Army (Macomb, BG)

Total regulars: approximately 1,500, described by Macomb as "mostly recruits, invalids, and detachments" [cite:01]. Despite their weakness, Macomb handled them skillfully.

**Regiments present at Plattsburgh:** The 6th, 13th, 15th, and 29th U.S. Infantry regiments are documented as present, along with detachments of other units [cite:01]. Seven currently active regular battalions perpetuate lineages of units at Plattsburgh. Macomb noted in his official report that he had "four companies of the Sixth Regiment" [secondary citation, not directly read].

**Key officers:**
- **Major John E. Wool** (29th U.S. Infantry): commanded the Beekmantown delaying action and the defense of the Saranac crossings. Age at battle: 26. Became a significant general in the Mexican War and Civil War [cite:01].
- **Major Daniel Appling:** commanded approximately 110 riflemen [cite:01].
- **Captain George McGlassin:** led the September 9 night raid [cite:01]. (Note: McGlassin was later court-martialed in 1818 for harsh treatment of soldiers — irrelevant to 1814 but a marker of character [cite:09].)
- **Major Joseph Gilbert Totten:** designed the fortifications (engineer) [cite:01].

**Fortifications:** Three redoubts (Forts Brown, Moreau, and Scott) and two blockhouses on the south bank, linked by fieldworks. An "invalid battery" on Crab Island was manned by sick and recovering soldiers [cite:01]. The fortifications were designed specifically for the south-bank defensive line of the Saranac.

**General Izard's role (earlier):** Before the battle, General George Izard (who had commanded the forces at Plattsburgh) assigned free African Americans to build the forts. His white troops refused to work alongside the Black laborers, and Izard sought guidance from the War Department [cite:09]. Izard's force was subsequently redirected westward, leaving Macomb with the skeleton force that fought the battle.

### New York Militia (Mooers, MG)

**Major General Benjamin Mooers:** Born approximately 1758; a Revolutionary War veteran who had served as a lieutenant under his uncle Moses Hazen in the 2nd Canadian Regiment. Between the wars he was sheriff of Clinton County and a major landowner ("Chief Purchaser") in the Champlain Valley [cite:19]. Commissioned Major General of the Northern New York Militia district (including Clinton, Essex, Franklin, Warren, Washington, Rensselaer, and Columbia Counties) in 1811 [cite:19].

**Units at Plattsburgh:** The 36th New York State Militia (Azariah C. Flagg served in this regiment) and the 8th N.Y. Militia (Colonel Thomas Miller commanding) are specifically documented [cite:01]. General Mooers ordered militia to "harass [the British] day and night, and keep [them] in continual alarm" along the Saranac [cite:01].

**Militia numbers at Beekmantown:** approximately 700 from Clinton and Essex Counties [cite:17]. Overall militia at Plattsburgh during the battle: approximately 700 NY militia plus Vermont volunteers; some sources estimate up to 2,000 militia total, but many fled before or during the action [cite:01].

### Vermont Militia

Additional forces under Major General Samuel Strong (Vermont) were present at Plattsburgh; their specific strength is not precisely documented here [cite:01]. **Inference (not in sources):** Vermont militia were called across the lake after British forces entered New York territory. They played a limited combat role relative to the regulars.

### African American and Indigenous Participants

**Free Black laborers and soldiers:** Free African Americans built Forts Brown, Moreau, and Scott under General Izard; white troops refused to work alongside them [cite:09]. The broader context: New York State authorized regiments of Black soldiers during the war, and at least some free Black men served in the Lake Champlain theater [cite:20].

**Black sailors on American naval vessels:** Approximately one quarter of the crews at the Battle of Lake Erie (1813) were Black sailors [cite:01]; **Inference (not in sources):** A similar proportion likely served aboard Macdonough's Lake Champlain vessels, consistent with U.S. Navy practice of the period, which did not formally segregate by race.

**William Apess (Pequot):** A Pequot man born in 1798, Apess enlisted in the American regular army and served at Plattsburgh in 1814. In his 1829/1831 autobiography *A Son of the Forest*, he writes: "For six days and nights we did not leave our guns, and during that time the work of death paused not, as every day some shot took effect." This is a primary source account from an Indigenous soldier at Plattsburgh [cite:09].

## Section 6: British Land Forces

### Command Structure

- **Lieutenant General Sir George Prevost:** Commander-in-Chief; commanded the overall invasion. Relieved of command in Canada following Plattsburgh; demanded a court martial to clear his name but died in 1816 before it could convene [cite:01].
- **Major General de Rottenburg (Francis):** divisional commander [cite:01].

### Brigades and Regiments

**1st Brigade (Major General Frederick Philipse Robinson):**
- 3rd Battalion, 27th Foot (Inniskilling)
- 39th Foot (Dorsetshire)
- 76th Foot
- 88th Foot (Connaught Rangers)

The 88th (Connaught Rangers) had fought in the Peninsula under Wellington; they sailed from France after Napoleon's abdication and disembarked at Quebec in August 1814, reaching Plattsburgh directly [cite:01]. The entire 1st Brigade consisted of Peninsular War veterans who believed they could have taken the American works after crossing Pike's Ford, and who bitterly protested Prevost's retreat order [cite:01].

**2nd Brigade (Brigadier General Thomas Brisbane):**
- 2nd Battalion, 8th Foot
- 13th Foot
- 49th Foot (Royal Berkshire)
- Régiment de Meuron (Swiss mercenary regiment in British service)
- Canadian Voltigeurs
- Canadian Chasseurs

Brisbane's brigade made the feint attack at the lower Saranac bridges on September 11 [cite:01].

**3rd Brigade (Major General Manley Power):**
- 3rd Foot (Buffs / Royal East Kent)
- 5th Foot (Royal Northumberland Fusiliers)
- 1st Battalion, 27th Foot
- 58th Foot (Northamptonshire)

Power's brigade supported Robinson's attack on the right flank [cite:01].

**Supporting arms:**
- One squadron, 19th Light Dragoons
- Siege train: 2 × 24-pounder brass field guns, 1 × 8-inch brass howitzer, 3 × 24-pounder naval carronades on field carriages
- Congreve rocket detachment [cite:01]

Each brigade had a battery of five 6-pounder guns and one 5.5-inch howitzer [cite:01].

### September 11 Land Battle Sequence

1. **Brisbane's feint** at the lower bridge crossings — repulsed; plan required this to pin American attention while Robinson turned the flank [cite:01].
2. **Robinson's march to Pike's Ford** — misdirected by staff officers; found ford with delay; eventually crossed, formed battle line on south bank [cite:01][cite:05].
3. **Retreat ordered** — messenger reached Robinson after he had crossed; Robinson withdrew despite believing victory was achievable in "twenty minutes" [cite:05].

### British Retreat (September 12)

The army marched north in haste, abandoning ammunition, stores, and equipment in Plattsburgh. At least 234 soldiers deserted — predominantly from Régiment de Meuron (a unit of mixed European nationalities susceptible to desertion), 2/8th Foot, and 1/27th Foot [cite:01]. British land battle total casualties for September 6–11: 37 killed, 150 wounded, 57 missing [cite:01]. Three officers and 31 other ranks of the 76th Foot were captured [cite:01].

## Section 7: The Saranac River Line

### The River and Its Approaches

The Saranac River ran west-to-east through Plattsburgh village before emptying into Cumberland Bay. The south bank formed the American defensive line; the north bank was held by the British during the standoff (September 7–10).

### Lower Bridge (Downtown Plattsburgh)

Located at the present-day Bridge Street crossing [cite:05]. In 1814 this was the main road crossing into the village from the north. When the Americans retired across the river on September 6, they "tore up the planks from the bridge"; Macomb then ordered the planking formed into a breastwork on the south bank [cite:01]. The Americans defended the crossing from a stone gristmill on the south bank's eastern approach [cite:05]. British snipers occupied houses on the north bank; American hot shot set these on fire, eventually destroying approximately 15 buildings [cite:05]. The feint attack by Brisbane's brigade on September 11 was directed at this crossing.

### Upper Bridge

An upper bridge crossing also existed above the lower bridge in town [cite:05]. **Inference (not in sources):** This was likely a secondary road crossing perhaps a half-mile or mile above the lower bridge. The exact location is not precisely fixed in the sources consulted.

### Pike's Ford

Located approximately 1.5 to 3 miles above Plattsburgh (sources disagree on measurement baseline — see "Disputed Points" below). Near the site of "Pike's Cantonment," the winter encampment established by Colonel Zebulon Pike in 1812–13 for approximately 2,000 soldiers; the cantonment was burned by British troops in summer 1813 [cite:01]. Robinson's brigade used this ford on September 11.

The brief specifies "three miles upstream of Plattsburgh village" for Asa's uncle's mill — this is consistent with the ford being at or near this location, since an uncle's sawmill 3 miles up the Saranac and the ford used by Robinson's brigade are at approximately the same distance from the village.

**Disputed Point:** Wikipedia says the ford was "3 miles (4.8 km) above Macomb's defenses"; mywarof1812.com says "one and a half miles above Plattsburgh." Pike's Cantonment was "a mile and a half above Plattsburgh" per one source [cite:01]; another says "three miles" [cite:05]. These may be measuring from different reference points (the town center vs. the outer fortifications). **Inference (not in sources):** The ford most likely lay between 1.5 and 3 miles above the village center, consistent with all sources.

## Section 8: Naval Gunnery — Quarter-Gunner Role and Gun Crew Procedure

### The Quarter-Gunner

In the United States Navy, one quarter-gunner was assigned for every four guns — identical to British practice [cite:13]. The quarter-gunner was a petty officer who served under the gunner's mates. His responsibilities were the **maintenance and operation** of his assigned guns: keeping them ready for action, overseeing their training and elevation during exercise, ensuring the supply of powder and shot, and managing the gun crew during battle [cite:13].

**For the novel:** Silas has "charge of a section of the starboard battery — four long twenty-fours." This is exactly the quarter-gunner's role — one gun section per quarter-gunner, precisely four guns. He would know the guns intimately: their quirks, their tackles, the way each one ran out.

The quarter-gunner was not the gun captain of any individual gun (the gun captain aimed and fired); he was the petty officer responsible for a cluster of four. **Inference (not in sources):** During action, the quarter-gunner would move among his four guns, directing, correcting, supplying, and replacing casualties as needed.

### Gun Crew Positions (Period Nomenclature)

For a long 24-pounder, a full crew ran to approximately 14 men [cite:14]:

- **No. 1 / Gun Captain:** Commands the gun; aims using handspikes and the quoin (wedge); fires via flintlock ("gunlock") or slow match on a linstock
- **No. 2 / Second Captain:** Assists; backs up No. 1; often handles priming
- **No. 3 / Loader (left side):** Receives and loads the cartridge and shot
- **No. 4 / Sponger (right side):** Operates the sponge rod and rammer alternately; both sponging the barrel and ramming the charge
- **No. 5 / Assistant Loader (left)**
- **No. 6 / Assistant Sponger (right)**
- **Nos. 7–8 / Handspike men:** Move gun laterally for aiming using handspikes
- **Nos. 9–10 and additional men:** Haul on the side tackles and train tackle to run the gun in and out
- **Powder monkey:** A boy (typically age 10–14) who carried powder cartridges from the magazine in a cartridge box; ran between the magazine and the gun deck. Powder was kept in flannel cartridges to reduce fire risk [cite:14].

Carronades (the 42-pounder and 32-pounder guns on *Saratoga*) required smaller crews than long guns due to their lighter weight and simpler mounting (a sliding carriage rather than a wheeled carriage with tackles) [cite:14].

### Firing Sequence

Period practice, applicable to both long guns and carronades [cite:14]:

1. **Sponge the barrel** — wet swab mops out the bore to extinguish any burning embers from the previous charge; failure to sponge could cause a premature ignition of the next charge
2. **Load powder cartridge** — a flannel cartridge (or occasionally a paper/parchment cartridge) inserted into the muzzle by the loader and rammed home
3. **Insert wad** — cloth wad (canvas and old rope) rammed home atop the powder to keep the charge seated
4. **Load shot** — the cannonball inserted and rammed home
5. **Insert retaining wad** — a second wad atop the shot to prevent the ball from rolling out if the muzzle depresses
6. **Run out** — men heave on the gun tackles until the carriage is hard against the bulwark; the barrel protrudes through the gun port. A large gun carriage could total over two tons [cite:14].
7. **Pierce and prime** — the cartridge is pierced through the touch hole with a priming wire; the touch hole is then primed with fine gunpowder (or a pre-filled quill)
8. **Aim** — gun captain and handspike men adjust train (horizontal angle) and elevation using the quoin (wedge) beneath the breech
9. **Fire** — gun captain fires via the flintlock mechanism; the gun recoils inboard against the breeching tackle

The gun is then "bowsed up" (hauled back to its inboard position for reloading) and the cycle repeats.

### Rate of Fire

A well-exercised crew could fire 2–3 rounds per 5 minutes [cite:14]. An inexperienced crew — such as *Confiance*'s — would fire far slower and with worse accuracy. British post-action accounts noted that *Confiance*'s gunners failed to reset their gun wedges (quoins) after each shot, causing the guns to lose elevation progressively [cite:03].

### Gunnery Vocabulary (Period American Usage)

Key period terms that belong in the manuscript [cite:14]:
- **Breeching tackle:** Heavy rope or chain absorbing recoil
- **Gun tackle / train tackle:** Lines used to run gun out or traverse
- **Quoin:** Wooden wedge under the breech, adjusted for elevation
- **Handspike:** Long wooden bar for traversing (rotating) the gun
- **Linstock:** Staff holding slow match, used to fire gun if gunlock fails
- **Gunlock:** Flintlock mechanism on the vent for firing
- **Priming wire:** Metal spike pushed through the vent to pierce the cartridge
- **Cartridge box:** Container in which the powder monkey carried the flannel cartridge
- **Magazine:** Below-decks space where powder was stored; copper tools only (no iron = no sparks)
- **Worm:** Corkscrew tool for removing a misfire charge from the bore
- **Run out / run in:** Commands to push the gun to the port or haul it inboard

## Section 9: Infantry Drill Vocabulary — U.S. Regular and Militia

### Governing Manual

From March 30, 1812, by presidential order, Alexander Smyth's *Regulations for the Field Exercise, Manœuvres, and Conduct of the Infantry of the United States* replaced the Steuben *Blue Book* as the official U.S. Army infantry manual. Smyth's *Regulations* were published in Philadelphia in 1812 by Fry and Kammerer [cite:note]. The work was an abridgment of a French 1791 tactical system adapted for both regular and militia use [cite:note].

The militia typically trained to the same manual but with less consistency and less regular drill. Wool's regulars would have been drilled to Smyth; the Clinton County militia would have had varying levels of adherence — some companies well-drilled, many not.

**Note:** The Smyth manual PDF was not readable in the fetching tool; command sequences below are drawn from period military knowledge and general secondary material, and should be flagged as partially inferred.

### Infantry Organization (Two-Rank System)

- **Company:** Basic tactical unit; 8–10 subdivisions (sections), commanded by a captain
- **Battalion/Regiment:** Multiple companies; commanded by a colonel or lieutenant colonel
- **Platoon fire:** Companies or sections fired in sequence to maintain continuous fire
- **Two ranks:** U.S. (and French/British) infantry stood in two ranks to fire; first rank fired, second rank fired over the first rank's heads or stepped through

### Key Commands (Period Vocabulary)

From Smyth's system (partially inferred from standard period sources [cite:note]):
- **"Make ready"** — bring musket to half-cock; prepare to load or fire
- **"Present"** — raise to the shoulder, bring to full cock, aim
- **"Fire"** — pull trigger; discharge
- **"Load"** — begin the loading sequence (bite cartridge, pour powder in pan, load ball and cartridge into barrel, ram)
- **"Prime and load"** — combined instruction
- **"Fix bayonets"** — self-explanatory
- **"Charge bayonets"** — level bayonets forward at the enemy; used in the British advance at Halsey's Corners on September 6

### Militia Distinctions

New York militia in 1814 was organized into brigades and regiments under Governor Tompkins's wartime organization. Mooers commanded the 2nd Division (or northern division), which included the regiments of Franklin, Clinton, Essex, Warren, Washington, Rensselaer, and Columbia Counties [cite:19]. Individual militia companies were called up by county; the men of Clinton County had varying levels of training — some steady, some prone to break under fire (as they did at Beekmantown on September 6) [cite:17].

Militia rank terminology: general officers (Major General, Brigadier General), colonel, lieutenant colonel, major, captain, lieutenant, ensign, and sergeant. Non-commissioned officers: sergeant, corporal.

## Section 10: Contemporary Accounts

### Macdonough's After-Action Letter (September 13, 1814)

Addressed to Secretary of the Navy William Jones. Written from USS *Saratoga* off Plattsburgh [cite:04]:

> "The Almighty has been pleased to grant us a signal victory on Lake Champlain, in the capture of one frigate, one brig, and two sloops of war, of the enemy."

> "Sir: I have the honor to give you the particulars of the action which took place on the 11th instant on this lake. For several days the enemy were on their way to Plattsburg by land and water, and it being understood that an attack would be made at the same time by their land and naval forces, I determined to await at anchor the approach of the latter."

> "At 9, he anchored in a line ahead, at about three hundred yards distance from my" [ships].

> [Describing the winding maneuver:] "a stern anchor was let go, the bower cable cut, and the ship winded, with a fresh broadside on the enemy's ship."

Macdonough reported: total American casualties 52 killed and 58 wounded; *Saratoga* alone: 28 killed and 29 wounded; Lieutenant Peter Gamble killed early in the action [cite:04]. British losses estimated at 260 killed and wounded in the captured vessels [cite:04].

The letter's opening phrase — "The Almighty has been pleased to grant us a signal victory" — appeared in newspapers across the country within days. Macdonough's note to Macomb earlier on the same day reads: "Will you if possible send us Surgeons, we are much in want of them. 'The Almighty has been pleased to Grant us Victory'" [cite:founders-note].

### Macomb's Reports

Macomb wrote a series of letters during and after the battle. In a letter to his father, he stated American land losses were "115 killed and 130 wounded" [cite:01]. A letter from Macomb to Madison exists in the Founders Online archive, dated September 11, 1814 [cite:01]. The full text of Macomb's official after-action report to the Secretary of War was addressed from Plattsburgh and dispatched after the British retreat; it was subsequently published in *Official Correspondence with the Department of War* (William Fry, Philadelphia, 1816) [cite:01].

### J.C. Hubbell (Civilian Observer)

The civilian Hubbell observed the naval battle from shore: "The firing was terrific, fairly shaking the ground, and so rapid that it seemed to be one continuous roar, intermingled with the spiteful flashing from the mouths of guns, and dense clouds of smoke soon hung over the two fleets" [cite:nps-lake].

### Simeon Doty (1886 Interview, Published 1964)

A teenager at the time, Doty gave an interview in 1886 recorded in *Recollections of Clinton County and the Battle of Plattsburgh: 1800–1840* (1964). He helped bury the dead on Crab Island:

> "We went to Crab Island. I helped bury the dead there… The dead were carried off southward and were buried in trenches without coffins. Redcoats and bluecoats were put in together."

He noted the dead were "broken up and smashed up a great deal" and that his brother fainted at the sight [cite:09]. Two plank hospitals stood on the north part of the island.

### William Apess (*A Son of the Forest*, 1829/1831)

A Pequot soldier who served at Plattsburgh in 1814. This is a primary source from an Indigenous participant [cite:09]:

> "For six days and nights we did not leave our guns, and during that time the work of death paused not, as every day some shot took effect."

He also described the spread of illness among common soldiers sleeping in open fields during the standoff.

### HMS *Confiance* Eyewitness (on Downie's death)

> "His skin was not broken, a black mark about the size of a small plate was the only visible injury. His watch was found flattened, with its hands pointing to the very second at which he received the fatal blow." [cite:03]

### British Court Martial (Pring, Robertson, et al.)

Held August 18–21, 1815 aboard HMS *Gladiator* at Portsmouth. The court fully acquitted Pring and the surviving officers — the standard finding when defeat arose from circumstances (Downie's death, the unready crew) beyond the surviving officers' control [cite:01]. The brief mentions "Captain Robertson's trial of *Confiance*" — note that Robertson was a lieutenant (the senior surviving officer), not a captain; the court martial covered all surviving officers of the squadron.

## Section 11: Race and Demography in the Champlain Valley, 1814

### Abenaki and Indigenous Peoples

The western Abenaki (an Algonquian people) had occupied the Champlain Valley since pre-contact times. By the early 19th century, most Abenaki had been displaced northward into Canada or retreated into the Adirondack mountains, where individuals such as Sabael and Mitchell Sabattis later became renowned wilderness guides [cite:middlebury]. Some Abenaki remained in the region as part of mixed communities [cite:middlebury].

**Inference (not in sources):** By 1814, the overt Indigenous presence in Clinton County was small and largely not visible to incoming militia and regular forces. Individual Indigenous people lived in or around the valley — as guides, trappers, or residents of mixed communities — but no organized Indigenous military force operated in the Plattsburgh theater in September 1814.

### Free Black Residents

Free African Americans lived throughout northern New York in 1814, some as substantial community members. The social reality of upstate New York was complicated: formally a free state (gradual emancipation underway since 1799; full emancipation for those born before 1799 would not come until 1827), but racial discrimination was pervasive in daily life [cite:20]. Izard's troops' refusal to work alongside free Black laborers building the Plattsburgh fortifications illustrates this [cite:09].

### Black Soldiers and Sailors

- **Soldiers:** Free Black men served in the American regular army and contributed to labor and combat roles at Plattsburgh [cite:20][cite:09]. No segregated Black regiment fought specifically at Plattsburgh in September 1814 (the promised "two regiments" were part of a broader wartime authorization and may not have materialized fully in the northern theater).
- **Sailors:** U.S. Navy crews were racially integrated by practice; approximately 10–25% of crews on Great Lakes and Atlantic vessels were Black sailors in this period [cite:20]. **Inference (not in sources):** Saratoga's crew of 212 almost certainly included Black sailors.

### Indigenous Soldiers

William Apess's service at Plattsburgh is documented [cite:09]; he was not the only Indigenous soldier in the American forces. The U.S. regular army did not formally exclude Indigenous men from service.

## Section 12: Saranac River Sawmill — Operation and Vocabulary

### Historical Context

The brief places Asa's uncle's sawmill on the Saranac River approximately three miles upstream of Plattsburgh village. The first specifically documented sawmill on the upper Saranac dates to the 1820s (Captain Pliny Miller's, circa 1827; Isaac McLenathan and Williams Wells at Franklin Falls, 1827) [cite:saranac-wiki]. **Inference (not in sources):** Sawmills were operating in the Champlain Valley well before 1814. The Saranac River offered multiple mill seats with adequate head (fall) for water power, and the lumber industry was already established in the region. An uncle's small sawmill approximately 3 miles up the Saranac in 1814 is historically plausible even without a specific documented mill at that exact location.

### Mill Type

By 1814, virtually all American sawmills were of the **sash (up-and-down) type**, in which a straight saw blade was mounted vertically in a wooden frame (the sash) and moved in reciprocating motion — cutting on the downstroke, with the log carriage advancing on the upstroke [cite:10][cite:12]. The circular saw was not in common use until the 1820s and later.

### Power System

Water from the mill pond was controlled by the **head gate** and directed through the **flume/penstock** to the **waterwheel** (typically a **flutter wheel** — an undershot or slightly-overshot water wheel — or in some later installations a **tub wheel**) [cite:10]. The rotating wheel shaft was connected via a **crank** and the **pitman arm** (a wood connecting rod) to the saw frame, converting rotary motion to reciprocating motion [cite:10][cite:12]. After passing the wheel, water drained away in the **tail race** [cite:10].

### The Saw and Carriage

The saw blade, approximately 6 feet long, was held in the sash frame by **stirrups** (metal loops) and **lug pins** [cite:10]. It cut by the stroke of the downward pass, with the log advancing on the upstroke. The log lay on the **log carriage**, a mobile wooden frame riding on wooden tracks (**ribbons** or **nogs**), advanced by the **ragwheel** (a ratchet/feed wheel) driven by the **evener** and **feeder** (hand-pole) from the saw frame's motion [cite:10].

The log was secured to the carriage by **bail dogs** — iron fastening devices whose teeth bit into the log ends [cite:10]. At the end of a board, the unsawed stub at the log end (the **stub shot**) was characteristic of sash sawmill production.

### Worker Roles and Tasks

- **Head sawyer:** Stood at the head block end; controlled the ragwheel and feed rate; set the thickness of the next board by adjusting the head block [cite:10]
- **Tail sawyer:** Stood at the tail block end; helped guide the log on the carriage [cite:10]
- **Millwright:** Built and maintained the mill machinery; a highly skilled trade. Asa is described as a "millwright's apprentice," which places him in the skilled-labor tier, learning the mechanical work of the mill rather than raw labor [cite:10]

Key hand tools:
- **Cant hook:** 4–5 foot wooden lever with a hinged iron spike, used to grip and rotate logs [cite:10]
- **Peavey:** Similar to the cant hook but with a fixed steel spike (pike) at the lower end; slightly different design for log work [cite:10]
- **Mill bar:** Long iron pry bar for moving logs and hammering in bail dogs [cite:10]
- **Whet:** To sharpen the saw with a file [cite:10]

### Daily Rhythm

A sash sawmill operating with adequate water and log supply might produce approximately 500 boards in a working day [cite:11]. Work was tied to water: if the pond level dropped (drought) or the water gate froze (winter), the mill stopped. Log drives on northern rivers typically came in spring; boards were stacked and seasoned through summer and fall [cite:11].

**Inference (not in sources):** A millwright's apprentice would have worked from well before dawn to after dark during peak season (late summer); tasks would include tending the gate and water level, repositioning logs, removing sawn boards, sharpening and adjusting the saw, clearing sawdust (churning), and assisting with mechanical maintenance. The physical environment would smell of fresh sawdust and water, and the constant reciprocating sound of the gate saw would fill the building.

### Period Sound and Texture

The sash sawmill produced a characteristic rhythmic sound: the slam of the saw frame at the top and bottom of its stroke, the slow advance of the carriage, the rattle of the ragwheel. Unlike the continuous sound of a circular saw, the sash mill had a definite beat — roughly 100–200 strokes per minute.

## Section 13: Routine Duty Aboard a U.S. Navy Corvette at Anchor in 1814

### Watch System

The naval day was divided into seven watches [cite:15]:

| Watch | Hours |
|-------|-------|
| Morning Watch | 4 AM – 8 AM |
| Forenoon Watch | 8 AM – 12 noon |
| Afternoon Watch | 12 noon – 4 PM |
| First Dog Watch | 4 PM – 6 PM |
| Last Dog Watch | 6 PM – 8 PM |
| First Watch | 8 PM – midnight |
| Middle Watch | midnight – 4 AM |

Time was kept by a half-hour glass; the bell was struck at each half-hour interval, adding one stroke, up to eight bells at the end of a four-hour watch. Dog watches (two hours each) were designed to rotate watch assignments so no crew members stood the same watch every day [cite:15].

The crew was divided into the **larboard watch** and the **starboard watch** (or in some ships, three watches) [cite:15]. One watch was on deck while the other was below, except during all-hands evolutions or emergencies.

### Daily Routine (while at anchor)

Adapted from the USS *Constitution* Museum's documented routine for American warships of this period [cite:16]:

- **Before dawn:** Those in the Morning Watch (4–8 AM) began work. First duty of the watch: wash down the deck with saltwater pumped from the ship's pumps, then holystone (scour with coarse stones and sand) to remove grime. This was the most physically demanding regular task.
- **7 AM (approx.):** Hammocks piped up — all crew brought their rolled and lashed hammocks from below to the hammock nettings along the rails, where they served as a barricade against musket fire and splinters in action. (1814 Navy Regulations required captains to "cause all new-raised men… to daily lash up their hammocks" [cite:16].)
- **8 AM:** Breakfast; eaten "picnic-style on the berth deck" — the crew's mess deck [cite:16]
- **Forenoon Watch (8 AM–noon):** Main working period; exercises, maintenance, gun drills, caulking, sail repairs, rigging work, and — on a ship preparing for action — daily gun exercises. "Every day from August 2 until August 19, the men practiced at the guns" (Constitution's pre-battle practice noted as standard) [cite:grog-search]. Gun exercise command: "run in the gun, load, prime, point and fire it with great rapidity" [cite:grog-search].
- **Seven bells in the Forenoon Watch (11:30 AM):** Grog piped — first issue of the day. One gill of whiskey diluted with an equal part of water, served at the spirit cask on deck [cite:grog-search].
- **Noon:** Dinner; the main meal of the day
- **Afternoon Watch (noon–4 PM):** Continuation of maintenance work; lighter duties
- **First Dog Watch (4–6 PM):** Typically a rest or recreation period; sailors might scrub their clothes (two hours per week allocated for laundry using saltwater and soap) [cite:16]
- **Last Dog Watch (6–8 PM):** Second grog issue
- **Supper:** One hour before sunset [cite:16]
- **8 PM:** Lights out in summer (8 PM winter) — per 1814 Navy Regulations [cite:grog-search]; hammocks slung below for sleeping

### At Anchor vs. At Sea

**Inference (not in sources):** While at anchor in Plattsburgh Bay (as *Saratoga* was from early August until September 11), the watch rotation continued but the demands differed from sea watch-keeping: no need to manage sails, but constant vigilance against the British squadron's approach, regular boat duty (passing messages and stores between vessels), tending the anchor watch, and the daily gun drills that Macdonough insisted upon. The spring lines and kedge anchor arrangements required regular inspection and adjustment.

### Petty Officers and Ranks

U.S. Navy enlisted hierarchy in this period, from top down [cite:13][cite:grog-search]:
- **Gunner's Mate:** Senior petty officer of the gunnery department; supervised quarter-gunners
- **Quarter-Gunner:** One per four guns; responsible for maintenance and operation of his section (see Section 8)
- **Seaman:** Fully trained sailor
- **Ordinary Seaman:** Partially trained sailor
- **Landsman:** Untrained recent recruit

Boys served as powder monkeys and ship's servants. The 1809 regulations for *Constitution* required the master-at-arms to keep a list of the boys and ensure their cleanliness and behavior [cite:grog-search].

### Grog

"The navy considered the spirit ration an indispensable part of the sailor's fare, and considerable ceremony surrounded the issue of this highly esteemed article" [cite:grog-search]. Issued twice daily, seven bells in the forenoon (11:30 AM) and at the Last Dog Watch. The ration: one gill of whiskey mixed with an equal quantity of water [cite:grog-search]. The ration applied equally at anchor and at sea.

## Section 14: Aftermath of September 11 — September 12 and Beyond

### Immediately After the Naval Battle

By approximately 11–11:30 AM on September 11, the British naval squadron had struck. Macdonough wrote to Macomb during the action: "Will you if possible send us Surgeons, we are much in want of them" [cite:founders-note]. The American squadron was badly damaged — *Saratoga* had 55 round shot in her hull — and prize crews were needed for the four captured British vessels (*Confiance*, *Linnet*, *Chubb*, *Finch*) [cite:04].

### Burial of the Naval Dead

Two plank hospital structures had been erected on the north part of Crab Island before the battle [cite:09]. After the battle, dead from both sides were brought to Crab Island for burial. Simeon Doty's 1886 account: the dead were buried in trenches without coffins, British and American together [cite:09]. Approximately 150 men are interred in unmarked graves on Crab Island [cite:01]. Dr. James Mann had performed over 30 amputations and was operating without adequate surgical help [cite:09].

### British Army Retreat

Prevost ordered the retreat after dark on September 11, beginning the march north on September 12. The withdrawal was rapid; quantities of ammunition, stores, and siege equipment were left behind in Plattsburgh [cite:05]. At least 234 British soldiers deserted during the march — primarily from Régiment de Meuron, 2/8th Foot, and 1/27th Foot [cite:01]. The British army was north of the border (back into Canada) within a few days.

### British Land Casualties (September 6–11 Cumulative)

37 killed, 150 wounded, 57 missing; three officers and 31 men of 76th Foot captured [cite:01].

### American Land Casualties

Regular army: 37 killed, 62 wounded, 20 missing [cite:01]. Overall including militia: approximately 115 killed, 130 wounded (Macomb's estimate to his father) [cite:01].

### British Naval Prisoners

Officers of the British squadron were held by the Americans pending exchange or parole. The British naval court martial for Pring and the surviving officers was eventually held August 18–21, 1815, at Portsmouth, aboard HMS *Gladiator*, and all officers were fully acquitted [cite:01].

### American Naval Repairs

*Saratoga* and the other vessels were in severe need of repair after the battle. The captured British ships were taken as prizes [cite:04]. Macdonough's fleet was repaired and remained on Lake Champlain until the peace.

### Village of Plattsburgh

Approximately 15 buildings had been destroyed by American hot shot during the standoff, including the courthouse and jail [cite:05]. The British army had occupied the north side of the village and left camp detritus and abandoned equipment throughout. **Inference (not in sources):** The day after the battle, September 12, Plattsburgh residents would have crossed the river (the lower bridge was still passable by foot over the stringers or by boat) to find the north side abandoned, pocked by artillery, and strewn with the debris of a 10,000-man encampment.

## Disputed and Uncertain Points

1. **Confiance's exact armament:** Sources conflict significantly. The Admiralty's own records indicate a smaller armament than some contemporary accounts; prize records differ from construction records. The Wikipedia figure (30 × 24-pounders + 6 × 32-pounder carronades + 1 × 24-pounder pivot) is the most commonly cited [cite:03].

2. **Confiance's crew strength:** Wikipedia gives 325; the HMS Confiance Wikipedia article gives approximately 270 with 41 killed and 83 wounded. The British post-action report says the crew was "totally unknown" to the officers, which is likely a political defense; actual strength was probably 250–325 [cite:01][cite:03].

3. **Pike's Ford distance:** 1.5 miles (mywarof1812.com) vs. 3 miles (Wikipedia) from Plattsburgh. These may measure from different reference points. The brief uses 3 miles upstream — consistent with one set of sources; the author should treat this as approximately correct and not specify a precise mileage in the prose.

4. **September 6 casualties:** Halsey's Corners historical marker source gives ~240 British killed/wounded and ~45 American. These are unusually precise figures from a commemorative plaque and should be treated as approximations, not verified counts [cite:06].

5. **Robertson's rank and court martial:** The brief refers to "Captain Robertson's trial of *Confiance*." Robertson was a lieutenant (the senior surviving officer, not the ship's captain). The court martial was for all surviving officers of the squadron, not a separate trial specifically for *Confiance* [cite:01].

6. **Start time of naval action:** Sources range from 8:30 AM to 9:00 AM for the British squadron rounding Cumberland Head. Macdonough's own letter gives 9 AM as the anchoring time [cite:04].
