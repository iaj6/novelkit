# Drafter Guidance — *A Line of Smoke*

## Voice Register

The target register is literary historical fiction in the tradition of Shaara's *The Killer Angels* (restrained third-limited per chapter, military vocabulary in stride, brief and earned interiority, the world rendered through what the soldier does and sees), O'Brian's *Master and Commander* (naval texture without explanation, the crew's world as the natural language the prose trusts the reader to enter), and Frazier's *Cold Mountain* (period vocabulary and landscape carrying what exposition would carry in a lesser book; landscape as the prose's method for interior life the characters will not narrate). The register is not lyric; it is not minimalist; it is specific and purposeful and restrained. Every sentence earns its place through precision, not through beauty.

The single most important constraint: **the two POV voices must be felt as different within the first paragraph of any chapter.** This distinction is not mood; it is the frame of reference each man brings to the world.

## Sentence-Length Expectations

- **Average sentence length:** 14–20 words in descriptive and approach passages; 8–14 words in action
- **Maximum acceptable sentence length:** 35 words; a sentence beyond this is a warning sign for unwanted sprawl
- **Short sentence use:** Short sentences (under 8 words) are not the default — they arrive as weight when they arrive. Three to four in a row is a deliberate rhythm; if they continue past five without variation, vary.
- **Action scenes:** Sentence length actively tightens. Do not sustain long sentences through physical crisis.

## Chapter-Opening Conventions

**Silas's chapters** must open on observable, physical, technical fact — typically involving his guns, his watch, his section of the ship. Do not open Silas on interior reflection. Do not open Silas on landscape description that precedes any human action. The first sentence should place him at his work or in motion. Example register (not a draft): "He had the morning watch and the guns to check before the morning watch."

**Asa's chapters** may open on a physical setting — the river, the mill, the ford — but the setting should enter through the senses as he notices it, in the order he notices it. Sound is often first. The opening can have more accumulation and density than Silas's, but should not be lyric; it should be observational. Do not open Asa on a thesis about the morning or the war.

**Day-before chapters** open on the ordinary day already in progress — no setup, no "this is a normal day before everything changed." Drop into the work. The reader knows the title.

**Action chapters** open mid-pressure: the engagement is underway or about to begin; no long approach. Cut to the moment of commitment.

## Chapter-Ending Conventions

**No manufactured cliffhangers.** This is an explicit brief constraint. Chapter endings should not be contrived for emotional impact; they should end when the scene's unit of action or time is complete.

**Day-before chapters:** Literary fade — the ordinary day winding down. The prose observes what is there; it does not signal what is coming.

**Day-after chapters:** Literary fade — the changed landscape at evening. Observational, not philosophical. Show what remains; do not say what it means.

**Approach/standoff chapters (most of the book):** Varied deliberately. Across the ten approach/standoff chapters, target approximately: 3 resolved beats (the day's action complete; the man settles), 4 mid-action or hard cut (ending in motion, a task unfinished or a sound arriving), 2 unresolved (tension held into the next day), 1 turn (a fact that changes the chapter's register, then ends without comment). **Do not produce runs of the same ending mode.** Alternate.

**Climactic action chapters:** End in mid-action or a hard cut to aftermath — not a lyric close. The action is not finished by the end of the chapter; what is finished is the chapter's unit of the action.

## POV Strategy

Strict two-POV alternation throughout: Silas chapters and Asa chapters, alternating, 8 each. The plotter's outline will specify the opening POV and the chapter assignments; hold to them exactly. **Do not slip the POV** within a chapter — not even briefly. No omniscient dip. No passage of interiority from a minor character.

Within each chapter, the POV is bound tightly to one person's sensory and cognitive access. If the character cannot see it or know it, the prose cannot report it.

## The Two Registers — Concrete Markers

These differences must be maintained across all chapters, not just "voice" passages.

**Silas's markers (apply always):**
- Technical naval vocabulary in the first three sentences
- Short declaratives; the verb is usually a verb of doing or checking
- Numbers, measurements, angles, weights (counts of roundshot, position of the sun, wind direction with compass bearing when known)
- Physical observation as the proxy for interior state: fear is a dry mouth, a re-check of the primer; grief is an absence, noted once, returned to its position
- Simile rare; when it appears, it is nautical or coastal (the angle of the sun; the feel of a line paying out)
- The sentence that names a fact and stops: "The garland held eighteen shot. He counted them before first light, and found eighteen."

**Asa's markers (apply always):**
- River or mill vocabulary in the first three sentences of any chapter, or in the first physical observation
- Sound enters before source is identified; the cannon fire approaches as pressure, as wrong weather, before it resolves into what it is
- He notices things in the order they enter his attention; his paragraphs sometimes accumulate before settling
- Simile from practical experience: a musket is like a lever with the weight at the wrong end; the ford sounds different under his feet than under the regulars' feet
- The body is present: cold water, muscle strain, the smell of the millrace
- More sentence variety; the occasional clause-stack as a thought pursues itself, then a short sentence to resolve

## Dialogue Density

**Low to moderate.** This is not a dialogue-driven book. Exchanges are short — orders, acknowledgments, brief practical speech. Extended conversations are reserved for moments of genuine weight (the sergeant instructing the militia; a word between Silas and his gun captain at a pause in the action). Neither character is given to speech about their feelings.

**Dialogue tags:** "said," "asked," "told him," "called." Never adverbial: not "said quietly" or "answered bitterly." The tone lives in the words and in what the POV character notices about the exchange, not in the tag.

**No dialogue gloss.** Do not follow dialogue with interpretive sentences ("as if to say," "which meant," "in a tone that suggested"). If the meaning of an exchange needs annotation, rewrite the exchange.

## Comic Timing and Tonal Balance

There is no comedy in this book. The register is serious throughout. Moments of human texture (a joke among the gun crew, Asa's wry observation about the sergeant's instructions) are permitted and welcome, but they do not shift the tonal register; they are part of how ordinary people sustain themselves in ordinary moments that happen to be surrounded by something not ordinary.

Resist any impulse toward irony at the level of the narrative voice. The narrator does not stand above the events; the narrator is bound inside the POV character's access.

## Anti-Defaults — What the Drafter Must Actively Avoid

This brief asks for adult literary fiction, which is close to the model's default register. But four specific failure modes are highly tempting here:

1. **The significance sentence.** "He could not know yet what the day would mean." "Neither of them would ever understand the importance of that morning." Any variant of this sentence is explicitly forbidden by the brief and must not appear in any form.

2. **Silas becoming an interiority character.** The AI's default register produces deep interior access; Silas's register specifically forbids it. If Silas is thinking for more than two sentences without a physical observation or an action, something has gone wrong. His interiority lives in what he checks, counts, and does — not in what he reflects on.

3. **The two POVs drifting toward the same register.** By the middle of drafting, both voices tend to flatten toward the same sentence rhythm and vocabulary. The difference must be actively maintained in every chapter. Check each chapter's first paragraph: if you could not tell whose chapter it is from the first three sentences, you have lost the register.

4. **Foreshadowing in the day-before chapters.** Any sentence in the day-before chapters that signals the coming storm — in weather, in mood, in a character's unnamed unease — violates the brief. The day-before chapters are ordinary. The prose observes; it does not hint. The reader knows the title; the prose does not assist.

5. **Philosophizing in the day-after chapters.** The inverse constraint: the day-after chapters must observe what has changed without weighing what it means. A sentence about the cost of war, the weight of the dead, what the valley has lost — any of these violates the brief. Show the inventory; do not annotate it.
