# Reader Guidance — *A Line of Smoke*

## Intended Audience

Adult readers of literary historical fiction — specifically, readers who have found their way to Michael Shaara's *The Killer Angels*, Patrick O'Brian's Aubrey-Maturin series, and Charles Frazier's *Cold Mountain*. They come to this book expecting technical precision (naval gunnery, period infantry drill, early 19th-century mill and river work), restrained prose that does not over-explain, multiple distinct viewpoints that do not flatten into each other, and the kind of historical action that makes the war comprehensible through the individual body rather than through strategy. They are not looking for plot mechanics (spy thriller, romance, political intrigue); they are looking for a story that makes them feel the weight of the day from the inside.

Reading-grade target: college. The reader is assumed to be patient with period vocabulary and unfamiliar technical worlds. This is not a glossary book.

## Brief-Adherence Checks to Perform

The reader must evaluate the manuscript against these specific yes/no questions, in addition to general craft review:

1. **Are the two POV voices distinct within the first paragraph of any chapter?** If a chapter's opening paragraph could belong to either Silas or Asa without sounding different, the voices have drifted. This is a critical failure mode.
2. **Does Silas's prose stay in the spare/technical register throughout his chapters?** Does it use naval vocabulary in stride (not italicized, not glossed, not apologized for)? Does his interiority surface as physical observation rather than announced feeling?
3. **Does Asa's prose stay in the river/sound/practical register throughout his chapters?** Is the cannon fire on September 11 rendered first as sensory/atmospheric before it resolves into identified source?
4. **Are the day-before chapters free of foreshadowing?** No signal, no unnamed unease, no atmospheric omen. Do they observe ordinary life without preparing the reader for what is coming?
5. **Are the day-after chapters free of philosophizing?** No sentence about what the battle meant, what the dead gave, what the valley learned. Do they observe what remains without annotation?
6. **Is historical significance ever announced?** Any variant of "he could not know yet what the day would mean" or "this morning would be remembered" is a critical failure; flag it explicitly wherever it appears.
7. **Do the two POV characters ever meet, share a scene, or learn of each other's existence?** They must not.
8. **Is the naval vocabulary accurate and in stride?** Cross-reference key terms against *canon/research.md* and *canon/glossary.md*. Flag anachronism or error.
9. **Is the chapter-ending distribution varied?** If more than two chapters in a row (across either POV) end the same way (all literary fade, all cliffhanger, all resolved), flag the run.
10. **Do named historical officers remain external to the POV?** Macdonough, Macomb, Mooers, Wool, Brisbane, Pring — none of them should be interiored. They are seen; they are not felt from inside.

## What "Good" Looks Like for This Book

A good chapter of *A Line of Smoke* reads as follows:

**A good Silas chapter** opens in the middle of technical work or watch-keeping with no preparatory setup. Within three sentences, the naval vocabulary is present and unexplained. The chapter's interiority arrives in physical notation — a count of shot, a check of a primer, a noted wind direction — and does not pause to explain what those physical facts stand for emotionally. If his brother surfaces, it is in a single sentence, precise and unrepeated. The action, when it comes, narrows and tightens without losing precision. The chapter ends when its unit of work is complete — not when the emotional note is right.

**A good Asa chapter** opens with a sensory approach — sound or cold or the feel of the ford underfoot — and the river or mill vocabulary is present in the first observations. The prose accumulates before it settles: two or three short noticing-sentences, then a longer sentence that fixes them in place. His similes are practical, not literary. The cannon fire approaches as atmospheric disturbance before it is identified. The chapter does not explain what he is feeling; it places him in the physical reality of the ford and trusts the reader.

**A good climactic chapter** does not step back for an overview. The reader understands the action because they understand what the character is trying to do, not because the prose provides a strategic map. The sensory experience (noise, concussion, smoke, the weight of the gun, the cold of the ford water) is primary. The tactical context arrives through what the character can see and hear from his position.

**Good day-before and day-after chapters** are the hardest to write correctly. Good ones read as genuinely ordinary — not as setup, not as coda, not as elegy. They observe what is there. The test: would a reader who did not know the book's title feel that something is being withheld? If yes, the chapter has failed its brief.

## What to Flag Explicitly If the Manuscript Drifts

Even if the prose is good in a craft sense, the following drift patterns must be named explicitly:

- **Silas drifts toward interiority.** If Silas is reflecting for more than two consecutive sentences without a physical action or observation, name it: "Silas's register is drifting toward interiority — his chapters should show his interior life through physical notation, not through sustained reflection."
- **The two voices blend.** If a Silas chapter and an Asa chapter open with similar rhythm and vocabulary, name it: "The POV registers are blending — the brief requires the difference to be felt in the first paragraph."
- **Significance is announced.** Any sentence (in either thread, in either the action or approach chapters) that tells the reader what the day meant, what history was being made, or what the characters would later understand — flag as a critical violation, by exact quote.
- **The day-before chapters signal.** If a day-before chapter contains weather that is ominous, a character's unnamed sense of dread, or any prose device that prepares the reader for the coming storm, flag by exact quote: "This sentence foreshadows — the day-before chapters must be genuinely ordinary."
- **The day-after chapters editorialize.** If a day-after chapter contains a sentence about sacrifice, the weight of what the men bore, the meaning of the battle — flag by exact quote: "This sentence philosophizes — the day-after chapters must observe without comment."
- **The characters meet.** If any chapter places Silas and Asa in any shared scene, knowledge of each other, or direct sensory access beyond the audible cannonade, flag as a structural failure.
- **Historical error or anachronism.** Cross-check vocabulary and timeline against *canon/research.md*. Flag specific errors by quote and citation to the research dossier.

## What to Applaud

The reader should specifically defend the following in its "what to leave alone" section if the manuscript hits them well:

1. **Period vocabulary in stride.** If a chapter uses naval gunnery terms, sash-mill terms, or period infantry commands without glossing or italicizing them, and it does not impede comprehension, do not flag this as an accessibility problem. This is the O'Brian contract; it is correct.

2. **Landscape as emotional register.** If a chapter gives significant paragraph-weight to weather, the river level, the direction of the wind, the light on the lake — and this landscape is carrying what the character will not say about his interior state — do not flag this as "purple prose" or "over-written." This is the Frazier method; it is the prose doing its work.

3. **Brief earned interiority.** A single sentence of genuine interior access — Silas's brother surfacing once, Asa's father present as a weight in the back of his attention — is correct for this register. Do not flag it as a failure mode; flag it as a success if it is brief, precise, and the prose moves on.

4. **Short chapters that feel complete.** The day-before and day-after chapters target 1,800–2,400 words. If a chapter in this range feels complete and honest, do not flag it as "thin." Length and density are not the same thing; this book's shorter chapters earn their brevity.
