# Threads — *A Line of Smoke*

Five threads braid this novel. Two belong to Silas; three belong to Asa. Two are external (plot-level tension); two are internal (character-level question); one is mixed. Two are deliberately unresolved. Every chapter advances at least one.

---

## Thread 1: The Anchor Cables

- **Question:** What is the unusual spring-and-kedge arrangement Silas keeps noticing on the *Saratoga*'s deck — and will Macdonough's secret preparation be enough to survive the British fleet when it finally comes?

- **Enters:** Ch 03 — Silas notices an unfamiliar cable configuration on the main deck (a spring line running to a separate anchor, not the bower chain), stores the observation without understanding it, and files it the way he files a discrepancy in the garland count: without alarm, without dismissing it.

- **Advanced in:**
  - Ch 05 — the kedge is fully rigged astern; the British fleet is still absent from the lake; the preparation sits there, purposeful and unexplained
  - Ch 07 — the gunner's mate amends the ammunition allocation toward more grape and canister; Silas reads the implication backward: *close action*; the preparation is beginning to resolve into probable meaning
  - Ch 09 — Silas overhears the ship's carpenter describe the winding maneuver (*let go the stern, cut the bower, haul on the kedge*); he visits Marsh's port battery for the first time; the full shape of the preparation becomes clear and is stored

- **Resolves in:** Ch 13 — the starboard battery is shot to pieces exactly as prepared-for; the *Saratoga* winds on the kedge; Silas crosses to the port battery and fires into the battered British line.

- **What it's about underneath:** This thread is about the chain of trust that runs from command to deck. Silas cannot ask Macdonough what he is planning; he can only store observations and trust that they will eventually resolve into sense. The preparation — Macdonough's careful arrangement made months before the battle — is a form of care that reaches Silas only as a sequence of unexplained facts. The thread pressurizes the question of what a man owes a system he doesn't fully see, and whether trusting that system constitutes wisdom or submission. The winding maneuver's success answers with unusual cleanness: the care was real; the preparation held; the facts were worth storing.

## Thread 2: The Brother's Absence

- **Question:** Has Silas's conversion of grief for his dead brother into a form of concentrated competence been enough to carry him — and will it hold through what the battle costs him?

- **Enters:** Ch 01 — in the quiet of the fo'c'sle at last light, Silas does not think about his brother. The entry is the absence: the thing not thought about in the one moment of unstructured time in an otherwise fully occupied day.

- **Advanced in:**
  - Ch 09 — the interiority chapter; Silas's mind does unfamiliar work (tracing the geometry of the winding maneuver) and the prose brushes past the one gap in his accounting — the stored fact that does not resolve into operational use
  - Ch 11 — the extreme attention before battle: he has checked the flints at midnight, at two, and again before leaving his hammock; the grief-as-preparation is at its highest concentration
  - Ch 13 — Fry goes down; *"Fry. He notes the fact. He moves to gun #2."* — the only form in which grief is available to Silas; the note is made; the movement continues

- **Resolves in:** Ch 15 — *deliberately unresolved.* The accounting continues: two guns may be repaired, two cannot; *new gun captain for gun #1.* Fry is noted as alive; the one dead man from his section is noted. The brother is never named. The grief stays in its converted form, and Silas goes to write the gunner's mate's report.

- **What it's about underneath:** This thread asks whether a man can sustain an interior organization built in response to loss — whether the substitution (grief → competence, fear → checking the primer) can hold through a real battle's cost. The answer is that it holds — but only because the battle's losses, though real, do not directly require Silas to feel anything his system cannot process as inventory. The brother stays dead and unmentioned. The form survives because it is never directly challenged. The deliberately unresolved ending is not evasion; it is the book's honest observation that some conversions are permanent.

## Thread 3: The Father's Charge

- **Question:** Will the specific, earned knowledge of a place — what Asa's father told him to give the army — be exactly what the moment needs?

- **Enters:** Ch 02 — the Saranac River is Asa's native world; the father is present as weight on the evening walk home, already present in the labor of knowing the river's every stone and current. Ch 04 — the father's lucid hour: *"You know the river better than any man Mooers's got. Tell them."* The uncle echoes the instruction the next morning without having heard it — both men sending Asa the same message from two directions.

- **Advanced in:**
  - Ch 06 — Asa tells the sergeant he knows Pike's Ford; Cole says "good" and turns to the companies
  - Ch 08 — Asa walks Cole through every feature of the ford: the limestone shelf, the gravel trough that shifts underfoot, the sinkhole under the alders on the north bank, the submerged log in the willows downstream
  - Ch 10 — the British patrol scans the ford from the north bank and cannot read its bottom; Asa watches them and knows what they don't know

- **Resolves in:** Ch 14 — the British flanking party steps on the submerged log Asa described six days earlier; they go down in the willows, exactly as he knew they would. Ch 16 coda: Asa looks at the ford from above for the first time, files the new angle, thinks briefly of his father.

- **What it's about underneath:** The thread asks whether the practical, inherited knowledge of a specific place — learned not in training but in living — has military value. The answer is yes, once, in one specific moment, in a way that no general could have planned for. The thread is also about inheritance: what the father gave Asa was not military instruction but a way of knowing a place. The instruction (*tell them*) was the act of passing that knowledge out of its original context and into the war's use. The resolution is quiet: no one commends Asa for knowing about the log; the British simply go down.

## Thread 4: Beekmantown's Lesson

- **Question:** After the militia's humiliating break at Beekmantown, can Sergeant Cole's two-rank drill and Asa's knowledge of the ford turn a county levy into a force that holds its line when the British come in force?

- **Enters:** Ch 06 — the militia breaks at first contact north of Beekmantown Corners; most of the company runs; Cole holds the rearguard with the few who stay; the question is raised in the starkest possible terms: can four days of drill fix what broke in four minutes?

- **Advanced in:**
  - Ch 08 — the drill begins at the ford; it is bad at first (Webb fumbles the sequence and nearly fires into the first rank); Asa finds the key in the mill's ragwheel — the automatic advance; Cole corrects Veeder's grip without comment
  - Ch 10 — the drill is sharpening; the British patrol is visible on the north bank; Cole praises Asa's midnight-watch patience with one word (*good*); the question has become personal and immediate
  - Ch 12 — the company is fully manned at their positions before first light, without orders, in the dark; the wait holds; the sequence is in the bodies now

- **Resolves in:** Ch 14 — the ford holds; the two-rank sequence holds under fire; the British pull back in order; Cole says *"stand easy."*

- **What it's about underneath:** This thread is about what a professional soldier can give an unprepared civilian in four days — not courage, not confidence, but a repeatable procedure that holds when the head goes blank. The thread's metronome is Cole's word *good,* delivered as a fact across three chapters and one climax. The resolution is not triumph; it is sequence. The militia held the ford because the sequence held, and the sequence held because Cole taught it and Asa made it automatic — the way a ragwheel advances one tooth every upstroke, whether you think about it or not.

## Thread 5: The Man Upstream

- **Question:** While Asa stands his watch at the ford, his father is three miles upstream in the fever that did not leave after Sackett's Harbor — will he still be there when Asa walks back?

- **Enters:** Ch 02 — the walk home along the south bank at dusk; the father as the weight of the walk; the fever that has been the fact of Asa's life since he was fifteen. Ch 04 — the lucid hour; then the fever takes him back before Asa leaves; and Asa goes to his room and takes down his musket, and that is the last clear thing between them.

- **Advanced in:** Chs 06, 08, 10, 12 — *present in absence.* The ford demands all of Asa's attention in the approach chapters; the father is not mentioned by name in any of them. The thread advances through its own silence: the reader tracks the father by watching him go unthought-about, in the way that a man who cannot afford to think about something does not think about it. The accumulation of this silence *is* the advancement — the weight that sits three miles upstream of every drill, every midnight watch, every moment the north bank stays empty.

- **Resolves in:** Ch 16 — *deliberately unresolved.* Asa thinks of his father briefly, obliquely, without comparing the two men's service. He turns back toward camp. The book ends before he walks the three miles home. Whether his father is alive, worse, or recovered, the narrative does not say — because Asa does not know either, and the book holds to what he knows.

- **What it's about underneath:** This thread is the invisible freight of all of Asa's war: the responsibility he cannot discharge from the ford, the weight that sits upstream of everything he does. It runs parallel to the military action without ever intersecting it — the war does not wait while a man checks on his father, and the book does not either. The deliberately unresolved ending is the thread's argument: the war created this gap; the narrative does not close it because closing it would be a lie. The gap is real, and Asa carries it home.

**Dead chapters:** None. All sixteen chapters advance at least one thread.

**Notes on singleton chapters** (advancing only one thread): Chs 01, 03, 05, 07, and 15 each advance a single thread. This is not a defect — each is a tightly focused chapter whose function is to advance one question with full attention. The weave is dense enough across the rest of the novel to carry these quieter chapters.

**Convergence pattern:** Threads 1 and 2 (Silas's external and internal questions) converge and resolve in chs 13 and 15 respectively. Threads 3 and 4 (Asa's external questions) converge and resolve in ch 14. Thread 5 (Asa's internal, unresolved) touches every Asa chapter and is closed — by being deliberately not closed — in ch 16. The two deliberately unresolved threads (2 and 5) are one per POV, which distributes the unresolution evenly across both characters.
