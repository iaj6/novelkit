# Style — *A Line of Smoke*

## POV and Tense
Third-person limited past tense, strictly chapter-bound. Each chapter belongs entirely to one POV: Silas or Asa. The POV does not wander; no access to any other character's interiority, no omniscient dip, no free indirect transition to another consciousness.

## Narrative Distance
Close but not interior-monologue. The prose follows the character's attention and gives access to what he observes and thinks, but does not narrate inner states directly when they can be shown through observation or action. "He noted the wind shifting southwest" is the prose's method. "He felt afraid" is not — unless the character would actually think that, which Silas would not and Asa would do only glancingly.

The model: Michael Shaara's *The Killer Angels*. Each chapter-POV is a world unto itself, rendered from within. Interiority is earned and brief; it arrives as a single precise sentence and the prose moves on.

## Sentence Rhythm
Sentences breathe but do not sprawl. The base unit is a medium sentence (12–20 words). Long sentences are permitted in descriptive passages — establishing weather, the ford in the morning light, the geography of the bay — but should not proliferate in action or in Silas's register specifically. Short sentences arrive with weight; they are not the default.

Action scenes tighten: sentence length drops. The prose stays in the POV character's immediate sensory world — what he can see, hear, smell, touch from where he stands. No overview in action. No bird's-eye. The action is comprehensible because the reader understands what the character is trying to *do*, not because the prose provides a map.

The exemplar registers:
- **Shaara**: restrained third-limited per chapter, military vocabulary in stride, brief and earned interiority, the world rendered through what the soldier does and sees
- **O'Brian**: naval texture without explanation, crew vocabulary as the natural language of a working world the prose trusts the reader to enter
- **Frazier**: literary historical prose where period vocabulary and landscape carry what exposition would carry in a lesser book; landscape as emotional register

## The Two Registers

The difference between Silas's chapters and Asa's chapters must be felt within the first paragraph of any chapter. This is not a matter of author's choice by scene; it is structural — the two men's prose is distinct because their frames of reference are distinct.

### Silas's register
**Markers:** Naval vocabulary in the first sentences. Short, declarative sentences dominate. Physical observation, not interior reflection. Numbers, measurements, weights, angles. Verbs of doing and checking — "sponged," "primed," "laid," "checked" — rather than verbs of feeling. When metaphor appears it is naval: the wind, the angle of the sun, the trim of a loaded gun. The sequence of facts is the sequence of a working man's attention.

**What fear looks like in Silas:** A dry mouth. He checks the primer again. He notes the wind.

**What grief looks like in Silas:** An absence. A fact he has noted and set aside. Once per book, the prose may allow one sentence to surface it; then it is returned to its position: set aside.

**What the sentences sound like:** "The garland held eighteen shot. He counted them in the dark before first light, the way he did before any drill, and found eighteen." Not: "He felt reassured by the count." The count is the reassurance; the prose does not say so.

### Asa's register
**Markers:** River and mill vocabulary in the first sentences. More varied sentence length — sometimes a rush or a pile-up of short observations before a long settling sentence. Sound prominent: the cannon fire enters as weather, as pressure, before it resolves into source. He notices things in the order they enter his attention, and his chapters sometimes have the texture of noticing.

**What fear looks like in Asa:** The current. The way he is more aware of the ford's bottom than he was before. The fact that he knows exactly where a man would lose his footing on the British side.

**What the sentences sound like:** "The ford smelled of cold water and yesterday's fire. Upstream, behind the willows, a wren was going on about something. He had time to notice this." Not: "He was surprised to notice a bird singing despite everything." The contrast is implicit; the prose does not explain it.

**Simile in Asa:** Present, but earned. Always practical: a thing is like another thing from the mill or the river. "The musket felt different than the cant hook, less balanced, like a lever with the weight at the wrong end." The similes are his, not the author's.

## Dialogue
Spare. Period-appropriate; no anachronism. Dialect is not phonetically rendered; characters speak in a compressed, plausible period register without burlesque. Dialogue tags are plain: "said," "asked," "told him," "called." No adverbial tags. The interiority does not live in the dialogue; it lives in the reported observation before and after.

Brief exchanges of practical import predominate — orders, acknowledgments, confirmations, the occasional terse exchange between men who know each other. Extended conversation is reserved for moments of genuine weight: the sergeant's instruction on loading procedure, a word passed between Silas and his gun captain in the action.

## Period Vocabulary
In stride, not in italics. When Silas thinks about his guns he uses the language he would use — "long twenty-four," "flannel cartridge," "prime the vent," "run out" — without announcing it. When Asa sets a cant hook or calls the ragwheel he uses the mill vocabulary without definition. The reader follows the world through the work. Exposition is rationed strictly.

This is the O'Brian contract: the crew knows their world; the prose trusts the reader to follow.

## What the Prose Does Not Do
- Does not announce the historical significance of the battle — ever
- Does not editorialize about death or injury; describes, does not comment
- Does not foreshadow in the day-before chapters (the prose observes; it does not signal)
- Does not philosophize in the day-after chapters (shows what remains; does not weigh what it means)
- Does not signal irony through the narrative voice
- Does not use chapter endings as emotional emphasis — ends when the scene ends
- Does not bring the two POV characters together in any way (no shared scene, no common acquaintance who carries news between them)

## Failure modes

The AI-author patterns most tempting in this story's register. The drafter should actively avoid these; the compression pass should cut them.

- **Post-scene subtext naming** — after a scene closes, the prose must not deliver a paragraph explaining what it was "really about" emotionally or thematically. The scene has done its work. In a book that explicitly forbids announcing significance, this is the prose failure mode most likely to violate that contract.
- **Thesis restatement** — no abstract sentence stating the significance of the battle, the meaning of the men's sacrifice, or the weight of history. "He could not know yet what the day would mean" is the canonical failure sentence for this book; it is forbidden in all its variants.
- **Emotional structural analysis** — dramatizing a feeling (Silas's suppressed grief; Asa's controlled fear) and then explaining its architecture: "His grief had become a practice, a form of attention rather than a feeling." Show the practice; do not explain it.
- **Redundant transition / re-summary** — no chapter should open by recapping the previous chapter's events; no scene transition should summarize what just occurred. The reader was there.
- **Dialogue glosses** — no "as if to say," "which meant," "in a tone that suggested." The prose trusts the reader. The dialogue means what it says, or the dialogue should be rewritten.

## Lean-into patterns

Patterns the drafter and editors should NOT treat as failures, because this register actively wants them.

- **Period vocabulary in stride** — technical worlds (naval gunnery, sash mill, infantry drill) are the world. The prose does not pause to explain them. This is a feature; do not treat it as an accessibility problem to solve with parenthetical glosses.
- **Landscape as emotional register** — weather, river level, wind direction, the light on the lake: these carry what the characters don't say. In Frazier's manner, the landscape is not backdrop; it is the prose's method for the interior life that the characters will not narrate. Do not cut landscape passages as "purple prose."
- **Brief earned interiority** — a single precise interior sentence (Silas noting his brother's name briefly; Asa registering his father's absence from the line) is a feature of this register. Keep it brief, keep it once, move on. The Shaara method: the interiority earns its one sentence and the prose returns to action.
- **The sentence that names a physical fact and stops** — "The wind had shifted to the southwest. He noted it and checked the primer." This is not deficient; this is the register. Do not expand it with elaboration.
