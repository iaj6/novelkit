# Characters — *A Line of Smoke*

## POV Characters

### Silas Ware
**Age:** 22  
**Role:** Quarter-gunner, starboard battery, USS *Saratoga*  
**Background:** Maine-born, from a fishing and coasting family on the Penobscot coast. Signed onto coasters at fifteen; enlisted in the Navy in 1812 after the declaration. He has been on Lake Champlain since the squadron was built at Vergennes the winter and spring of 1813–14 — he helped haul timber and watched Noah Brown's men frame the *Saratoga* in forty days. His older brother died at the River Raisin in January 1813, one of the Americans surrendered by General Winchester and killed in the aftermath. Silas does not speak of this to anyone.

**Drives:** Competence as a substitute for grief. His brother's death produced not rage or sorrow (both of which he would know how to express) but a form of concentrated attention: he tends his guns, he checks the flint before every drill, he knows the powder weights and the range tables and the weaknesses of each man in his gun crew. He is responsible for a section of four long twenty-fours. Keeping that section ready, keeping it right — this is the form his interior life has taken. He is not indifferent to his circumstances; he has organized his feelings into gun-keeping.

**Voice:** Spare, technical, observational. The world is presented as observable fact. He notes the wind; he notes the angle of the sun; he counts the shot in the garland and registers a discrepancy without drama. Interior states surface as physical observation: fear is a dry mouth and a deliberate check of the primer; grief is a fact he has noted and set aside. Naval vocabulary is his native language — in stride, no italics, no glossing. The prose of his chapters does not explain what a long twenty-four is; it shows him at one.

He is not laconic by affect or performance; he is laconic because that is genuinely how his attention works. When he is around men he talks enough. When he is working, he is working.

**Relationships:**  
- His gun crew: the men he is responsible for. Developed in drafting; at minimum a gun captain under him and a team of eight to ten.
- The gunner's mate: his immediate superior on the battery; the person through whom Macdonough's orders reach him in diluted, practical form.
- His brother: dead since January 1813, not discussed, present as an absence that shapes his behavior.
- Macdonough: seen from the gun deck — heard, occasionally glimpsed on the quarterdeck. Never spoken to directly. Seen as the officer who makes the decisions that Silas then executes without question.

**What he doesn't know:** He doesn't understand the spring-and-kedge anchoring system as tactical preparation until it is used. He stores the oddly-laid anchor lines as fact without consequence — until consequences arrive.

**What he carries out of the book:** The starboard battery, shot to pieces by noon on September 11. What it means that he is on the other battery when it's over. He does not say.

### Asa Beckwith
**Age:** 19  
**Role:** Militiaman, Clinton County levy; assigned to Pike's Ford, Saranac River  
**Background:** Born and raised on the Saranac River, three miles upstream of Plattsburgh village. His father served at Sackett's Harbor in 1813 and came home with a fever that became chronic — the kind that a man lives inside rather than recovers from. Since he was fifteen Asa has been the wage earner: apprentice to his uncle's sash sawmill, sawing boards for the valley's construction trade, learning the river in the process of living on it. He was called up the last week of August when Prevost's army crossed the border. He brought his own musket.

**Drives:** The specific competence of knowing a place. Asa is not a soldier; he is a young man who knows this river. That knowledge — where it runs knee-deep, where the bottom shifts underfoot in September water, where the current pulls a man left whether he wants it to or not — is what he contributes to the ford defense that no regular-army sergeant can replicate. He is not brave in any abstract sense. He is present, and presence is what the moment requires.

**Voice:** Closer to the noise of nineteen-year-old consciousness. Not interior monologue exactly, but a prose that registers what he notices in the order he notices it, with the density and interruption of a young man's actual attention. He thinks in the river — in flow, in fall, in depth, in shoals and stones and the sound of fast water — not as literary reach but because those are his native categories, the concepts he grew up solving problems with. Sound carries in his chapters: the cannon fire from the bay enters as a pressure in the chest before it is a noise with a source, experienced as weather, as something wrong with the air, before it resolves into what it is.

He uses simile more than Silas does; his similes are mill and river similes. He is not uneducated — he can read, he can cipher, his uncle taught him to keep the mill's accounts — but his frame of reference is the practical world of the Saranac valley.

**Relationships:**  
- His uncle: the millwright, owns and operates the sawmill. Asa's employer, guardian-in-effect, and the man who taught him the river as well as the mill. Not called up himself — age, or the sawmill's designation as war-essential supply. His absence from the line is something Asa doesn't examine.
- His father: sick at the house upstream. Present in Asa's thoughts as weight, as responsibility that doesn't release during the standoff days. He is the reason Asa has been the man of the house for four years.
- The regular-army sergeant: a figure in the approach chapters, name to be established in drafting; the professional soldier who has been teaching the militia to load in two ranks and mean it. Asa respects him without liking him.
- His company: the Clinton County men at the ford. Names and brief characterization to be developed in drafting.

**What he doesn't know:** He has no information about the naval engagement beyond sound and smoke visible above the tree line to the east. He doesn't know whether the navy is winning or losing. He knows the river.

**What he carries out of the book:** The ford held. He doesn't philosophize about what that means.

## Historical Figures (Seen From Below; Never POV)

All named historical officers appear at most as figures seen from a distance, overheard in command, or passed in the chain of orders. None of them are ever interiored.

### Thomas Macdonough
**Age:** 30 (born December 23, 1783, The Trap, Delaware)  
**Role:** Master Commandant, commanding officer USS *Saratoga* and the American squadron  
**Appears in:** Silas's thread — on the quarterdeck, visible from the gun deck; heard in orders relayed down the chain  
**Key facts for the text:** Macdonough was the architect of the spring-and-kedge anchoring system. The winding maneuver — letting go the stern anchor, cutting the bower cable, hauling the ship 180° on the kedge — was his preparation and his decision to execute. He is not explained; he is observed.

### George Downie
**Age:** ~35  
**Role:** Captain RN, commanding officer HMS *Confiance* and the British naval squadron  
**Appears in:** If at all, at extreme range from *Saratoga*'s gun deck — a figure on the enemy flagship. Killed early in the battle when his own gun was dismounted by *Saratoga*'s fire and struck him.  
**Key facts:** Downie was pressured by Prevost to attack before the *Confiance* was ready; the British crew was short-handed and the guns had not been properly test-fired.

### Alexander Macomb
**Age:** ~30  
**Role:** Brigadier General, commanding American land forces at Plattsburgh  
**Appears in:** Asa's thread — a distant officer on horseback; an order passed down through the chain; possibly a figure seen across a field

### Benjamin Mooers
**Role:** Major-General, New York State Militia (Northern Division), Clinton County  
**Appears in:** Asa's thread — the general of the militia, seen at a distance, not encountered directly

### John E. Wool
**Role:** Major, 29th U.S. Infantry; commanded the Beekmantown delaying action September 6  
**Appears in:** Asa's thread — the professional whose fighting withdrawal the militia was part of; the name mentioned by the sergeant with a particular tone

### Daniel Pring
**Role:** Commander RN, HMS *Linnet*; effectively commanded the British squadron after Downie's death  
**Appears in:** Very unlikely to appear directly

### Sir George Prevost
**Role:** Governor-General of British North America, commander-in-chief of the British army (~10,000–14,000 men)  
**Appears in:** Never directly encountered; the army is felt; the general is a rumor

### Brigadier-General Brisbane
**Role:** British brigade commander who directed the Saranac crossing attempts on September 11  
**Appears in:** Asa's thread — the British force attempting the ford is Brisbane's, but he himself is not seen

## Notes for Drafting

- No invented characters should become significant beyond what the brief establishes. Silas's gun crew and Asa's company may be minimally named and briefly characterized as the chapters require; they should not become competing protagonists.
- Black and Indigenous characters present in the Champlain Valley should not be invisible in the world, but their treatment avoids both erasure and centering. A Black powder monkey in Silas's chapters; a free Black laborer who helped dig the fortifications visible from the ford in Asa's chapters — this is the level of integration.
- Silas and Asa never meet. They do not communicate. They do not know of each other. They share a single sensory fact on September 11: the naval cannonade is audible from Pike's Ford.
