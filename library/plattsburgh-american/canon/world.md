# World — *A Line of Smoke*

## Location

The Champlain Valley, northern New York State, September 1814. The story is centered on three points:

1. **Plattsburgh Bay** — a sheltered hook of water on the western shore of Lake Champlain, bounded to the northeast by Cumberland Head. Macdonough chose it for its geometry: any British fleet approaching from the north had to round Cumberland Head and enter in column, crossing under American guns before it could form a line. The American squadron lies at anchor in the bay's throat. In clear weather from the gun deck the Vermont hills are visible across the lake to the east, green and still beginning to yellow.

2. **Plattsburgh village and the Saranac River line** — the Saranac enters the lake at the village. The south bank of the lower Saranac is the American defensive position; Macomb's regulars hold the earthworks and blockhouses. Three crossing points exist: the lower bridge (destroyed by the Americans before the battle to deny it to the British), an upper bridge in the village itself (contested), and Pike's Ford, approximately one to three miles upstream, held by militia including Asa's company.

3. **The Saranac River, three miles upstream** — Asa's uncle's sash mill. A water-powered operation driven by a millrace off the river, small enough that two men and a laborer run it. The mill is a working place, not a stage set: the pitman arm, the log carriage riding its ragwheel, the kerf of a fresh-cut board, sawdust banking in still water at the tail race.

### Additional geography
- **Cumberland Head** — the long peninsula forming the northeastern boundary of Plattsburgh Bay; the anchorage is sheltered behind it. The British squadron must round it to enter.
- **Beekmantown** — a hamlet on the Plattsburgh road, approximately 8–10 miles north of the village. Site of Major Wool's September 6 delaying action; where most of the militia broke before it got serious.
- **Vergennes, Vermont** — thirty-plus miles south on the Vermont side of the lake, up the Otter Creek. Where Macdonough's squadron was built from raw timber in the winter and spring of 1813–14 under Noah Brown's shipwrights — the *Saratoga* (143 feet, built in approximately 40 days) among them.
- **Crab Island** — a small island in the bay used as an American hospital during and after the battle; burial trenches afterward.

## Period

The United States has been at war with Britain since June 1812. Napoleon abdicated in April 1814; Peninsular War veteran regiments — the 3rd Foot, 76th Foot, De Watteville's, Meuron's — have been freed for service in North America. Sir George Prevost has marched an army of ten to fourteen thousand south from Montreal.

The summer has been building. Macomb's regulars have been at Plattsburgh since summer; Mooers's militia has been drilling in the fields. Prevost's van crossed the border approximately August 31 and skirmished at Chazy on September 4. On September 6 the British right column, approximately four thousand strong, overran most of the militia at Beekmantown before a regular-led fighting withdrawal brought what remained back to the river. Since September 7 the British have been on the north bank and the Americans on the south, waiting for the navy to resolve what the land alone cannot.

## Sensory World

**On the water.** The *Saratoga* at anchor smells of pitch, slow-match, and the particular salt-and-bilge of a ship that has been on fresh water long enough to lose its sea smell. The guns are cold in the morning. The flannel powder cartridges come up from the magazine in leather pass-boxes carried by boys. The lake is gray-blue in September, the surface choppy when wind moves down from the north, glass-flat in dead calms at dawn. The guns' muzzles are plugged against moisture. The garlands hold their roundshot in neat rows.

**At the ford.** The Saranac in early September runs clear over stone and gravel, knee-to-thigh deep at the ford depending on recent rain, the current brisk but not dangerous for a man with his footing. Alder and willow crowd the south bank; the militia have been cutting firing lines through the brush for three days. The smell is cold water, mud, smoke from the cook fire upstream, and — on September 11 — powder smoke drifting in from the west.

**At the sawmill.** The sash mill driven by a millrace: the groan of the pitman arm, the lurch-and-advance of the log carriage, the kerf splitting open in green pine, sawdust drifting down to bank in the still water of the tail race. On a still morning the sound of the saw carries across the water. The smell is pine sap, iron filings from the saw, the mineral cold of the millrace.

**Weather.** Early September in the Champlain Valley: cool nights (40s°F), warm afternoons (mid-60s to low 70s°F), morning ground fog in the low spots along the river. September 11, 1814 was a clear morning with light wind from the northeast; the wind shifted during the battle, a fact with tactical consequences that Macdonough had prepared for and Downie had not.

## Technology and Material World

**Naval gunnery.** Long guns and carronades; smoothbore muzzle-loaders fired by flintlock lanyard. The *Saratoga*'s main battery: eight long 24-pounders. Carronades: twelve 42-pounders and six 18-pounders. Effective aimed range for a long 24-pounder: 400–700 yards. At close action, carronades are devastating. Grape and canister for clearing decks. Rate of fire for a trained long-gun crew: 2–3 rounds per 5 minutes. The gun is not a machine; it is a managed combustion event requiring ten men working in sequence.

**Infantry.** Musket and bayonet. The regulars drill from Smyth's 1812 Regulations (an abridgment of the French 1791 system). Load in nine steps. Two-rank volley fire, first rank fires while second rank loads, then reverse. Bayonet charge for decisive action. Smoothbore effective range: 50–100 yards. The militia has been taught this by regular-army sergeants over three days; some of them are beginning to believe it.

**Sawmill.** The sash mill: a reciprocating saw blade in a wooden frame (the sash), driven by a pitman arm from the millwheel, cutting on the downstroke, the log carriage advancing on each upstroke by the action of the ragwheel. A full day's operation: approximately 500 boards. The labor is the logs — rolling them onto the carriage with a cant hook, setting the bail dogs to hold them, gauging the depth of cut.

**Communication.** Written orders, mounted couriers, signal guns. No telegraph. On September 11, the progress of the naval battle is communicated to the land forces entirely by sound and the smoke visible above the trees and hills to the east.

## Social World

The Champlain Valley is not wilderness. It is a working agricultural and mill economy with a small-town commercial center at Plattsburgh. The war has been going on for two years; the valley has been under periodic threat since 1812. The people who live here know what an army passing through costs.

Free African Americans are part of the social fabric: in the naval squadron (approximately a quarter of the crew at comparable engagements on the lakes), in the labor that built the Plattsburgh fortifications. Black laborers dug the redoubts. Indigenous people — Abenaki, Mohawk — have been part of the valley's population for generations, displaced but not absent. These presences are rendered accurately and without erasure or centering.

The militia are not volunteers in a romantic sense. They are men called from farms and mills and trades by the legal obligation of their county, led by officers elected from their own communities, trained inadequately, and posted to a river line that the British army is about to try to cross in force.

## Mood

Specific and non-heroic. The tone of the approach chapters is compression — ordinary life continuing until it cannot. The tone of the battle chapters is sensory overload without the command view that would make it comprehensible: the POV characters know what is immediately in front of them. The tone of the day-after chapters is the silence that follows noise, and the specific, patient inventory of what is no longer there.
