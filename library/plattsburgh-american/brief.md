# Brief

## Premise

Lake Champlain, the first week of September 1814. Two American lives move toward the same Sunday morning from different sides of Plattsburgh Bay.

**Silas Ware**, twenty-two, is a quarter-gunner on the U.S.S. *Saratoga* — Master Commandant Thomas Macdonough's twenty-six-gun corvette and the American flagship on Lake Champlain. Maine-born, he signed onto coasters at fifteen and into the Navy in '12; he has been on the lake since the squadron was built at Vergennes the previous winter. His older brother died at the River Raisin in '13, a fact he does not mention to anyone. He has charge of a section of the starboard battery — four long twenty-fours — and he thinks about powder weights, range, and the trim of his gun with the steadiness another kind of man might bring to prayer.

**Asa Beckwith**, nineteen, is a militiaman in Major-General Benjamin Mooers's Clinton County levy. A millwright's apprentice at his uncle's sawmill on the Saranac, three miles upstream of Plattsburgh village, he was called up the last week of August when Sir George Prevost's army crossed the border from Lower Canada. He knows the river — every ford, every bend, every place a man can or cannot cross in September water — better than most of the regulars now on the south bank. His father served at Sackett's Harbor and came home with a fever that did not leave. Asa has been the man of the house since he was fifteen.

The threads do not cross at the level of people. Silas and Asa never meet, never see each other, never know the other exists. They cross at the level of the day: on Sunday, the eleventh of September, 1814, between half past eight and noon, Macdonough's squadron engages Captain George Downie's at anchor in Plattsburgh Bay while Brigadier-General Brisbane's brigade attempts to force the Saranac at three points to break the American land line. The naval cannonade is audible from the upper fords. The British retreat begins after the squadron strikes. Silas spends the morning on the *Saratoga*'s starboard battery and then, when the starboard battery is largely shot to pieces, learns what Macdonough has prepared for. Asa spends the morning at Pike's Ford with two companies of Clinton County men and a regular sergeant who has been showing them how to load standing in two ranks.

The story unfolds across roughly nine days — opening with each of them in his world as it was before the army came south, and closing on the evening of the day after the battle. Neither thread is subordinate to the other.

## Length and shape

- Target length: ~40,000 words
- Number of chapters: 16

The chapters strictly alternate POV — Silas, Asa, Silas, Asa, and so on — across narrative time. The plotter chooses which POV opens the book; once chosen, the alternation holds. Chapter lengths vary with the demands of the beat (roughly 1,800–4,500 words; the average is around 2,500). Day-frame chapters tend to be shorter and quieter; the September 11 action chapters run longer.

**Structural frame.** Each POV gets eight chapters arranged around the September 11 axis:

- **1 day-before chapter** — Silas and Asa each in his world as it was before the army came south. Routine, weather, work, the small textures of a normal life that is about to stop being normal. The prose observes; it does not foreshadow.
- **5 approach-and-standoff chapters** — the British arrival, Beekmantown (Sept 6), the Saranac standoff days (Sept 7–10, with internal time elision permitted within a chapter where needed), and the early hours of September 11.
- **1 climactic action chapter** — the main beat of the September 11 engagement in their thread. For Silas, the gunfight at anchor and the maneuver that follows the disabling of the starboard battery. For Asa, the British attempt on Pike's Ford and what holds the line.
- **1 day-after chapter** — September 12. What is left. The prose observes what has changed; it does not philosophize about what it meant.

The plotter has discretion within this frame; the day-before opening and day-after close are non-negotiable.

## Voice

Third-person limited past, bound tightly to whichever POV the chapter belongs to. Each voice has its own register; the difference between the two should be felt within the first paragraph of any chapter.

- **Silas's chapters**: spare, technical. The prose of a man whose interior life is largely a record of facts about powder, range, wind, and the men around him. Few interior monologues. Naval vocabulary in stride — guns are guns, gun crews are gun crews, the ship is a ship. The interior lives in what he doesn't say, not in what he announces. When he is afraid he notes the wind.
- **Asa's chapters**: closer to the noise of his nineteen-year-old consciousness — the farmboy still half-present in the militiaman. Dialect is rare and never burlesque. He thinks in the river: in flow, in fall, in shoals — because that is what he has known. The sound of cannon fire from the bay enters his prose as weather, not narration.

Across both: restrained period prose. Action when action calls. Reflection clipped. The story trusts the reader to feel what is not said.

## Audience and register

- **Target audience age range**: adult literary
- **Voice pace**: medium — sentences breathe but do not sprawl; action scenes tighten without becoming clipped
- **Reading-grade target**: college
- **POV count intent**: two co-leads, strict chapter alternation
- **Chapter-ending convention**: mixed — some resolved beats, some mid-action, no manufactured cliffhangers. Literary fade at the close of the book.

## Constraints

- Real-world historical accuracy. Order of battle, timeline, named officers, river fords, ship classifications, militia units — all real, all rendered correctly. Specific facts come from the researcher's dossier in `canon/research.md`; the drafter and editors must adhere to it.
- Macdonough, Macomb, Mooers, Pring, Downie, Prevost, and other named historical officers may appear but are NEVER POV. They are seen from below — sometimes spoken to, sometimes glimpsed across a deck or a tree line — never interior. The story is told at the deck-and-trench level, not the command level.
- No invented intelligence subplot. No spies. No secret messages. No coded signals. The war is in the ships at anchor, the regiments on the road, the militia on the river — not in plot mechanics.
- No "fate of the nation" narration. The historical significance of the battle (it effectively ended the British plan to invade from Canada and stiffened the American position at Ghent) emerges from the action, never announced. The characters do not know what the day will turn out to mean. The prose does not tell the reader either.
- No anachronistic dialogue. Period vocabulary is captured in `canon/research.md`; the drafter must use it.
- The two POVs never meet. They share a single day. The naval cannonade is audible from the upper fords; that is the only direct sensory crossover the prose may use.
- The book ends in the evening of September 12, 1814 — the day after the battle. No coda jumping forward beyond that. Whatever each character carries from those two bracketing days is what the reader gets.
- The day-before chapters observe normal life. They do not foreshadow. They do not signal the storm coming. The reader knows the title of the book; the prose does not need to remind them. Silas's day-before is routine watch-keeping on a ship at anchor; Asa's is a day at his uncle's sawmill. Both are ordinary in the way only fiction set days before catastrophe can render ordinary.
- The day-after chapters observe what has changed. They do not philosophize about what the battle meant, do not weigh the day's significance, do not eulogize the dead. Same restraint as the day-before chapters, turned the other direction: show what is, do not announce what it stands for.
- Race and class are facts of the period, not subject lectures. Silas is white; so is Asa. Black and Indigenous people are present in the Champlain Valley in 1814 and should not be invisible, but their treatment in the prose avoids both erasure and centering.
- Period military and naval vocabulary in stride, not in italics. The reader is trusted to follow.
- The wartime period is real history; Silas Ware and Asa Beckwith are invented. Names of real places (Plattsburgh, Vergennes, Champlain, Pike's Ford, Cumberland Head, Beekmantown) may appear; names of real people (Macdonough, Macomb, Pring, Downie, Prevost, Mooers, Brisbane, Wool) may appear; no other real people should be invented into the narrative.

## Research scope

The researcher should produce `canon/research.md` covering at minimum:

- **September 6–11, 1814 timeline.** Beekmantown (Sept 6, Wool's delaying action), Saranac standoff (Sept 7–10), naval and land battle (Sept 11). Hour-by-hour for Sept 11 where the record allows.
- **American naval squadron.** *Saratoga* (Macdonough, flag), *Eagle*, *Ticonderoga*, *Preble*, and the ten gunboats: armament, crew composition, where built, who commanded each. Macdonough's biography to age 30. Gun crew roles and procedure on a U.S. corvette of the period — specifically how a quarter-gunner functioned during action.
- **British naval squadron.** *Confiance* (Downie, flag), *Linnet* (Pring), *Chubb*, *Finch*, and the twelve gunboats: armament, crew composition, manning shortcomings.
- **Macdonough's preparation in Plattsburgh Bay.** The anchor and spring arrangement, the kedge anchors astern, the maneuver that allowed *Saratoga* to wind 180° mid-action.
- **American land force.** Macomb's regulars (which regiments), the New York militia under Mooers (which units, from which Champlain Valley towns), Wool's delaying action at Beekmantown.
- **British land force.** Prevost's army composition (which Peninsular War veteran regiments specifically), Brisbane's brigade, the three Saranac crossing attempts and how each was repulsed.
- **The Saranac River line.** Lower Bridge (in town), Upper Bridge, Pike's Ford. Approximate distances, terrain features, what was destroyed and what was defended.
- **Period vocabulary.** Naval gunnery commands and gun crew nomenclature (American usage). Infantry drill terminology of the U.S. regular army and the New York militia. Militia ranks and organization. Period names for terrain features in the Champlain Valley.
- **Contemporary accounts.** Macdonough's after-action letter to Secretary Jones. Macomb's reports. The British court-martial transcripts (Captain Robertson's trial of *Confiance*). Period newspaper coverage from Plattsburgh, Burlington, and Albany. Surviving militia memoirs.
- **Race and the period in upstate New York.** Who was in the Champlain Valley in 1814. Black and Indigenous presence in the militia and as residents. What the period actually looked like demographically and socially.
- **Saranac River sawmills and milling work of the early 19th century.** How a small water-powered sawmill operated, the labor and vocabulary, the rhythm of a working day. Used to ground Asa's day-before chapter — his uncle's mill is a real working place, not a stage set.
- **Routine duty aboard a U.S. Navy corvette at anchor in 1814.** Watch organization, drills, daily life of an enlisted seaman in a squadron preparing for action. Used to ground Silas's day-before chapter — what a working day on the *Saratoga* actually was before September 11.
- **The immediate aftermath of September 11.** Casualty handling and burial practices on both sides, prisoner-of-war routing, the British army's retreat march of September 12, the American squadron's repairs and prize procedures. Period documents covering September 12–13.

Tool budget is 30 `WebSearch` and 30 `WebFetch` calls. Prioritize primary sources: after-action reports, court-martial transcripts, period newspapers, surviving memoirs. Modern secondary sources only to triangulate.
