# Developmental Editor's Letter — *A Line of Smoke*

---

## Overall Impression

*A Line of Smoke* arrives as a disciplined, accomplished draft. The premise — two men, one day, the same stakes, never meeting — could have collapsed into structural exercise; instead, the strict alternation and formal parallelism turn out to be load-bearing. The reader holds the shape of a day that neither character can see. The prose holds its register across sixteen chapters without omniscient drift, without announced significance, without the false convergence that would have been tempting to impose. The day-before chapters are genuinely ordinary. The day-after chapters genuinely resist the philosophical closure a lesser draft would reach for. When the *Saratoga* winds on her kedge and Silas crosses the gun deck to fire into the battered British line, the reader has waited for it the way Silas waited — in a chain of filed observations, each stored in its right place, each one prepared to be of use.

---

## What's Working

**Register distinction.** The brief asks for the difference between Silas and Asa to be felt within the first paragraph of any chapter. It is felt, consistently, across all sixteen chapters. Silas's chapters open in declaration and enumeration — counting shot, checking flints, noting the wind. Asa's open in sensory arrival — the river heard through alder brush, the ford smelling of cold water and yesterday's fire. The two prose textures are not merely different in vocabulary; they are organized by different cognitive modes. Silas sequences observations as a working man's attention sequences tasks. Asa notices things in the order they arrive, accumulating before settling. This distinction holds under pressure: even in the climactic chapters, where both men are under fire, the two voices are unmistakable.

**Thread 1 (The Anchor Cables).** The strongest thread in the manuscript. From the single stored observation in Ch 03 (the odd cable angle, filed without alarm and without dismissing it) to the assembled geometry in Ch 09 (the overheard carpenter's fragment — *let go the stern, cut the bower, haul on the kedge* — received through the hatch grating, partial and compressed, exactly as a man on the gun deck would receive it) to the precise rendering of the winding maneuver in Ch 13 (*She was not moving; the* Saratoga *was rotating*; the *Confiance* moving right through the gun port frame, then the *Linnet*, then open water): this thread enacts what it is about — trust as a chain of unexplained facts, care at the top of the chain and patient attention at the bottom.

**Threads 3 and 4 (Father's Charge and Beekmantown's Lesson).** Both deliver with precision and economy. The submerged log is planted in Ch 08 in a single sentence and pays off in Ch 14 in four words — the knock of a man losing his footing, and Asa not looking. Cole's metronome across four chapters — "Good." / "You waited. Good." / "Stand easy." / "They're going home." — is among the book's best sustained achievements: a character realized entirely in behavior, never explained, never inflated.

**Day-before and day-after chapters.** These are the brief's most demanding requirement, and the draft meets it. Ch 01 and Ch 02 observe without signaling; a reader who did not know the book's title would not know these are the days before. The mill world of Ch 02 — the pitman arm, the ragwheel indexing one tooth per upstroke, the hidden knot in the afternoon — is fully realized as work, not setup. Ch 15 and Ch 16 do not philosophize. "He went to find paper and ink" is the flattest, most correct final sentence in the manuscript. Asa turning south in Ch 16 before his thought of his father deepens is the right non-closure — the book ends before he walks the three miles home, because that is what Asa knows, and the book holds to what Asa knows.

**Cole.** The best secondary character in the manuscript, and arguably its most efficient piece of characterization — fully realized in behavior across four chapters: corrections delivered short, his stillness during the rout while the militia came past him on both sides, the single-word praise, the flat military deflation of the ford holding. "Check your flint" is the chapter's prize and the character's completeness in one phrase.

**Ch 12 (Before Light) and Ch 13 (Port Battery).** The manuscript's two strongest chapters. Ch 12 executes the brief's explicit specification — the naval cannonade arriving as pressure-before-sound — with more care and precision than any other passage in the book: the change in the quality of the air from the east, not a sound but a difference; the accumulation from single reports to the continuous rolling of the bay battle; the north bank empty while the smoke grows above the ridge. Ch 13 uses the loading sequence (*Sponge. Load. Run out. Prime. Fire.*) as formal structure throughout the action — Silas's consciousness runs in procedure, the prose runs in procedure, and the two are the same thing. The Fry moment — *"Fry. He notes the fact. He moves to gun #2."* — lands without inflation, as Thread 2 requires.

---

## What Isn't Working

One structural concern rises above the others.

**Thread 2 (The Brother's Absence) functions below its stated weight.** This is Silas's interior question — whether the conversion of grief into competence will hold through what the battle costs. Architecturally the thread is correctly staged across the manuscript: the absence in Ch 01, the oblique glance in Ch 09, the triple flint-check in Ch 11, the Fry notation in Ch 13, the deliberately unresolved accounting in Ch 15. The design is sound. On the page, however, the thread is barely felt.

This is partly the consequence of the brief's own disciplinary constraints working at their limit. The brief asks that Silas's interiority surface as physical observation rather than announced feeling — and it does. But those same constraints have made the thread nearly invisible to a reader who has not read the architecture document. The triple flint-check in Ch 11 reads as competence; nothing in the surrounding prose establishes it as the form grief has taken. The Fry moment lands as an inventory note — correctly — but without the thread being felt, the inventory note carries no weight of suppression. And the deliberately unresolved ending in Ch 15 (the brother never named; the grief staying in its converted form) asks the reader to feel what Silas is not allowing himself to feel — which requires the reader to have been tracking this thread through the approach chapters. A reader who has not tracked it is simply in a quiet day-after chapter with a man doing his work.

The brief allows "once per book, the prose may allow one sentence to surface" the brother. The existing candidate — Ch 09's oblique reference to "things he had stored there — in the winter before last — that did not belong to any chain that resolved into action" — is good and correctly restrained, but too oblique to activate the thread for a reader who didn't arrive primed. This is a medium-severity concern: not a dropped thread, but an underlighting that costs Silas's arc its full weight.

**Ch 07 (At Anchor) is thin.** The chapter advances Thread 1 correctly, and the moment when the crew's work stops spontaneously at the sound of distant musket fire — "the work had stopped by itself" — is one of the act's better small observations. But the chapter processes the musket fire event twice: once in the immediate scene, once in the mess-table recapitulation. The second pass covers no new ground. The speculation about the probe is plausible but arrives after the reader has already processed the event. The chapter has enough material; it uses it twice when once would suffice.

**Two instances of over-articulation in the action chapters.** In Ch 13, the prose has already inventoried all four guns' states before the sentence "The starboard battery was finished" arrives — the scene has shown this; the declaration tells it after the fact. In Ch 14, the correctly observed discolored ford water is followed by "He looked at this once and noted it" — the reader can already process the image; the annotation tells them how to. Both are single sentences and the prose recovers in both cases, but both are the style guide's explicitly named failure mode (*post-scene subtext naming*), and both should come out.

**Cyrus in Ch 15 lacks the grounding its callback requires.** "He was the same. Twelve years old and the same: the same narrow face, the same set to his shoulders" is reaching for the resonance of constancy amid the wreck of the battery. The emotional beat is real. But Cyrus is not given a face or physical signature in Ch 13, where he appears twice by name. A reader who has not been given Cyrus's face cannot feel what "the same" asks them to feel.

## Pacing Assessment

Strong across the book. The day-before / approach / standoff / climax / inventory structure builds without interruption, and the alternating POV rhythm sustains momentum through the patience of the standoff days, where the two characters' different textures — Silas's technical work, Asa's social and sonic world — provide sufficient variety to keep the wait from becoming monotonous. Ch 07 is the only flat chapter, and it is short enough not to disrupt the act. The climactic chapters (Ch 13 and Ch 14) are correctly the longest chapters in the book. One local tightening opportunity in Ch 13: the powder-spill incident late in the action extends the chapter without adding necessary pressure and may be a candidate for trimming without loss.

## Character Assessment

**Silas Ware**: alive and earned. The gun-keeping works as an organized interior life; a reader who stays close to the prose understands that a man shaped by loss and not by sentimentality is in charge of these four guns. The register holds throughout sixteen chapters.

**Asa Beckwith**: alive and earned. His river-knowledge carries the weight the brief assigns to it, and the practical similes — the ragwheel, the cant hook, the way a cut goes on a log — are his, not the author's. The chapter texture is appropriately noisier than Silas's without becoming interior monologue.

**Cole**: the best secondary character in the book. Efficient, earned, fully realized in behavior alone across four chapters.

**Macdonough**: correctly handled. Heard through the chain, glimpsed on the quarterdeck, never interiored. The anchor-spring preparation is his act of care, and the book treats it as such — observed, not explained.

**Historical figures generally** (Macomb, Mooers, Wool, Downie): all handled from below, in every instance. No violations.

**Supporting ensemble** (Fry, Mead, Veeder, Webb, Luce, Holt): lightly but correctly sketched. Present enough to be real without becoming competing protagonists.

---

## Thread Assessment

- **Thread 1 (Anchor Cables)**: Fully delivered. The strongest thread in the manuscript.
- **Thread 2 (Brother's Absence)**: Technically present across the correct chapters; functionally underlighting. This is the book's primary concern. See "What Isn't Working."
- **Thread 3 (Father's Charge)**: Delivered with precision and economy. The log pays off without announcement; the ford seen from above in Ch 16 is the right coda.
- **Thread 4 (Beekmantown's Lesson)**: Delivered. Cole's arc and the ragwheel analogy are among the book's best achievements in the Asa thread.
- **Thread 5 (Man Upstream)**: Advanced correctly through accumulating silence across five Asa chapters. The deliberate non-resolution is handled correctly: Asa turns south before the implications deepen; the book ends before he walks the three miles home.

No thread is dropped. No thread is announced. The weave is intact.

---

## Voice Assessment

The register holds across sixteen chapters. No omniscient dip, no announced significance, no philosophical close in either day-after chapter. The brief's canonical failure sentence ("he could not know yet what the day would mean") never appears in any form — not as itself, not as a variant. The two POVs remain distinct in every first paragraph.

One cross-chapter micro-tic: the phrase "filed it the way he filed a discrepancy in the garland count" appears verbatim in both Ch 03 and Ch 05. The formulation is right for the register; the exact repetition is not. This is the lowest-priority issue in the manuscript.

The wren sentence in Ch 16 — "A wren somewhere in the alders downstream was going on about something" — is very close in syntax and diction to the style guide's own exemplar for Asa's register. The wren works, and the sentence works. Worth a glance on revision to confirm it is the character's construction and not the style guide's phrase borrowed back.

## Suggested Revisions

**1. Thread 2 (primary revision).** Consider whether a single additional concrete moment in the approach chapters — not explained, not announced — can establish the connection between Silas's competence and his loss in a way a reader without the architecture document can feel. The Ch 09 oblique reference ("in the winter before last — that did not belong to any chain that resolved into action") is the existing candidate; it may need a fraction more purchase — one concrete detail that a reader who is not tracking the thread can also hold as a presence. The brief allows one sentence per book to surface the brother. Use it so that the deliberately unresolved ending in Ch 15 asks the reader to feel something specific, rather than to notice a gap.

**2. Ch 07.** Tighten or cut the mess-table recapitulation of the musket fire. Advance directly to Silas's close-action calculation. The event has been processed once; the second pass softens the chapter without adding information.

**3. Cyrus (Ch 13 and Ch 15).** Establish his face or physical signature once in Ch 13 before "He was the same" in Ch 15 makes its claim.

**4. Minor over-articulations in Ch 13 and Ch 14.** Cut "The starboard battery was finished" and "He looked at this once and noted it." The passages that precede them have done the work.

**5. Stylistic tic.** Vary the phrasing of the garland-count filing formula in Ch 05 (the Ch 03 instance, where it is first used, should remain as the original).

---

## What to Leave Alone

**The ragwheel analogy (Ch 08)**: exactly right, exactly earned, exactly long enough. This is the best instance of Asa's practical simile in the manuscript. Mill vocabulary applied to infantry drill, from his world. Do not trim it.

**Cole's single-word vocabulary throughout**: "Good." / "Stand easy." / "They're going home." This is not a voice limitation; it is the character. Do not expand it, do not vary it for variety's sake.

**Both deliberately unresolved endings**: the brother never named in Ch 15, Asa not walking the three miles home in Ch 16. The non-closures are the book's honest argument about what the war left its survivors with. Resist any impulse to close them on revision. If a line editor suggests adding a closing reflection in either chapter, decline.

**The landscape-as-emotional-register passages throughout**: the wind on the lake, the river in the dark, the smoke above the ridge, the ford seen from the limestone outcrop in Ch 16. These are the Frazier method working correctly — landscape carrying what the characters will not narrate. They are not purple prose. Do not cut them.

**Period vocabulary in stride, in both threads.** No italics, no glosses, no apology anywhere in the manuscript — for "long twenty-four," "flannel cartridge," "prime the vent," "ragwheel," "cant hook," or any other technical vocabulary in either thread. This is the O'Brian contract, and the manuscript honors it. A line editor should be told explicitly: do not add glosses or parenthetical definitions. The reader is trusted to follow.

**The structure itself.** The two men never meet. The cannonade is the only sensory crossover. The gap between Silas and Asa is the book's argument about how the war was actually experienced — in separate, non-communicating tracks of labor and terror, by people who did not know the other tracks existed. This should not be revised toward any form of convergence. The reader holds both threads; the characters do not. That is the point.
