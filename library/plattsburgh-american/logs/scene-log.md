
## 01-the-garland
**Summary:** Silas Ware's day-before chapter. Pre-dawn: he counts 18 roundshot in each of the four long-twenty-four garlands on the starboard battery, finds a chipped flint on gun one's lock, replaces it from the spare in his jacket pocket. Morning watch (4–8 AM): dawn rises over the Vermont mountains across seven miles of lake; wind southwest, light; the squadron—Eagle, Ticonderoga, Preble—lies quiet at anchor. Breakfast below: mess with Jonas Fry (gun captain, gun #1; Connecticut, 26), Webb, and Pinney (late of USS Preble). Fry complains about biscuit; Silas eats and says nothing; leaves the replaced chipped flint on the mess table. Morning gun drill (not battle drill): sponge-cartridge-shot-wad-run out-prime, twice for timing. Cyrus (powder monkey, ~12, Black) corrects a route around the ring bolt at the main hatch coaming on the third correction; his time improves half a second. Tanner (gun #3; Vermont farmer, 3 months' service) observed stopping the sponge stroke short of the base of the bore—arm wrong—watched three times, not yet corrected. Early afternoon: gunner's mate Eldridge (New Hampshire) checks the magazine tally against Silas's count; counts match; Silas notes the replaced flint; discusses Tanner's sponge problem; Eldridge says he'll have a look and does not. Afternoon watch, another drill, muster. Evening fo'c'sle: Silas sits on the bitt with Webb, Pinney, and Hals (Swedish gunboatman, visiting); lamprey boats work their nets to the south; Pinney plays jaw harp badly. Silas thinks about Tanner's sponge stroke. He does not think about his brother [Thread 2 enters]. The watch changes; he goes below; the guns are covered; he is asleep as the ship moves on its cable.

## 02-the-head-race
**Summary:** Asa Beckwith's day-before chapter (~August 29, 1814). Pre-dawn: arrives at the mill alone, checks the head race, gauges water level by the nick he cut in a flat rock two summers ago (half-inch above the nick — right), opens the sluice gate to fill the head race. Morning: uncle Eli Beckwith arrives (blocky, ~50, laconic, teaches by demonstration), they open the wheel gate together; the millwheel turns, the pitman arm takes up its stroke, the sash blade begins its reciprocating run. Morning's work: cutting second-growth pine for a Plattsburgh framing contract (storage building, south side of village); Asa sets logs with the cant hook, sets the bail dogs, the ragwheel advances the carriage one tooth per upstroke; the unnamed Abenaki log-yard worker handles the boards and warns about a suspect pine with a dark place in the bark. Noon stop: mill goes silent; Asa washes at the millpond shallows, eats dinner on the bank; observes the Saranac is wider and slower here than at Pike's Ford; notes the deep trout hole below the dam and the gravel bar 50 yards downstream as water-level indicators; "He knew the river the way he knew his hands" (Thread 3 enters). A kingfisher works the shallows. Uncle eats inside; he always does. Afternoon: the warned pine's hidden knot catches the blade on the second cut — lateral crack, carriage jumps two inches, no broken blade, no one hurt; uncle finds the burl by hand, shows Asa the bark rises before a knot (a slight hump along the grain). Asa admits he saw the dark place and set the log anyway. They lose an hour clearing the jam and re-cutting around the burl; boards come out narrow. End of day: wheel stopped, tools racked, blade covered, sawdust swept from wheel housing; Asa checks head race (down three-quarters of an inch below the nick — a full afternoon's draw; sluice closed; will know in the morning if the river recovers). Walk home: upstream along south bank at dusk, hill color fading, path found in feet not eyes. His father is three miles upstream in the fever since Sackett's Harbor; Asa has been the wage earner since fifteen; he does not think about it at length; it is the weight of the walk (Threads 3 and 5 enter). Chapter closes on the sound of the river thinning as the trees come in between Asa and the bank.
**New facts:**
- Eli Beckwith is Asa's uncle, the millwright who owns and operates the sash sawmill; approximately 50 years old, blocky and round-shouldered, laconic, teaches by demonstration rather than instruction.
- The sash sawmill is on the Saranac River approximately three miles upstream of Plattsburgh village; driven by a millrace from the river.
- The head race is filled via a sluice gate; the millwheel is driven by a separate wheel gate — two distinct mechanisms.
- Asa cut a nick in a flat rock at the millpond outlet two summers before the narrative (approximately August 1812 or 1813); it marks the August working water level the millwheel requires. He checks it every morning the mill is running.
- Water level on the morning of this chapter: half-inch above the nick at dawn; three-quarters of an inch below the nick at close of day.
- The ragwheel advances the log carriage one tooth per upstroke of the sash, moving the log into the blade by the thickness of one board per cycle.
- The sash mill has a tail race where still water collects; sawdust banks there.
- An Abenaki man of mixed descent (unnamed) works the log yard seasonally; has worked three years running at the time of the chapter; was there before Asa started (approximately 5+ years in total). He handles boards off the back of the carriage and stacks them in the drying shed.
- The mill is under contract to cut framing lumber (two-inch pine planks) for a storage building on the south side of Plattsburgh village.
- The Saranac at the mill reach is broader and slower than at Pike's Ford downstream.
- Below the mill dam there is a deep hole in the gravel where Asa has caught trout since age nine.
- A gravel bar sits approximately 50 yards downstream of the mill dam; when half-submerged the river is at working height; when clear the river is low. Asa uses this as a secondary water-level indicator.
- Asa's father is three miles upstream of the mill in the house at the edge of a woodlot, in the chronic fever he has had since returning from Sackett's Harbor.
- Asa has been the wage earner at the mill since age fifteen — consistent with his father's illness onset (also established in brief).
- A hidden knot in a pine log causes the saw blade to deflect and the carriage to jump two inches sideways; uncle demonstrates that bark rises slightly before a knot in the grain — a discoloration and a hump are the indicators.
- The path between Asa's home and the mill passes: a root by a big birch, a place where the bank steepens and the path drops a foot, and a flat stone at the ford of a spring run.
**Loose threads:**
- Thread 3 (The Father's Charge) has entered: the Saranac is established as Asa's native world; the father is present as weight on the walk home; the specific knowledge of the river (trout hole, gravel bar, ford, current) is established for later chapters.

## 03-first-reports
**Summary:** Silas's first approach chapter; September 1–5, 1814. Five compressed scenes across four days. Scene 1 (Sept 1): morning gun drill on the starboard battery interrupted by a dispatch longboat from shore; word passes down the battery in pieces — Prevost's column, Champlain NY, cavalry scouts; Silas tells the crew to finish the drill; they finish; at mess Fry says "Well" twice; Silas says nothing. Bridge (Sept 2): drills run longer; Silas watches Tanner's sponge stroke a fifth and sixth time, building the picture before correcting it. Scene 2 (Sept 2–3): Macdonough inspects the starboard battery — crouches at each touch-hole, speaks only to Eldridge, does not speak to Silas; spends notably longer at gun four; Eldridge afterward orders action-rate drills in place of maintenance; Silas runs seven rounds at action pace that evening — first round rough, third round under two minutes and a quarter; afterward stands at gun four's carriage in the quiet: "Keeping it right was not the same as keeping it ready." Scene 3 (Sept 4): gray-white smoke appears to the northeast above the tree line, approximately twenty miles off, for about an hour — consistent with the Chazy skirmish; fo'c'sle watch turned toward it; no one says what it is; Silas watches two minutes and goes back to his work. Tanner's sponge stroke corrected off-page between Sept 2–4; the corrected arm confirmed at the next drill. Scene 4 (Sept 5): ship's carpenter Fales and his mate working at the after bitt; a new set of falls runs from the after bitt at approximately 40 degrees off centerline toward the water, not the bower cable nor the mooring spring — an arrangement suggesting an anchor placed well astern and to one side so that hauling would rotate the stern around a forward point; Silas stands and looks; Fales glances up; Silas files the observation without asking. Scene 5 (Sept 5 evening): gun one at three rounds and a quarter in 4:50, improvement of a full round over two days prior; Tanner's corrected stroke audible from gun one; chapter cuts mid-drill on the muzzle coming into battery and "Again." Thread 1 (anchor cables) enters. Macdonough seen from below, does not speak to Silas — consistent with continuity #6.
**New facts:**
- Dispatch longboat arrived alongside USS Saratoga from shore on the morning of September 1, 1814, delivering news that Prevost's column was at Champlain, NY, with cavalry scouts reported on roads above the village.
- Wind on September 1, 1814 in Plattsburgh Bay: northwest, approximately 4–5 knots; lake slight-choppy; gunboats at anchor to the south riding with a low scend.
- Macdonough inspected the starboard battery approximately September 2–3, 1814 — crouched to examine the touch-holes, spoke only to gunner's mate Eldridge, did not speak to Silas; spent notably longer at gun four than at the other long twenty-fours.

Not building-fire smoke (which rises dark and rolling); consistent with musketry discharged in quantity — the Chazy skirmish.

## 04-the-levy
**Summary:** Asa's first approach chapter; late August to September 1, 1814. Six compressed scene fragments, each a distinct cut. Scene 1 (at the mill): Asa is in the log yard when Eli comes out with the handwritten levy notice from the Clinton County sheriff — "muster at Plattsburgh no later than the first day of September, 1814." Asa's name is on the list. Eli says "I'll manage the mill" and goes back inside. Asa folds the paper and puts it in his jacket. Scene 2 (that evening, the house upstream): his father is in bed with the fever running bad this week, but has a lucid hour. Nathan Beckwith listens as Asa tells him about the levy, then says carefully: "You know the river better than any man Mooers's got. Tell them. Tell them what you know." The clarity fades. Asa sits a while, then takes the musket down from the wall. Scene 3 (morning departure): Eli is at the sluice gate when Asa arrives. Without words, Eli goes into the mill and returns with Asa's father's powder horn and shot pouch — the initials N.B. on the stopper. Asa takes them without asking. Eli: "Tell them you know the river." (He echoes the father's instruction, having not heard it.) The mill wheel starts; Asa walks south. Scene 4 (the march, two days): the levy — forty or fifty men — walks south on the Beekmantown road, the lake visible to the east at intervals. Rufus Holt (35, farmer, north of county, long rifle, Eli planked his barn floor) walks beside Asa on the second day. Isaac Webb (22, blacksmith's striker, volunteered the day before the notice, five days in the company, affecting a military air) noted. The men's talk about Prevost's numbers and Macdonough's fleet; the numbers grow. Scene 5 (arrival at Plattsburgh village): newly dug earthworks on the south bank of the Saranac. Free Black laborers carrying earth in barrows, digging under a regular-army corporal. American regulars drilling two-rank formation south of the river. At the east end of the main street, through a gap between buildings: the lake, and on it the squadron at anchor in the bay — six masts visible above the warehouse roofline, two lower ones further out. Asa stops, counts, recognizes the three-masted flagship as the ship he saw from upstream in the spring without knowing it for what it was. The column moves; he moves with it. Scene 6 (first night in camp): bivouac south of the river near the lower bridge. Tent with Holt, Webb, and Veeder (broad, quiet, north townships, good ax and wool coat). The lower Saranac sounds different — faster, pressed, not the low wide sound of the mill reach. He knows it's the same river. Lies on his blanket; the father's instruction surfaces briefly; the river is the last thing he hears before sleep.
**New facts:**

- Asa's father's powder horn bears the initials N.B. cut into the flat end of the stopper with a knife. Eli has held them in the mill's keeping for over a year (since the father came home sick from Sackett's Harbor).
- Asa's father's lucid instruction to Asa: 'You know the river better than any man Mooers's got. Tell them. Tell them what you know.' (Thread 3 delivered here.)

- The Clinton County levy notice was handwritten (not printed), from the sheriff, calling all named men to muster at Plattsburgh no later than September 1, 1814.
- Rufus Holt is approximately 35 years old, a farmer from the north of Clinton County; he carries a long rifle he has hunted with every October for fifteen years; he walks at an even pace; he knows Asa slightly because Eli Beckwith planked the floor of Holt's hay barn two winters past.
- Isaac Webb (militia) is approximately 22, a blacksmith's striker from Plattsburgh village; he enlisted the day before the levy notice; he had been five days in the company when Asa joined the column; he affected a military air and used words like 'interval' and 'dress the line'; he walked near the front of the column. He will not maintain this air after Beekmantown.
- Veeder is a Clinton County militiaman from the north townships; approximately 35 years old; broad and quiet; brought a good ax and a wool coat; contributed little to conversation; shares a tent with Asa, Holt, and Isaac Webb.
- Asa's first camp is south of the Saranac River near the lower bridge on the village road. His tent mates are Rufus Holt, Isaac Webb, and Veeder.

- American regulars in blue coats were drilling two-rank formation in a field south of the Saranac River as the levy arrived.

He identified the three-masted flagship as the ship he had glimpsed from the mill in the spring without recognizing it.

## 05-spring-lines
**Summary:** Silas's second approach chapter; September 6–7, 1814 (night of the 7th/8th). Four compressed scenes. Scene 1 (Sept 6, morning watch): Silas checks the starboard battery before the bell — tompions, breeching, touch-holes, flints — and finds nothing on the lake. At half past eight he hears light artillery from the north-northeast: two or three guns, sequences of three to four reports, perhaps twenty minutes total, then gone. Webb and Pinney stop work to listen; Fry turns at his port. Fourteen or fifteen distinct reports. News comes down the battery in pieces: the militia engaged a British column north of Beekmantown; most of the militia withdrew; British on the road to Plattsburgh. Eldridge himself comes down to deliver the final piece plainly: the regulars (Wool's people) conducted a fighting withdrawal to the Saranac line; British right column is at the north bank. Silas says nothing and returns to his guns. Scene 2 (Sept 6, afternoon off-watch): Crossing the main deck, Silas sees the working party at the taffrail completing the anchor arrangement he first observed on September 5. The kedge line is now fully rigged from the stern post aft and to larboard into the water; spring lines already run from the bower chain back to two positions amidships. He assembles the full geometry: bower forward, springs from bower chain to midship, kedge astern to larboard. Works through the logic: if the bower were let go, hauling the kedge would rotate the hull — not advance, but turn — the stern drawing aft and larboard, the bow swinging on the remaining forward point. He holds this until certain, then moves on without asking. Thread 1 advanced. Scene 3 (Sept 7, morning): Crouching at gun one's port, Silas can see above the north bank's tree line half a dozen steady smoke columns — cook fires, not musketry; a large camp settled in. The lake below the smoke is empty. The ship's routine continues around the observation (carpenter's crew working aft, Eldridge forward). Cyrus is at the adjacent port watching the smoke; Silas sends him below to count the flannel supply and pass-boxes. Silas re-checks all four bores, reseats tompions, then goes below. Scene 4 (night, late Sept 7 / early Sept 8): Silas awake in his hammock past midnight; the lake's swell at anchor holds him near sleep. Cannon fire arrives from the south — the American position on the Saranac — not from the north. Felt through planking before heard in the air. Twelve reports over half an hour, no pattern in the intervals: a probe or a nervous gun captain. Gun deck around him quiet but not asleep — men holding still and listening. The twelfth report comes and silence returns. The lake outside is unchanged. Ending: British army on the north bank of the Saranac; British fleet not come; the lake north of Cumberland Head empty. Chapter closes on 'He lay still and listened to the water against the hull.' Ending mode: unresolved / tension held — the question of the British fleet's absence is present but never voiced.
**New facts:**
- The distant cannon fire Silas heard from the starboard battery on the morning of September 6, 1814 comprised approximately fourteen to fifteen distinct reports in sequences of three to four, from the north-northeast, over approximately twenty minutes — consistent with light artillery (Wool's 3-pounders at Beekmantown Corners, approximately 8 miles). The sound arrived as a faint pressure on the ear rather than a definitive report.
- Eldridge delivered the Beekmantown news personally to the gun deck on September 6: the regulars conducted a fighting withdrawal from the Beekmantown road to the Saranac line; the British right column is at the north bank of the Saranac.
- By September 6 afternoon, the kedge arrangement on USS Saratoga is fully rigged: kedge cable from the stern post aft and to larboard into the lake (anchor set well astern), plus spring lines running from the bower chain at the hawse back to two cleats at amidships positions on the hull.
- Silas has worked through the geometry of the anchor arrangement (as of September 6): he has deduced that letting go the bower and hauling the kedge astern would rotate the hull — bring it around to face the opposite direction — but does not yet have a tactical name for this maneuver or know when it would be used. Thread 1 advanced.
- By the morning of September 7, approximately half a dozen columns of white-gray smoke were visible above the tree line on the north bank of the Saranac from USS Saratoga's gun ports — consistent with the British army's encampment settling in on the north bank.
- On the night of September 7–8, approximately twelve cannon reports were heard from the American position on the Saranac south bank over approximately half an hour past midnight — spaced, without pattern, not a sustained engagement. Silas counted twelve reports. Consistent with a probe or nervous fire from the American defensive position.
- The lake north of Cumberland Head remained empty of British naval vessels as of the night of September 7–8, 1814 — the British squadron had not appeared on Lake Champlain as of this date.
**Loose threads:**

## 06-beekmantown-road
**Summary:** Asa's second approach chapter; September 6, 1814, dawn to late afternoon. Scene 1 (pre-dawn march north): the levy — roughly forty men in Asa's part of the column — steps off from camp south of the Saranac before four o'clock, marching north on the Beekmantown road in the dark. Holt is three men behind Asa; Webb is near the front. Sergeant Cole (Wool's 29th Infantry) walks the column's right side without speaking. Scene 2 (first contact): first scattered shots from the column's front, then a flank volley from the tree line west of the road — one compressed percussion that lands on the chest before it registers as sound. The column breaks; most of the militia runs south. Asa takes four steps south before stopping. Scene 3 (the staying): Cole stands still at the column's right edge, musket at order arms, and says twice, at the same volume: 'Those who are stopping, face north.' Asa stops. Holt has already stopped. Veeder stops. Four or five others. Webb does not stop. Cole says 'Good' and orders them forward through the Corners. Scene 4 (the withdrawal): three hours of Cole's sequence — move to fence, fire, reload, fall back to oak, repeat. Through Beekmantown Corners (stone wall on west side; Asa fires first shot at a red coat 50 yards up the road, does not observe the result, is already reloading), to Culver Hill where Captain Leonard's two 3-pounders are firing, the first discharge a physical concussion that hits the chest before the ears catch it, the guns switching to grape as the British column slows but doesn't stop. Asa loads behind a stone wall on the hillside with cartridge shaking in his hands; corrects his grip to Cole's two-finger technique; counts seven cartridges left. Scene 5 (Halsey's Corners): the withdrawal checks at a farmyard. British advance halts. Cole: 'Stand easy.' Asa counts three cartridges remaining. Five men held from the forty in his part of the column. Holt and Veeder among them. Scene 6 (return south): rearguard marches back in loose order; British do not follow to the river. Asa's legs steady, the shaking gone, hunger arriving. Hears the Saranac before he sees it. Scene 7 (assignment): in camp, Cole consults an officer (blue coat, epaulettes, not named). Calls Asa, Holt, and a third man — Luce, approximately Asa's age, from south of Beekmantown — to him. Assigns them to Pike's Ford; 'Two companies.' Asa asks where the ford is. Cole: 'Three miles upstream from the village. West of the road. Shallow ford. Bad ground on the north bank for a formed column.' Asa says: 'I know that ford.' Without drama. Cole: 'Good.' Chapter ends. Ending mode: TURN — the fact that the ford Cole is sending Asa to is his uncle's home water has shifted the chapter's register; stated as fact, without elaboration. Thread 3 (The Father's Charge / know the river) advances: Asa's river knowledge has become operationally useful.
**New facts:**
- Sergeant Elias Cole is from Wool's 29th U.S. Infantry, assigned to drill the Clinton County levy. He was with the levy for three days before September 6. His characteristic praise is a single 'Good' — used here when the five men stop and face north, and again at the chapter's close. He does not raise his voice; he does not waste emotion.
- Isaac Webb (militia) broke and ran with the first volley — before Beekmantown Corners, before Cole's voice found those who were stopping. He is not seen again on September 6 until he returns to camp on his own.
- Rufus Holt did not run. He was already turned north before Cole finished speaking the first time. He had his rifle up and his eyes on the road north. He is assigned to Pike's Ford with Asa.
- Veeder did not run. He is among the five or six who stayed in Asa's part of the column and held through the withdrawal to Halsey's Corners. He will be at the ford in subsequent chapters.
- Luce — approximately Asa's age, from a farm south of Beekmantown Corners — fought through the withdrawal and is assigned to Pike's Ford as the third man Cole specifically names alongside Asa and Holt. Asa is not certain of the name as of this chapter.
- The Beekmantown withdrawal route: from north of Beekmantown Corners south through the Corners (low stone wall on the road's west side; a farmyard with a well on the road's east side) to Culver Hill (Captain Leonard's two 3-pounders on the road's crown) to Halsey's Corners (farmyard, fence rails, banked ditch on the road's west side).
- Captain Leonard's two 3-pounders at Culver Hill switched from round shot to grape during the withdrawal phase as the British column slowed but continued advancing.
- The first British flank volley on September 6 north of Beekmantown Corners came from the tree line west of the road. It arrived as chest-pressure before registering as sound — physically distinct from scattered musket fire in kind, not just degree.

After a few hundred yards the bank rises at a limestone outcrop and he sees the ford whole from above for the first time — the tilt of the limestone shelf, the false cover the willows make on the north bank, the logic of the ground that held and the crossing that failed. He files it without knowing why. He thinks of his father briefly — Nathan Beckwith, Sackett's Harbor, the fever that did not leave — without comparing the two men's service. He turns south and walks back. A kingfisher goes along the river. Holt has made coffee; Asa takes a cup and stands at the bank. The sound from the northwest is gone. The river is what remains: higher note off the limestone's eastern face, lower note over the gravel trough, the sound of his whole life. Literary fade on the river.
**New facts:**
- The British retreat of September 12, 1814 was audible from Pike's Ford as a weight in the air from the northwest that resolved into the sound of feet on a road and a single drum keeping step — then faded under the river and the tree line. The sound was gone by mid-morning.
- Three British soldiers remained in the willows' upstream reach at Pike's Ford after the withdrawal on September 11 (ch 14). Asa did not approach them on the morning of September 12.
- The British encampment on the north bank of Pike's Ford was abandoned by September 12 dawn: fires cold, fire rings intact, a canteen left against a root, a section of cartridge box dropped, a folded blanket set at the base of an alder.
- Sergeant Elias Cole's line on the morning of September 12, 1814, watching the empty north bank from Pike's Ford: 'They're going home.' Four words, delivered as a fact. He then turned and walked back to the cook fire.
- The limestone shelf at Pike's Ford tilts slightly — the eastern edge approximately a half-foot lower than the western edge — so that a man crossing from the north against the current fights both the current's pull and the pitch of the stone. Asa first saw this tilt from above, from the limestone outcrop on the south bank curve upstream of the ford. Previously he had known it only by feel.
- The south bank of the Saranac rises at a limestone outcrop at a bend in the river a few hundred yards upstream of Pike's Ford. From this outcrop, the ford is visible in its whole shape — the shelf, the gravel trough, the north-bank alders and willows — from above and downstream.
- A kingfisher was observed flying along the Saranac upstream of Pike's Ford on the morning of September 12, 1814 — low, direct flight following the river's line.
- The book's final chapter closes on the sound of the Saranac River at Pike's Ford on the morning of September 12, 1814. The narrative ends here; no events after this morning are narrated.
