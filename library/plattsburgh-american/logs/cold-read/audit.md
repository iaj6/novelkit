# Panel Audit
*Prepared 2026-08-13. Auditor read all seven reviews, the verification report, the synthesis, and sixteen manuscript chapters. All manuscript claims are checked against the text; chapter citations refer to the draft/ files.*

---

## 1. IS THE PANEL BEING TOO GENEROUS?

**Score band: 6–8, mean 7.21, spread 2.** On its face this is suspicious — seven reviewers with incompatible priorities (one tracking frigate rates, one tracking airport-reader attrition, one tracking structural innovation) whose scores cluster within two points. But the suspicion is only partly warranted.

The genuine convergence underneath the narrow band is real: all seven lenses independently found the same structural achievements (kedge maneuver, ford topography) and the same weaknesses (brother sentence, Ch. 07 stall, gun-notation artifact). That kind of corroboration across incompatible priorities is exactly what a panel should produce. The narrow band reflects a novel that does not divide cleanly on genre lines — it punishes the airport reader less than most literary fiction and satisfies the verisimilitude reader more than most literary fiction — rather than a panel that has converged on a single judgment wearing seven masks.

The inflation, where it exists, is specific. The **verisimilitude lens awards 8** to a novel that contains a confirmed factual error (Confiance frigate rate, Ch. 11), a verified loading-method inconsistency across four chapters (paper-cartridge versus ball-and-patch), and a persistent arithmetic gap between "nine steps" and the actual enumeration. The verisimilitude lens correctly identifies all three failures and then scores the book 8 out of 10. Those three failures in a novel whose entire authority claim rests on technical precision should have cost it more. A 7 would be defensible; an 8, given those specific misses, is not. The lens praises the garland arithmetic so specifically that it appears to have overweighted self-consistency within a thread and underweighted the errors that fell outside it.

The **literary lens awards 8** while acknowledging Silas's mid-book structural repetition and the dangling brother sentence as "real" weaknesses. Those are real, but the literary lens also asserts — as a strength — that "The ragwheel comparison appears in both POVs across chapters." I checked every Silas chapter in the manuscript and found no ragwheel comparison. The ragwheel is exclusively an Asa device: Ch. 08 (Cole's loading demonstration read through the mill's indexing mechanism), Ch. 10 (Asa thinking explicitly about the ragwheel while drilling), Ch. 14 ("The sequence lived in his hands now the way the ragwheel's advance lived in the log carriage"). The literary lens's highest structural claim about cross-POV thematic architecture rests on a passage it did not find in Silas's chapters, because it isn't there. The 8 is built partly on a piece of evidence the text doesn't provide.

**Verdict:** Not generously biased as a panel, but the two 8-scores are individually inflated for documented reasons — one due to inconsistent weighting of technical failures, one due to an attribution error compounded into a structural claim.

## 2. GROUNDED CLAIMS VERSUS PADDING

### Grounded and verified

The following claims were checked against the manuscript and hold:

**"He did not think about his brother" appears once and never returns.** The sentence is in Ch. 01 in a specific position: Silas has just been thinking about Tanner's sponge stroke, and the sentence closes the fo'c'sle scene as a deliberate negation. It does not appear in Ch. 09, Ch. 13, Ch. 15, or anywhere else. All seven lenses are correct. The literary lens is also correct that it reads as a "pointing gesture" — the syntactic construction ("He did not think about X") implies that X is a thing one might think about, and points toward it while refusing to go there. Whether that's setup or residue, it is unresolved.

**The gun-notation shift is exactly where the panel says it is.** Ch. 01: "Gun one's lock had a chipped flint." Ch. 09 (the last Silas pre-battle chapter): spells out throughout. Ch. 11: "He came to his position at gun #1 in the dark." The hash-numeral format runs consistently through Ch. 13 and Ch. 15. The split is clean at the chapter boundary. The panel's description of this as an unnormalized draft convention is accurate.

**The kedge setup and ford topography deliver exactly what the panel says they deliver.** The kedge geometry is assembled in Ch. 09 ("Stern let go, bower cut, kedge hauled: the hull comes around and the port guns bear"), confirmed by the ship's turn in Ch. 13. The ford hazards from Ch. 08 (the gravel trough, the sinkhole under the alders, the submerged log in the willows) are activated in Ch. 14. The British soldiers step onto the submerged log, the log rolls, and the current takes them. The structural payoff is precise. Propulsion, comparative, verisimilitude, and authenticity are all right about these.

**The Confiance frigate rate is wrong.** Ch. 11: "thirty-seven guns, a sixth-rate frigate." By Royal Navy rating classification, a 37-gun ship is a fifth-rate. Sixth-rates carried 20–28 guns. The verisimilitude lens is correct: the gun count is right; the rate classification is wrong.

**The ball-and-patch/paper-cartridge inconsistency is real.** Ch. 08 establishes the loading sequence as: "Bite, prime, frizzen, barrel, ball, paper, ram" — paper from the cartridge serving as wad. Ch. 14 has: "Ball and patch. Ramrod — one full stroke to the bore's base." These are incompatible methods. Patched-ball loading requires a separate patch cloth; paper-cartridge loading does not use one. The same weapon cannot use both techniques. The verisimilitude lens is correct.

**The nine-step count is arithmetically wrong.** Ch. 08: "He loaded at full speed, all nine steps... Bite, prime, frizzen, barrel, ball, paper, ram." That's seven items. Ch. 14 enumerates: bite, powder to pan, close frizzen, powder down barrel, ball and patch, ramrod, return the rod, cock — eight items, then announces "Nine steps." The count is never right. The verisimilitude lens caught this correctly.

**The Beekmantown arithmetic inconsistency is real.** Ch. 06: "Thirty men ahead of him. Thirty behind." — implying a column of roughly sixty-one. Same chapter: "Five men total. Out of forty." — implying a total of forty. These cannot both be true of the same unit. The airport lens is the only lens to catch this. The synthesis ignores it entirely. This is a verified error with no acknowledgment in the synthesis.

**"Those who are stopping, face north." is correctly verified** in Ch. 06, said twice at conversational volume. The literary lens's "best single line of dialogue" claim is a subjective judgment but the quote is accurate.

**"The river ran." is the final sentence of Ch. 16.** Verified. The full closing context: Asa stands with his coffee, the morning sun on the ford, the sound of the British retreat gone under the river. "The river ran." is the last sentence. Six lenses praise this ending. It holds up.

### Padding (unfalsifiable or disconnected from evidence)

**"No comparable work of American naval historical fiction handles this particular technical pivot with this kind of formal restraint"** (comparative). No comparators named. Unfalsifiable. This is a superlative without a comparator, the clearest form of padding.

**"One of the most effective minor characters I've read in historical fiction in some years"** (ordinary, on Cole). This is enthusiasm without evidence, and the evidence within the same review (Cole's pedagogical method is already established by Ch. 06) actually supports the authenticity lens's competing reading that Cole is an idealized function rather than a character.

**"This is technically difficult to execute and the novel executes it across all 16 chapters without reverting to cheaper methods"** (comparative). The same lens names Ch. 07 as repetitive and Ch. 08 as too long and the dual-narrative middle as a structural sag. It cannot both execute across all 16 chapters and have a structural problem in the middle. The claim is contradicted by findings within the same lens.

**Airport's claim that Ch. 14 is a standout resting on "Check your flint."** The synthesis flags this claim as discounted because the verification report marks "After a moment he said: 'Check your flint.'" as ABSENT from Ch. 14. This discount is wrong. The line IS in Ch. 14; I found it: "After a moment he said: *'Check your flint.'*" The verification tool failed on typographic differences — the manuscript uses markdown italic formatting (asterisks) and single quotes around dialogue; the airport lens cited standard double quotes with no asterisks. The exact string didn't match. This is a tool false positive, not a fabricated quote. The panel health warning "1 fabricated quote" is incorrect for this instance. The airport lens is not unreliable on Ch. 14; its observation about Cole's line is supported by the text.

## 3. DID ANY REVIEWER FAIL TO READ THE WHOLE BOOK?

### Comparative lens: Cyrus in Ch. 15

The comparative lens claims Cyrus "is not in chapter 15 at all," and uses his absence from Ch. 15 as evidence of incomplete characterization: "The novel signals him clearly as a character of consequence, then does not give him one."

Cyrus IS in Ch. 15. The text:

> Cyrus was on the gun deck. He was carrying debris aft from the starboard battery — sections of the broken carriage from gun #4, pieces of the traverse block from gun #3, a length of damaged tackle — with the load held in both arms against his chest. He set it down when he saw Silas. He looked at him. He was the same. Twelve years old and the same: the same narrow face, the same set to his shoulders. He picked up his load and went aft.

This is a four-sentence appearance that does exactly what the literary lens called the Cyrus thread's strongest technique: building a character across chapters with very few words, none announcing themselves as characterization. "He was the same" — morning after the battle, twelve years old, back at work, same face. This is Cyrus's aftermath. The comparative lens's claim that Ch. 15 contains nothing is demonstrably false.

The synthesis accepted this error and placed Cyrus in the "what to fix" column: "consider placing him in the Ch. 15 aftermath." He's already there. This is the synthesis propagating a reviewer error without checking the manuscript.

### Literary lens: ragwheel "in both POVs"

The literary lens claims: "The ragwheel comparison appears in both POVs across chapters." The specific claim in the strongest-points section is that this structural parallel "is carried through at the metaphor level, not just the plot level."

I read every Silas chapter (Ch. 01, 03, 05, 07, 09, 11, 13, 15) and found no ragwheel comparison. Silas uses his own mechanical metaphors — a splice reading differently under tension before it parts, the trim of a fitting — but not the ragwheel. The ragwheel appears in Ch. 08 (Asa reading Cole's loading demonstration through his mill experience), Ch. 10 (Asa thinking about the ragwheel while drilling), and Ch. 14 (Asa reflecting mid-battle on the sequence living in his hands). It is exclusively Asa's analogy, native to his mill experience. When the literary lens praises the cross-POV metaphor parallel, it is describing a structural feature the novel does not have.

This matters because the literary lens's 8 score is partly constructed on this claim. Strip away the ragwheel-in-both-POVs argument and what remains is a book whose thematic architecture is carried by one strand (Asa), while the other strand (Silas) runs its own mechanical metaphors in the same cognitive register without the specific connecting device the literary lens identified. The structural parallel exists, but it's shallower than the literary lens claimed.

## 4. ARE ANY TWO REVIEWS SUSPICIOUSLY SIMILAR?

The convergence on the brother sentence, the gun-notation artifact, and Ch. 07 as the put-down point is genuine — these are the most obvious checkable features in the manuscript, and independent readers finding them is what a healthy panel should produce, not a red flag. The airport lens's Beekmantown arithmetic catch, which no other lens made, is itself evidence of independent reading.

The most substantive overlap is between the **literary and comparative lenses** on the non-convergence of the two strands. Both conclude it's a formal achievement, using similar language ("trust in the reader," "structural counterpoint"). But they arrive there from different routes: the literary lens argues from the battle-chapter parallel (Asa hears the guns cease in Ch. 12/14; we have seen in Ch. 13 why they ceased); the comparative lens argues from the full sixteen-chapter interleaving architecture. Different evidence, same conclusion — that's real corroboration, not shared prior.

The **propulsion, ordinary, and airport lenses** all identify the same mid-book structural problem (dual strands in simultaneous waiting mode, Ch. 05–09), but propulsion diagnoses it mechanically (the alternation provides no momentum dividend when both strands are still), ordinary diagnoses it experientially (Ch. 07 adds nothing Ch. 05 didn't establish), and airport diagnoses it from audience behavior (the reader who noticed the smoke columns in Ch. 03 and Ch. 05 will feel the stall). Same observation, three different analytical frames — genuine convergence.

One near-suspicious overlap: both the airport lens and the authenticity lens call Silas's emotional opacity a weakness while arriving at different severities (airport: "mechanism more than a man"; authenticity: the twin-POV voices are indistinguishable). But their prescriptions diverge (airport wants a thread for Silas; authenticity wants genuinely distinct voices), which suggests they're coming from different diagnoses of the same symptom rather than sharing a prior.

No fabrication from shared source is detectable.

## 5. THE SINGLE MOST DAMAGING TRUE THING THE PANEL IS COLLECTIVELY UNDERTIPPING

**The dual-narrative structure produces one consciousness in two settings, and the panel treats this as a disagreement when it is an empirical question.**

The authenticity lens (score 6) says directly: "A professional Navy gun captain and a rural mill worker turned militia levyman do not have identical interiority. The novel imposes its single stylistic sensibility on two radically different men and produces one voice in two settings."

Six lenses don't agree. The literary lens calls the shared mode of attention "the novel's actual subject." The synthesis treats it as a reader-expectation split: "the authenticity lens is right that the two voices are not strongly distinguished at the sentence level; the literary lens is right that this may be the point."

But "this may be the point" is not the same as "this works." I read Ch. 01 (Silas) and Ch. 02 (Asa) back to back. Both characters:

- Perceive their world exclusively through expertise
- Suppress any personal or emotional content with the same syntactic pattern ("He did not think about X"; "He noted the fact and moved on"; "He did not find it strange")
- Use the same cataloguing rhythm for sensory experience (cold of the iron, smell of the slow match; cold of the millrace, smell of the pine sap)
- Make the same type of professional observation followed by professional action with no interior bridge

If you swapped the chapter titles and replaced domain-specific details (iron/pitch/ship vs. pine/millrace/ford), you would have two chapters by the same person. This is not a claim about what the novel intends. It's a description of what the text delivers.

The panel's failure is accepting the novel's framing of its own achievement. The framing is: *two men who share a way of knowing the world.* The panel receives this as a formal achievement and scores it accordingly. The actual question — whether *two men sharing a way of knowing the world* requires two distinguishable voices to justify the interleaved structure, or whether one voice in two domains is sufficient — is exactly what the panel does not ask.

The comparative lens gets closest: "Two novellas interleaved do not automatically become one novel, and the book stops short of any convergence or contrast that would justify the interleaving in retrospect." But the comparative lens then retreats by praising the "backstory economy" as a "genuine strength" and placing the book in company with Mantel and O'Farrell. It cannot hold both positions simultaneously. If the interleaving lacks the contrast that would justify it, the formal economy is not a strength — it's a symptom of the same problem.

The truthful version of what the panel is tiptoeing around: **this is a single-voice novel structured as a dual-narrative, and the structural investment is not fully earned because the two POVs do not generate two actual perspectives.** The kedge maneuver, the ford topography, and the battle chapters are genuinely excellent. But they would be equally excellent in a single-character novel. The interleaving adds page count, a historical parallel, and a structural symmetry (two technical experts, two domains, one battle), without adding the thing a dual narrative is supposed to add: a second mind that sees the world differently enough from the first to justify the reader's investment in tracking two people instead of one.

The panel praises the structural architecture because the architecture is real and accomplished. What it does not adequately press is whether the architecture has a sufficient reason to exist — and that is the question the next draft should answer before anything else.

## CORRECTIONS TO THE SYNTHESIS

The synthesis is competent and largely accurate but contains the following errors that should be noted:

1. **The synthesis says "consider placing [Cyrus] in the Ch. 15 aftermath."** Cyrus is already in Ch. 15. The comparative lens's claim that he is absent was wrong; the synthesis propagated the error without checking. This item should be removed from the fix list.

2. **The synthesis discounts airport's Ch. 14 claim because of the "absent" verification verdict on "Check your flint."** This is a verification tool false positive caused by typographic differences between the manuscript's italic formatting with single quotes (*'Check your flint.'*) and the reviewer's standard double-quote citation. The line is in Ch. 14. The discount should be reversed.

3. **The synthesis says the literary lens's ragwheel structural claim — "the ragwheel comparison appears in both POVs across chapters" — is a genuine strength.** The ragwheel is exclusively in Asa's chapters. This claim is incorrect and the synthesis should not present it as established.

4. **The Beekmantown arithmetic inconsistency (Ch. 06: sixty-one-man column vs. "five men total out of forty") is caught only by the airport lens and not mentioned anywhere in the synthesis.** It is a verified inconsistency within a single chapter and belongs in the production-artifact list alongside the gun-notation shift.

5. **The panel health report flags "1 fabricated quote (2.6%)."** Based on manuscript examination, the airport quote is not fabricated — it is present in Ch. 14 and the verification failed on typography. Retaining this flag misrepresents the airport lens's reliability.
