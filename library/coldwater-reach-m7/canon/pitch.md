# Pitch

Coldwater Reach, Maine. October 1943 to May 1945. After a three-day autumn storm, a man's body washes up on the granite breakwater — no papers, no name, no explanation the Coast Guard bothers to provide. The town's harbormaster, Eira Bowman, fifty-two, knows what she saw at the dock the night before he came in, and she does not say.

Three miles inland, seventeen-year-old Hannah Pfeiffer wakes each morning to the sound of German voices in her father's barn — four POWs on federal labor assignment — while her Bavarian-born father retreats further into silence and her mother stops speaking altogether. In the high school on the hill, Dr. Marcus Klein, forty-seven, a Viennese refugee scholar who learned English in a Quebec internment camp, weighs every sentence he speaks in a language he still doesn't quite trust himself to own.

The body on the breakwater is the thing none of them meant to carry. Across eighteen months, through hard winters and the slow turn of the war, each of them moves through their private arc — Eira's about what it costs to hold authority in a body the office wasn't built for, Hannah's about the accumulation of small choices that makes a person, Marcus's about whether displacement is a wound or a permanent condition — while, separately, they circle what happened on that dock. The reader sees what each of them holds. None of them sees the whole.

The war ends in the background. The resolutions are smaller and older than the war.
