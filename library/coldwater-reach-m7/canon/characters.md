# Characters

## POV characters

### Eira Bowman — harbormaster, 52

Eira has been harbormaster of Coldwater Reach since November 1935, when her husband Owen drowned in that season's first major storm. The post was Owen's; she was his deputy in everything but title. After his death the town had no one else who knew the harbor, and the men who might have objected were taken by the war before they got around to it. She is the only woman serving as harbormaster on the Maine coast and has learned not to spend attention on that fact — it is a resource she needs elsewhere.

**External situation**: She runs the harbor with a competence that has stopped being remarked upon. The body on the breakwater on October 12, 1943 is a paperwork problem she is not permitted to solve, because the Coast Guard handles it and takes the record with them. It is also something she cannot stop knowing about, because she was at the dock the night before.

**Inner drives**: To hold the post she holds without making it mean more than it should — without performing either modesty or triumph. She is suspicious of her own grief (Owen has been dead eight years; she has categorized herself as past it) and correct about everything in that assessment except the conclusion. She is drawn into the body's mystery against her will and her better judgment.

**Voice**: Spare and procedural. Dialogue clipped. Her interior life reaches the prose through the language of harbor management — tide, draft, load, weather. When she is frightened she checks the barometer. When she is grieving she audits the log. The reader learns to read her technical observations as emotional data. She does not produce interior monologues in the conventional sense.

**Key relationships**: Owen (dead, but present through his handwriting in the log and the peacoat she wears). The Coast Guard officer who takes the body (foil: institutional, incurious). Hannah (peripheral at first; their paths cross around harbor business and the Pfeiffer milk deliveries). Marcus (barely acquainted early; mutual wariness; she respects precision and finds him precise in a way she doesn't trust yet).

**Physical**: She wears Owen's peacoat, re-fitted. Harbor-worker's hands. Cuts her own hair. Built for the work, in the way that eight years of physical labor in salt air builds a body.

### Hannah Pfeiffer — farmer's daughter, 17

Hannah lives with her parents on the Pfeiffer dairy farm three miles inland. She was born in Maine; her parents came from Bavaria in 1922. She is a junior at Coldwater school and one of three students taking German from Dr. Klein — the only one who arrived already knowing it from home.

**External situation**: Since April 1943, four German prisoners have been housed in the farm icehouse and work the fields under Lonzo Reed's escort. The youngest, Karl Reinmar, is nineteen and from a town in Bavaria less than fifty kilometers from where Hannah's father was born. Hannah's father has stopped speaking English at home. Her mother has stopped speaking at all.

**Inner drives**: To understand what is happening to her parents without becoming what is happening to them. She is seventeen and accumulating small choices without knowing which ones are consequential. She is drawn to Karl with the pull of the forbidden and the harder pull of recognition — he is from where her father is from, in the way she is not, and he is also nineteen and cold and far from home.

**Voice**: The most porous of the three registers. Interior consciousness at adolescent speed — faster, less filtered, more willing to drop things before they're resolved. German words and phrases surface naturally in her narration where her father's voice has reached her: single words, untranslated, not stage-managed, treated as part of the texture. Her chapters tend to open close to the body (what she hears in the dark barn, what the cold feels like, what the four-thirty routine demands) before rising toward perception and reflection.

**Key relationships**: Albrecht Pfeiffer (her father — bewildering, retreating, still loved; she watches him with the accuracy of someone who grew up reading a difficult face). Magda Pfeiffer (her mother — more frightening in her silence than Albrecht in his German; the silence feels chosen). Karl Reinmar (not a romance, or not only — a recognition across a barrier she hasn't decided whether to cross). Lonzo Reed (a comedy of inadequacy she has learned to work around). Marcus Klein (she is one of his three German students; he recognizes her father's dialect in her phrasing before she tells him anything about home).

**Physical**: Her mother's hands — wide, capable. Her father's habit of translating before she speaks, even within English. She is not slight; she does farm work.

---

### Dr. Marcus Klein — teacher, 47

Born in Vienna, 1896. An academic Germanist — his field was eighteenth-century philology — at the University of Vienna until 1938, when the Anschluss ended that. He fled to Paris, where he was caught by the fall of France; to London, where he spent fourteen months on the Emergency Committee's list; to Canada in early 1940 on a scholars' visa. He was interned at the Sherbrooke, Quebec camp from June 1940 to August 1941 under Canada's wartime internment of enemy aliens. He does not speak about Sherbrooke. He arrived in Coldwater in March 1942.

**External situation**: He teaches German (to three students who find it faintly shameful) and Latin (to twelve who find it merely difficult) at the Coldwater school. He boards with Henry and Lillian Crow — the only household in town that treats him as guest rather than specimen. He is assembling a second self in English, which is not the same as himself.

**Inner drives**: Whether displacement is permanent or only very long. By October 1943 he is beginning to suspect that the self he was trying to protect — the Viennese academic, the philologist, the man who knew which café to sit in — may not exist anymore, and that what is growing in its place might be someone he could become. He does not like this suspicion. The body on the breakwater gives him an occasion to be useful to the town in a way that has nothing to do with his credentials — the first genuinely interesting thing that has happened to him here.

**Voice**: Precise and slightly formal. The academic's habit of measuring his sentences — not for elegance but for safety. His narration carries the texture of a man who is always, slightly, translating. He uses subordinate clauses the way other people use gestures. His English is careful and somewhat overcorrect, built on the page rather than in the street. When something genuinely moves him the prose shortens — the qualifying architecture drops away and what remains is sparse and exact.

**Key relationships**: Henry Crow (his landlord and, increasingly, his most important friendship — though both men are careful not to call it that). Lillian Crow (warmer to him than Henry, which costs her something in the household economy of caution, and which Marcus is aware of). Hannah Pfeiffer (he recognizes her father's Bavarian dialect in her phrasing before she tells him anything). Eira Bowman (they meet around the body; he is curious about her procedural precision; she is wary of his scrutiny).

**Physical**: Slight, bespectacled. Dresses carefully in clothes that are slightly wrong for the Maine climate. Keeps a notebook in German. His English handwriting has become better than his German handwriting, which startles him.

## Supporting characters

### Owen Bowman (deceased)

Eira's husband. Harbormaster before her. Drowned in the November storm of 1935. He is present in the story only in retrospect and through objects: the peacoat, the log ledger (his handwriting in it through November 12, 1935, where it stops), the tide clock.

---

### Albrecht Pfeiffer, ~55

Hannah's father. Emigrated from Bavaria to Maine in 1922. He came for land — Maine farm prices were low and he had saved. He has been a successful dairy farmer for twenty years. Since the war started he has stopped speaking English at home. It is not stubbornness; it is something closer to grief and shame that he cannot name in either language. He is not a fascist and holds no ideology of that kind. He has received no word from his family in Bavaria in over two years. When Karl Reinmar arrives and names the village he is from, Albrecht tells him, in German, the name of the village where he was born. After that, something shifts in the farm's arrangement.

---

### Magda Pfeiffer, ~52

Hannah's mother. Also from Bavaria; arrived 1922. She has stopped speaking altogether — not selectively, as Albrecht has, but entirely. She works the farm with her usual efficiency and communicates by gesture and by the quality of her silence. A doctor in Rockland has no useful information. Hannah's private theory is that her mother has decided the war makes language unsafe and has declined to use it until the war ends.

---

### Karl Reinmar, 19

The youngest of the four German prisoners on the Pfeiffer farm. He was a farm boy before conscription. He does not appear to hold a formed ideology. He is from a Bavarian town within fifty kilometers of Albrecht Pfeiffer's birthplace, and when Albrecht tells him this, something shifts. He and Albrecht speak sometimes, in the evenings, in German through the icehouse window. Hannah witnesses this. Karl's English is poor and improving, absorbed from the farm work and from Lonzo Reed's vocabulary, which is a mixed inheritance. He writes letters to his mother; they are screened by the camp officer before they go.

---

### Lonzo Reed

A Coastal Patrol auxiliary in his late fifties. He was a fisherman before the war. He escorts the four prisoners from quarters to barn and back, twice daily, with a Springfield rifle he has not fired since the previous war. He is not cruel and not especially curious. He brings the prisoners cigarettes occasionally and does not admit to it. His principal occupation by January 1944 is trying not to freeze. He is a figure of gentle comedy without being a fool; he knows exactly what this job requires and does exactly that.

---

### Henry Crow, ~50

Chief custodian of the Coldwater school. His family came north from Norfolk, Virginia, in 1924. He has been in Coldwater eleven years. He is matter-of-fact about his situation there in the way people are who have learned exactly which facts are survivable. He is a private man who has made room for Marcus, and this act of making room is the book's quietest significant gesture. When he appears in chapters or as the rare POV, his voice is direct and exact; he does not editorialize about his circumstances or anyone else's. His friendship with Marcus develops in the margins: the breakfast table, the school hallway, a shared silence on the porch.

---

### Lillian Crow

Henry's wife. She runs the house and is warmer to Marcus than Henry, not because Henry is cold but because Lillian has a different calculus about what the caution costs. She was the one who told Marcus he was welcome before Henry's offer was formally extended. She makes what passes in Coldwater for good coffee, which says something about her.
