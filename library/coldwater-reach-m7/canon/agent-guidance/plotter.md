# Plotter guidance — Coldwater Reach

## POV count and rotation

Three primary POV characters (Eira Bowman, Hannah Pfeiffer, Dr. Marcus Klein), each carrying approximately ten chapters across thirty total. Rotation is **asymmetric and narrative-driven**: chapter order follows story time and story logic, not a fixed cycle. Longer or shorter runs in a single POV are expected when the story requires. Valid patterns: three consecutive Eira chapters during a period when her plot thread is accelerating; two consecutive Hannah chapters bracketing a key scene; a Marcus chapter that interrupts an Eira sequence because the story requires his vantage at that moment.

2–3 short chapters from a **fourth perspective** (Henry Crow, Lonzo Reed, or Karl Reinmar) may appear when the structure specifically requires something none of the three leads can see from their position. These are exceptional chapters, not a regular fourth rotation. Henry Crow is the strongest candidate: a chapter from his vantage can hold facts about Marcus — and about the town's relationship to both Marcus and the Crow household — that none of the three POV leads can render. If used, fourth-perspective chapters should be shorter than the standard (~1,200–1,800 words).

## Chapter-shape preferences

- **Single-scene chapters** work well for chapter 1 (establishing the body on the breakwater as the inciting fact), for chapters at key turning points in each arc, and for fourth-perspective chapters.
- **Multi-scene or time-lapse chapters** are appropriate for middle chapters advancing several weeks without a specific anchoring event. Use them to move through winter months where the story's texture matters more than its events.
- **Interiority-heavy chapters** are the expected default for Marcus. Acceptable for Hannah. Rare for Eira, where interiority is rendered through procedure rather than reflection.
- **Dialogue-led chapters** are correct when two characters are in direct contact: Hannah and Marcus in German class; Eira and the Coast Guard officer; Marcus and Henry in the evenings.
- **Avoid**: chapters whose only function is transition between two events. If a chapter has no scene with texture and weight, collapse it into its neighbor.

## Episode-vs-arc structure

Continuous arc, not episodic. Chapters flow into each other in narrative time. The mystery of the body is the spine question that runs across all thirty chapters without being resolved by any single one. The three personal arcs — Eira's arc of authority, Hannah's arc of moral formation, Marcus's arc of displacement — run parallel to and alongside the mystery spine, and each has a different rhythm:

- **Eira's arc** is the slowest to show visible change; she resists the personal implications longest and most consciously.
- **Hannah's arc** is daily and accumulative; small choices pile up across many chapters.
- **Marcus's arc** moves through a sequence of quiet recognitions — realizations that arrive as turns rather than confrontations.

The mystery should **deepen, not resolve** at the midpoint. Around chapters 14–16, each POV should learn something that reframes what they've been carrying — adding complexity, not providing answers. The full picture should exist only in the reader's assembled understanding across all thirty chapters, not in any single character's comprehension.

## Ending-mode distribution (across 30 chapters)

Target distribution:
- **Resolved beats**: 8–10 (the chapter's central question settles, even if the resolution is minor)
- **Literary fades**: 6–8 (sensory or atmospheric close; no resolution; the chapter finds its shape and stops)
- **Elliptical**: 4–6 (open question, unfinished gesture, deliberate ambiguity)
- **Turns**: 4–6 (new information or recontextualization; usually in the mystery thread or at arc pivots)
- **Cliffhangers or mid-action**: 2–3 (used sparingly; never in consecutive chapters; appropriate for moments of genuine immediate jeopardy in the mystery thread)
- **Coda**: 1–2 (aftermath or reflective beat; the final chapter is a strong candidate)

Do not stack the same ending mode in consecutive chapters. Three literary fades in a row is a pacing failure; two turns in a row dilutes both.

## Chapter word-count targets

~80,000 words across 30 chapters = approximately 2,600 words average per chapter. Expected variance:

- **Short chapters (1,200–1,800 words)**: turning-point chapters where a single scene has high weight; fourth-perspective chapters; chapters late in the book as compression builds.
- **Standard chapters (2,200–3,000 words)**: the default form.
- **Long chapters (3,200–4,000 words)**: reserved for major scenes requiring extended time in a single setting — no more than 3–4 in the full manuscript.

## Genre-specific structural conventions

This is quiet home-front literary fiction with a mystery spine. The plotter must observe these structural facts:

**The body is introduced early.** The inciting event — the body on the breakwater — occurs October 1943, when the story opens. It should be established within the first two chapters, even if not every POV learns of it immediately. The discovery can ripple through the POV sequence.

**No chapter solves the mystery.** Each POV contributes a piece. The body's identity and Eira's knowledge of what happened on the dock should be approached asymptotically: each new piece adds complexity, not resolution. Build a map of who knows what — each POV character's partial knowledge is a structural asset.

**Track who has met whom.** The three leads share a town but not a table. The plotter should maintain a roster of which characters have interacted, when, and under what circumstances, so that later chapters don't accidentally create an encounter that contradicts the careful distance the book maintains.

**The war marks time.** Place historically accurate war-news touchstones in chapters set during major events: D-Day (June 6, 1944), the Battle of the Bulge (December 1944–January 1945), the liberation of concentration camps and first press reports (April 1945). These appear as radio or newspaper texture in the background — not as plot, but as the clock the characters live inside.

**Protect the unresolved thread.** The brief requires that at least one thread remain genuinely open at the close. The plotter should designate which thread that will be (the body's full identity? Eira's disclosure? one character's ultimate fate?) and then flag it explicitly in the outline so that no subsequent chapter accidentally closes it. Mark the designated unresolved thread in the outline.

**The ending is before VE Day.** The final chapter is set in late April or early May 1945. The war's ending is approaching but not yet here. The story's resolutions — modest, earned, partial — should feel like what three people reach after eighteen months of carrying something, not like the war's own resolution. The scale is personal.

**No spy thriller mechanics.** Any outline beat that involves coded messages, submarine contact, Nazi agents, or covert network activity contradicts the brief and must be cut regardless of dramatic payoff.
