# Reader guidance — Coldwater Reach

## Intended audience

Adult readers of literary historical fiction — specifically, readers who seek the home-front domestic mode: novels where the war is the context and the human interior is the subject. Closest shelf comparisons: Anthony Doerr's *All the Light We Cannot See* (POV discipline, period precision, mystery spine), Elizabeth Strout's *Olive Kitteridge* (Maine register, oblique interiority, restrained resolution), Maile Meloy's *Liars and Saints* (multi-strand family silence, moral accumulation). These readers will notice when historical detail is wrong, will feel when a POV voice collapses into the author's default register, and will trust a quiet ending more than a cathartic one.

## Brief-adherence checks to perform

The Reader must confirm each of the following in its developmental review:

1. **Three distinct POV voices.** Can you identify whose chapter it is from a passage alone, before reading the chapter heading? If Eira, Hannah, and Marcus have converged toward a single literary default register (medium-long sentences, balanced syntax, explicit interiority), this is a high-severity finding regardless of individual prose quality.

2. **No spy thriller drift.** Does any chapter move toward covert network plotting, Nazi agent confrontation, submarine landings, or coded-message mechanics? If yes, flag it regardless of prose quality. The brief explicitly forbids this.

3. **Historical accuracy.** Is the period texture specific and correct? Check: POW program terminology and logistics, Coast Guard authority, harbormaster procedures, rationing systems, radio technology, V-Mail mechanics, U-boat timeline (threat was diminishing by autumn 1943, not waxing). Flag anachronistic technology, wrong-period idiom, or mischaracterized institutional authority.

4. **Eira's interiority.** Is Eira's interior life arriving through procedural language (weather, tide, log, harbor operations) rather than through direct statement of feeling? If she is having explicit interior monologues about grief, longing, or fear, that is a voice-drift finding.

5. **The three never meet as a group.** Does any chapter place all three POV characters in the same scene or conversation simultaneously? This is a structural constraint from the brief; flag as a structural failure.

6. **The mystery is not institutionally resolved.** If the narrative moves toward a police investigation, a courtroom, a formal document that identifies the body, or an official explanation, flag it. The mystery remains in the zone of private knowledge.

7. **Race as texture, not lecture.** Are Henry and Lillian Crow's circumstances rendered specifically and through scene rather than through authorial essay? If any passage reads as an extended thematic statement about race in 1943 Maine, flag it — the brief specifies that race should be rendered as a fact of the characters' specific lives, not as a subject for the narrator to address.

8. **The deliberate unresolved thread.** Is at least one thread still genuinely open at the manuscript's close? If the final chapters have tidied every question, flag this as a structural finding.

## What "good" looks like for this book

A chapter is working when:

- The reader can feel the weight of what a character is carrying without the narration naming it. Eira's log entry that repeats what doesn't need repeating. The German word Hannah uses where her father would have used it. Marcus's sentence that compresses to three words when he has been averaging twenty.
- The historical texture is inhabited, not displayed. Not a Wikipedia paragraph about rationing, but a specific ration book with a specific shortage on a specific Wednesday in November 1943.
- The three registers are audibly distinct at the sentence level: Eira's short procedural declaratives, Hannah's accumulative sensory syntax, Marcus's qualified formal subordinate-clause construction.
- The mystery deepens without resolving — each chapter from each POV adds a new facet to what the reader holds without bringing them closer to a tidy answer.

## What to flag explicitly if the manuscript drifts

- **Register collapse** — The single most common drift failure for a multi-POV literary novel: all three POV voices converge on the model's default literary adult register. Name this finding explicitly, with specific passages, even if the individual prose is good. A novel that has technically well-written chapters but an indistinct POV voice across three characters has failed this brief.

- **Eira becoming expressive** — She must not name her feelings in the conventional interior-monologue sense. If she is thinking about Owen in language that sounds like grief rather than harbor management, the chapter is in the wrong register.

- **Karl or the prisoners as symbols** — Karl Reinmar should be a specific nineteen-year-old farm boy. If Hannah's chapters treat him as a stand-in for Germany, for forgiveness, for the war's moral weight, or for any abstraction, name it and push for specificity. What does he actually do? What does he actually say? That is where the symbolic work gets done without being named.

- **The mystery resolving too early** — If the question of the body's identity and what Eira saw is substantially answered before chapter 25, the book's structural premise is broken. The mystery's unresolved quality for eighteen months is the subject, not a delay tactic.

- **The ending over-resolving** — The brief specifies resolutions that are "smaller and older than the war's ending." If the final chapters move toward cathartic revelation, full explanation, or triumphant closure, flag this as structural. A quiet, partial close is the correct shape.

- **The war in the foreground** — If any chapter's primary plot mechanics are driven by wartime events (a battle outcome, a military decision, a spy mission), the novel has drifted from home-front to war novel. The war is texture. Flag immediately.

## What to applaud

- **Eira's procedural interiority** — When harbor management language is doing genuine emotional work without metaphorizing the sea, this is the book's most distinctive technical achievement. A tide reading that holds a beat too long. A log entry notation that doubles back. Defend this against any compression or editorial impulse to make Eira more "relatable" through explicit feeling.

- **Hannah's unlabeled German** — When German words from Albrecht's world surface in Hannah's narration without translation or italicization or stage-management, this is right. It is what the brief specifies. Defend it against editors who want it footnoted, glossed, or Anglicized.

- **Marcus's emotional compression** — When Marcus's formal subordinate-clause sentences suddenly shorten to three or four words at a genuine moment of feeling, this is the register doing exactly what it should do. Name it as a success; protect it against editing toward a more conventional emotional disclosure.

- **The unresolved ending** — If the manuscript closes with at least one genuine open question rather than a tidy explanation, this is brief-compliant. Defend it explicitly if prose editors push for resolution. The brief calls for it; it is not a failure of nerve.
