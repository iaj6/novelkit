# World

## Coldwater Reach

An invented fishing village on the mid-coast of Maine, east of Penobscot Bay, where a tidal river called the Reach meets a working harbor. Population roughly nine hundred in 1943 and declining — men gone to the war, farms consolidating, the younger generation interrupted. The harbor supports lobster boats, two small draggers, and a twice-weekly mail boat from Rockland. The harbormaster's office sits at the head of the dock: a salt-weathered shack with a BC-1206 radio set, a tide clock that loses four minutes a week, and a log ledger whose earliest entry is June 1891.

The breakwater is granite, two hundred yards long, built by the WPA in 1937. It ends in a fixed red light visible from five miles offshore. On or about October 12, 1943, a man is found at its seaward end.

**The town proper** runs up the valley from the harbor: a Congregational church, a combined middle-and-high school (enrollment sixty, grades 7–12), a hardware store, a general store doubling as post office, a diner called Merrill's, a Coast Guard auxiliary station. Henry Crow's white clapboard house sits on the back street two blocks uphill from the school. Marcus Klein's rented room is on its second floor, east-facing, and smells of woodsmoke and cold books.

**The Pfeiffer farm** lies three miles up the inland road — an 1880s farmhouse, red barn, silo, and a renovated icehouse that since April 1943 has served as quarters for four German prisoners of war. The icehouse has a wood stove, four cots, one window with a hasp. The prisoners are escorted from their quarters to the milking barn and back by Lonzo Reed, twice daily. By October 1943 the arrangement has settled into a routine that feels nearly ordinary, which is the most unsettling thing about it.

**The high school classroom** where Marcus teaches is on the second floor, east end. German enrollment: three students, all girls. Latin: twelve. The room smells of chalk, damp wool, and the inadequacy of the coal furnace.

## Period texture (1943–1945)

**Rationing**: Meat, butter, sugar, gasoline, rubber, coffee. Local ration boards issue books by household. The Pfeiffer farm has advantages — dairy and beef — but faces price controls and production quotas. Marcus's ration book runs lean on everything except coffee substitutes.

**Communication**: V-Mail for servicemen's correspondence (letters photographed to microfilm, printed on arrival, to save shipping weight). The town telephone runs on a party line. Radio is the primary news source: the Zenith console in Henry and Lillian Crow's parlor, the shortwave set in Marcus's room. The BBC World Service and CBS Radio define the frame through which the war is known.

**The sea and U-boat threat**: By autumn 1943 the North Atlantic convoy threat has eased substantially; the Navy's hunter-killer groups reversed the 1942 losses. No more tankers burning off Cape Cod. But the memory of those years — merchant seamen washing up on New England beaches, ships ablaze thirty miles offshore — has not cleared from the people who watched that coast. Men who still scan the horizon in late 1943 are not being irrational.

**POW labor program**: Under the Geneva Convention, German enlisted prisoners could be compelled to work (officers could not). Prisoners were paid eighty cents a day in canteen coupons. Camp Houlton in Aroostook County was Maine's primary POW facility; satellite work groups were placed on farms and at canneries around the state under guard. Guards on farm details were often older men: limited-duty soldiers or Coastal Patrol auxiliaries.

**Coast Guard and Coastal Patrol**: By 1943 the Coast Guard had absorbed most beach-patrol duties. Lonzo Reed holds a Coastal Patrol auxiliary appointment — sufficient authority to escort prisoners, insufficient for much else.

**Emergency Committee in Aid of Displaced Foreign Scholars**: A real organization (New York) that placed refugee European academics in American positions. Marcus was on their rolls from autumn 1940, placed at Coldwater in March 1942 when the previous German and Latin instructor enlisted.

## Seasonal arc

The story opens in autumn 1943. It moves through the hard winter of 1943–44 (harbor inner cove icing over by late January), the mud season and thaw of spring 1944, the brief Maine summer, another autumn, the grinding winter of 1944–45, and closes in late April or early May 1945, as the news from Europe begins arriving in ways that can no longer be set aside.

## Sensory world

The harbor: diesel and herring bait and salt. Dock lights kept dim (blackout practice, relaxed from the 1942 peak but still cautious). November fog thick enough to muffle the foghorn. Ice forming in the inner cove by late January. The mail boat's Tuesday and Friday arrivals are the week's event in deep winter.

The farm: silage and hay and cold mud. Manure and the steam of the milking barn in the dark. The sound of cows at four-thirty a.m. is something Hannah has heard every morning of her life. The icehouse, renovated and drafty, smells of pine sap from the new boards.

Marcus's room: woodsmoke, library paste, the particular density of books in cold air. The shortwave hissing at night, finding and losing signals from across the Atlantic.

## Mood

Not nostalgia. Not combat. The texture of sustained waiting: people living lives that are half suspended, who have learned to treat the disruption as the new floor. The war is always in the room — in the ration books, in the missing men, in the German voices in the barn at four-thirty a.m., in the shortwave dial glowing at midnight. It is never the plot. That gap between presence and plot is the book's specific territory.
