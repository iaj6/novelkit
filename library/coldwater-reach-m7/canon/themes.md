# Themes

## 1. Authority and the body that holds it

Eira Bowman holds a post that was built for someone else's body, and she has been holding it for eight years. The question is not whether she is competent — she is, and the town has stopped remarking on it, which is the closest it will come to acknowledgment. The question is what authority actually requires of the person who exercises it, and whether competence is sufficient justification or only the baseline. Her arc runs toward the recognition that authority, when taken seriously, makes claims that go beyond the institutional. The body on the breakwater makes a claim on her that the harbormaster's office does not authorize her to meet, but which the harbormaster's office is exactly why she sees. She cannot put the office down to answer the claim, and she cannot answer the claim from inside the office. This is her predicament, rendered through tide tables and weather logs and the specific weight of Owen's coat.

## 2. Displacement and its possible end

Marcus Klein has been in motion since 1938 — Vienna, Paris, London, Sherbrooke, Coldwater — and has organized his interior life around the assumption that this motion will eventually reverse and return him to himself. Sherbrooke interrupted that assumption but did not end it; he carried it through the wire and out the other side. By autumn 1943 he is beginning to suspect the assumption is wrong: that the self he was trying to protect — the Viennese academic, the philologist, the man who knew which café — may no longer exist. What is growing in its place might be someone he could become. He does not like this, because becoming someone new requires conceding that who he was is gone. The question his arc poses is whether displacement is a wound with a terminus or a permanent condition that, after long enough, becomes a person. If the latter, the grief for the lost self is not a way station to recovery but a project with a different shape entirely.

## 3. The small choices that make a person

Hannah's arc is less dramatic and more consequential than either of the others. She is seventeen and at the edge of a series of small decisions whose significance she doesn't yet know how to assess. Whether to speak to Karl in German. Whether to tell her father what she has seen through the icehouse window. Whether to show Dr. Klein the letter she found in the barn. Whether to say, in class, what she actually understands about the language being discussed. None of these decisions is a plot climax. None of them is visibly momentous at the time it occurs. Together they constitute a moral formation. The book does not pass judgment on the outcome or name what kind of person Hannah is becoming. It renders the accumulation and trusts the reader to understand what they are watching.

## 4. The war as texture, not event

The war is present on every page and in the foreground of none. It is the reason the men are gone and the prisoners are in the barn and Marcus is in Maine and the rationing is lean and the telegraph is feared. It is the reason Hannah's parents went quiet and the reason Marcus crossed three countries and an internment camp to get here. The story does not follow the war's logic (investigation, espionage, confrontation, resolution). It follows the home-front logic, which is: people who do not know how long this will last continue being people, badly and well and inattentively and with great attentiveness, in the specific texture of the present they cannot escape. The war will end. It will end in the background. The resolutions the story offers are smaller and older than that.
