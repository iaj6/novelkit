# Chapter 05 — Tuesday German

**One-line summary**: German class with Dr. Klein; he notices something in Hannah's phrasing that he doesn't comment on; the walk home past the harbor; her mother's silence at supper.

**POV**: Hannah Pfeiffer
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: Coldwater school (second floor, east classroom); the road from town to the farm; the Pfeiffer farmhouse
**Time**: Late October 1943 (a Tuesday; German class is two days a week)

**Chapter shape**: Multi-scene
**Ending mode**: Literary fade
**Word target**: ~2,800

---

## Goal / Obstacle

**Goal**: No dramatic goal — Hannah is in the middle of a week, in the middle of a season, accumulating. She goes to class; she comes home. What she is accumulating is not clear to her yet.
**Obstacle**: The class itself, in its faint social awkwardness (three girls who know that German is the wrong language right now, one of whom actually knows it). The walk home past the harbor where the body was found. Her mother's silence at the table.

---

## Scenes

### Scene 1: German class (the classroom)
Tuesday afternoon. The classroom on the second floor: chalk smell, damp wool, coal furnace inadequate against the October cold. Three students: Hannah, Doris Hatch, Louise Pelletier. Doris and Louise are not hostile to German; they are mildly ashamed to be studying it. They translate with the care of people trying to give as little of themselves as possible to the exercise.

Dr. Klein sets a passage — a letter from a nineteenth-century text, nothing inflammatory. Hannah translates quickly, in the Bavarian-inflected German that is her father's. The inflection is different from the textbook's standard — a slight softening of certain vowels, a grammatical construction that places the verb differently than Hochdeutsch. Dr. Klein pauses. He does not correct her. He says, in German: "Your accent places you south, east of Munich." She says, in English: "My father is from Bavaria." He says: "Yes." That's all.

Something shifts slightly. She doesn't know what. He moves on to Doris and Louise.

After class, Hannah stays to ask about the grammar of one construction. What she really wants is to know if he noticed what she thinks he noticed. He answers the grammar question accurately and doesn't offer anything more. She puts on her coat and goes.

### Scene 2: The walk home (past the harbor)
Late October, the light already going by four. She takes the road past the harbor — not necessary, slightly out of the way, but she has been making this detour since October 12. The breakwater from the road: a granite line across the inner cove, the fixed red light at the end just lit as the dusk comes. Nothing different about it. Nothing marked. The body is gone three weeks and the breakwater is just the breakwater.

She doesn't stop. She looks at it for a few seconds and keeps walking. This is not dramatic. It is a habit forming.

### Scene 3: Supper (the farmhouse kitchen)
The evening meal. Her father eats. He says something to her in German — something about the south field, which requires attention tomorrow. She answers in English: she'll do it before school. He nods. Her mother sets food down and sits without speaking, eats, clears. Magda's silence has its own quality: it is not angry and it is not absent. It is the silence of someone who has made a decision about language and is holding to it. Hannah's private theory is that her mother has decided the war makes language unsafe and has declined to use it until the war ends. She doesn't know what to do with this theory.

After supper Hannah sits with the radio — the kitchen set, not Zenith quality, the signal wobbly. News from the Pacific mostly. She turns it off at eight. Her father has gone to the icehouse window. She can hear his voice through the wall, low and careful, speaking German to Karl.

**Close (literary fade)**: Hannah upstairs. The four-thirty alarm already set. What Dr. Klein noticed — or didn't say — about her German still sitting in her. Her father's voice through the floor, talking to Karl through the window in the dark. The season: the leaves off the maples, the cold coming in, October going to November. She falls asleep without having named any of what she's feeling. The chapter fades on the dark room and the faint sound of her father's voice.

## Outcome

The chapter does two things: establishes the German class relationship (Marcus notices her Bavarian accent; she realizes he has; he doesn't follow it up yet) and establishes her growing habit of watching the breakwater. Her world accumulates without organizing itself. The fade at the close is the chapter's appropriate ending — nothing resolved, nothing open in a dramatic sense, just the texture of her life in October 1943.

---

## New Question Raised

What will Marcus do with what he noticed about her German? What is Hannah's relationship to her father's language — is it hers, or is it borrowed?
