# Chapter 14 — A Report from Portland

**One-line summary**: A letter from the Coast Guard District office arrives: a Nova Scotia harbor reported a man missing from a vessel heading south in late September 1943; the description is partial; Eira files it privately; the mystery reframes from *who was he* to *where was he going*.

**POV**: Eira Bowman
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: The harbormaster's shack; the dock; Eira's house (evening)
**Time**: March–April 1944

**Chapter shape**: Single scene (with brief before and after)
**Ending mode**: Turn
**Word target**: ~2,400

**Note**: This is the midpoint reframe chapter. The mystery deepens, not resolves.

---

## Goal / Obstacle

**Goal**: A normal week in early spring — the ice beginning to go from the inner cove, the first lobster boats thinking about going back out, the harbor returning to life.
**Obstacle**: The letter arrives from Portland and Eira cannot un-know what it contains.

---

## Action Beats

1. **Before: the harbor in early spring**
   Late March into April. The cove is thawing — ice pulling back from the edges, the water dark and cold. She's been watching this for weeks. The radio more active as the boats plan to go back out. There is a quality of things opening that she is used to and which she has learned to receive without attaching too much to it: spring comes; she works. She has been harbormaster for eight and a half years.

2. **The letter arrives**
   Friday mail, April. An official envelope from the First Coast Guard District, Boston, via the Portland auxiliary. She opens it at the desk.

   The letter is administrative and brief. It asks whether she can provide any information regarding an inquiry received from the Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, port authority: a vessel reported missing in September 1943. The vessel (a small wooden motorboat, approximately eighteen feet) departed Yarmouth approximately September 28, 1943, reportedly heading south to make landfall in Massachusetts. The sole occupant: a male, middle-aged, described as *of continental European appearance*, with limited English. He was not reported missing immediately — the Yarmouth party who lent or rented him the vessel was uncertain of his final destination and assumed he had arrived. The inquiry comes through because a body fitting some of the description (middle-aged male, civilian, no identification) was recovered on the Maine coast in October 1943, and the District is routing the inquiry to affected ports.

   She sets the letter on the desk. She reads it again.

3. **What this tells her**
   The man came from Yarmouth, Nova Scotia. He left in late September 1943, heading south. He had *limited English*. He was *of continental European appearance*. He was alone in a small motorboat.

   The body on the breakwater: middle-aged, civilian, no identification. Found October 12. A three-day blow had preceded it.

   The boat she saw on October 11: small, wooden, no running lights, tied briefly at the dock's far end. The man who walked toward the breakwater.

   The math: September 28 from Yarmouth. A rough Atlantic crossing at this time of year in a small motorboat. If he ran into weather — which the end of September routinely provides on the coast of Maine — he could have taken weeks to make the distance. October 11 in Coldwater Reach, barely moving, barely able to dock. He may have been ill already. He may have been past the point where arrival mattered.

4. **The private filing**
   Eira does not respond to the letter as directed. She should — officially, she should confirm what she knows about the October body and direct the Yarmouth inquiry to Lt. Ennis's case file in Portland. She has the authority to do exactly this. She sets the letter in the back of the desk drawer, beneath the freight manifests from January. She will respond. She simply does not do it today.

5. **Evening (close — turn)**
   She sits in her own kitchen that night with the knowledge of what she now knows. The man was not from Maine. He was not from America. He came from Yarmouth, he was heading south, he had limited English and a continental European appearance, and he ran out of road at Coldwater Reach. He had no papers because — she thinks about this for a long time — he had either destroyed them or never had American ones. He was traveling without documentation by design, or necessity, or both.

   The mystery reframes. It is no longer *who was this man?* entirely. It is: *where was he going?* And: *who is waiting for him there?*

   She writes nothing in the log. She goes to bed. The chapter turns on this reframe: the dead man was in transit. He was heading toward something. He didn't arrive.

---

## Outcome / Turn

The midpoint turn: the mystery is deepened and reframed. The dead man was traveling from Canada, heading south, with no papers and limited English. He had a destination he didn't reach. This raises the urgency of the mystery for the reader while providing no resolution. The three-POV assembly of this picture continues: Eira holds the geography; Marcus holds the scholarly network possibility (Feld letter, Felix K.); Hannah holds the letter's phrase about "those who tried to go."

Who is waiting for the dead man in Boston or wherever he was headed? Is there a contact who received no arrival? Will Eira respond to the Yarmouth inquiry?

## Continuity Notes

- The Yarmouth, NS, inquiry: the vessel departed approximately September 28, 1943. Male occupant, middle-aged, continental European appearance, limited English, no papers filed. Destination: Massachusetts (loosely).
- The letter is from the First Coast Guard District, Boston, via the Portland auxiliary.
- Eira does not formally respond to this letter. She files it in the desk drawer. In Ch 26, when she writes her addendum, she will also formally respond to the Yarmouth inquiry (this can be implied, not dramatized).
- The letter does NOT confirm the dead man is Felix K. It does not contain a name. The match is probabilistic, not confirmed. **PROTECTED: the identity stays open.**
- The phrase *continental European appearance* is the letter's language, not Eira's.
- The timeline math: September 28 departure from Yarmouth; a difficult coastal voyage in autumn weather; arrival at Coldwater October 11. This is approximately thirteen days. Plausible for a small motorboat navigating carefully in bad weather.
- This chapter's information is Eira's alone — Marcus doesn't know about it; Hannah doesn't know about it. The reader assembles the picture across all three POVs.

## Voice Notes

Eira's chapter: spare, procedural. The letter should arrive without fanfare. Her processing of the letter is done in the language of logistics (what the harbor master knows about boats, weather, distance, time) — not in the language of emotion. The turn arrives at the close: *the dead man was heading somewhere. He didn't arrive.* This should land in the prose as a fact, not as a named revelation. The sentence "She writes nothing in the log. She goes to bed." is the right close.
