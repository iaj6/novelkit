# Chapter 10 — A Letter from New York

**One-line summary**: A letter from Dr. Feld in New York describes scholars lost in wartime transit; one partial name, last documented in Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, in September 1943; Marcus files it without yet connecting it to the body.

**POV**: Dr. Marcus Klein
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: The Coldwater school; the Crow house; Marcus's room
**Time**: December 1943

**Chapter shape**: Interiority-heavy
**Ending mode**: Literary fade
**Word target**: ~2,800

---

## Goal / Obstacle

**Goal**: Marcus is trying to get through the last weeks before the school's Christmas break. His goal in this chapter is low-key: answer correspondence, manage his students through their examinations, not freeze in his room.
**Obstacle**: The letter from Dr. Feld arrives on a Tuesday and does not leave him alone.

---

## Scenes / Movement

### Scene 1: The letter arrives (the school)
Tuesday mail comes to the school through the combined general store and post office; the school secretary sorts it. Dr. Feld's letter is from the Emergency Committee in Aid of Displaced Foreign Scholars (New York). Marcus has been on their rolls since autumn 1940; correspondence with Feld has been intermittent — practical letters about his placement, occasional bulletins about the wider network of displaced scholars.

This letter is different in tone. Feld is writing to Marcus partly because he knows Marcus, partly because he thinks Marcus might be able to confirm or deny information about a former colleague. The letter describes the problem of tracking displaced scholars who fell out of the network's records during 1942-43: some arrived at their placements and simply didn't notify Feld's office; some are unreachable in Europe; some appear to have been in transit and then stopped being in transit, with no subsequent documentation.

One specific case: a Dr. Felix K. (Feld does not give the full surname, citing privacy practice), a linguist of German-Jewish origin, formerly of Leipzig, last documented in a letter to the Committee postmarked Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, September 22, 1943. He had notified the Committee of his intention to travel south by sea to reach Boston by late October. He had a contact in Boston. He has not been heard from since. Does Marcus know anything of him?

Marcus reads this three times. He does not know a Felix K. from Leipzig — or rather, he half-remembers a man of that description at a philology conference in Vienna in 1936, though the name doesn't quite surface. He sets the letter on his desk.

### Scene 2: The school through December
Marcus teaches his examinations. Hannah Pfeiffer translates a passage from Goethe's letters with precision that continues to interest him; she has, he thinks, the literal translator's gift — she gives you the German's grammar rather than its sense. He does not comment on this yet.

Doris Hatch and Louise Pelletier are adequate. Latin is going well; twelve students who find it merely difficult are gratifying after three who find German shameful.

He has a brief exchange with Henry in the hallway about the window in the German classroom (Henry has fixed it). They discuss nothing else.

### Scene 3: His room (the shortwave and the letter)
Evening. Marcus reads Feld's letter again. He gets his notebook and writes, in German, what he knows: Felix K., Leipzig, linguist. Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, September 1943. Intended to reach Boston by October. He writes: *I do not believe I knew him*. Then, below: *Yarmouth to Boston by sea. The coast of Maine is between.*

He sits with this for a while. The shortwave. He does not find the BBC tonight; he finds a clear American signal — news from the Pacific — and listens to that instead.

The connection to the Coldwater body is available to the reader at this point, but Marcus does not make it yet. He is aware, vaguely, that there was a body in October. He is aware, vaguely, that the man came from somewhere. He does not put these two things together. This is realistic — he has no reason to connect them; the body was an unknown man, and unknown men wash up on Maine coasts.

**Close (literary fade)**: Marcus puts Feld's letter in the folder where he keeps the Emergency Committee correspondence. He closes his German notebook. Outside his east-facing window, December dark. He thinks about Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, which he has not been to and does not know. He thinks about the particular meaning of *last documented in* — the bureaucratic phrase that means someone went somewhere and stopped being accountable to anyone. He knows something about this. He has been *last documented in* a place himself, before Sherbrooke. He turns off the lamp. He doesn't write anything more. The room is dark and cold and smells of woodsmoke and the particular density of cold books.

## Outcome

The Feld letter is planted. The reader can make the connection to the October body if they choose; Marcus does not. The chapter deepens Marcus's interiority and his relationship to the Emergency Committee network while remaining, appropriately, at one remove from the mystery.

---

## New Question Raised

If the man on the breakwater was Felix K. — will Marcus ever make this connection? And if he does, what will it mean for him?

## Voice Notes

Marcus's register: precise and careful, with the academic habit of measuring every sentence for what it betrays. The letter from Feld should be paraphrased in Marcus's voice, not quoted directly — he summarizes it with the scholar's habit of extracting essentials. The moment of *Yarmouth to Boston. The coast of Maine is between* should arrive briefly in his notebook notation, short and exact, not expanded. The chapter ends on his silence, which is the appropriate form for Marcus's most significant moments.
