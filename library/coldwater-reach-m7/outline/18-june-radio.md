# Chapter 18 — June Radio

**One-line summary**: D-Day news comes through June 6; Hannah's father sits at the radio for hours; Karl is unreachable at the far end of the field; the chapter ends on her father's single sentence in German, spoken to no one in particular.

**POV**: Hannah Pfeiffer
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: The Pfeiffer farm — barn, kitchen, field
**Time**: June 6, 1944

**Chapter shape**: Multi-scene (the day from morning to evening)
**Ending mode**: Elliptical
**Word target**: ~3,000

---

## Goal / Obstacle

**Goal**: No particular goal — this is a day when the news overwhelms ordinary intention. Hannah is trying to get through a day that has turned into something different from a day.
**Obstacle**: What D-Day means is too large for any of the ordinary frames she has. Her father's reaction. Karl's silence. The fact that she cannot know what Karl is feeling and cannot ask him today.

---

## Scenes

### Scene 1: Early morning (the farm unaware)
Before the news: four-thirty, the milking, the ordinary dark. Hannah in the barn. Lonzo Reed arrives at eight with the prisoners. The work detail: June farm work — the early planting mostly done, now weeding, the south field and the hay cutting beginning. The day looks like a Tuesday in June.

The kitchen radio is on at breakfast — it's always on at the Pfeiffer table in the morning now. But the news when she sits down has changed. *Allied forces have landed on the coast of Normandy.* She sets down her coffee cup. The radio keeps talking.

### Scene 2: The day (the radio and the field)
Her father comes in from the barn early. He turns the kitchen radio louder. He sits down at the table with his coat still on and does not get up for two hours. The radio: fragmentary news, the BBC and CBS alternating, the enormity of the thing assembling itself across the morning. Her mother comes in, sets food on the table, sits. The three of them at the table, eating and not eating, while the war comes into the kitchen and fills it.

Outside: the prisoners are in the south field. Hannah can see them from the kitchen window — four figures at work, too far to read. Karl is the one at the northeast corner of the field, she can tell by his size and the way he moves. She does not know what he has been told. Camp operations would have informed the POWs this morning; it would be strange if they hadn't. She does not know what he is feeling.

She goes out to do the afternoon work — she has to; the farm doesn't stop. She passes close to the south field once, on her way to the north barn. Karl is working with his head down. She does not speak to him. It is not the day for it, and she doesn't know what she would say.

### Scene 3: Late afternoon (what the radio has built)
By four o'clock the scope of the landing is clear enough — massive, the largest sea invasion in history, the Allies on French soil. Her father has moved from the kitchen to the parlor where the better radio is, sitting in the chair he normally only uses in winter. He has a quality of stillness Hannah has seen before — the particular stillness of someone absorbing something very large very slowly.

Her mother moves in and out of the room. She has not spoken (she never speaks) but she brings Albrecht coffee without being asked and sets it next to his chair, and he drinks it without comment. This is its own kind of communication.

### Scene 4: Evening (the German sentence)
After supper. The prisoners are back in the icehouse. Lonzo Reed has gone home. The house in its evening quiet — the radio turned lower but still on. Hannah is at the kitchen table with a book she is not reading.

Her father comes in from the parlor. He stands at the kitchen window for a moment, looking out at the icehouse across the yard. He says, in German, to no one in particular — not to Hannah, not to her mother, to the window or to himself: *Nun ist es fertig.* ("Now it is finished.")

He doesn't mean the war is over. He means — Hannah thinks, she is not certain — that something is finished that will lead to the war being over, eventually. The long middle part of it is ending. He says it in German because that is the language in which this particular thing makes sense to him, and he has stopped offering the other one.

He goes to bed.

**Close (elliptical)**: Hannah sits at the table. The radio on low. Karl in the icehouse across the yard. Her father upstairs. She does not know, tonight, what *nun ist es fertig* means for any of them — for Karl, for the farm, for her parents, for herself. The chapter ends on the open air of what she doesn't know: what happens now? What does the end of this particular thing mean for the German speakers in her barn?

---

## Outcome

The chapter accumulates the weight of D-Day through the smallest possible frame: one family, one farm, the radio in the kitchen. Hannah's father's single German sentence at the close — *Nun ist es fertig* — is the emotional close without resolution. The elliptical ending is right: she is left holding the question of what the war's eventual ending means for Karl and the farm.

---

## New Question Raised

If the war is turning toward an end, what happens to Karl and the other prisoners? What happens to the farm's strange ordinary arrangement? What does Hannah's father mean by *fertig*?

## Continuity Notes

- June 6, 1944: D-Day. Allied landings at Normandy. This is a historical date; the news arrives by radio during the morning of June 6.
- The Pfeiffer kitchen radio (wobbly signal) and the parlor radio (better) are both in use.
- Albrecht sits at the radio for hours. This is the most presence he's had in the story — not speaking to Hannah, not speaking to Karl, just absorbing. His German sentence at the end is one of the few spoken lines he has in the whole book.
- *Nun ist es fertig* — "Now it is finished" — is Albrecht's sentence. It is untranslated in the text (Hannah speaks enough German to understand it; the reader who doesn't should be able to infer the general meaning from context).
- Karl is in the south field during the day; Hannah passes near him once and does not speak. They have been having brief German exchanges since April (Ch 15), but today is not the day for it.
- This chapter occurs approximately seven weeks after Marcus's notebook chapter (Ch 16, April-May). Marcus's D-Day chapter is Ch 19.
- Magda's coffee-bringing: her silence is total, but she communicates with Albrecht through small domestic acts.

## Voice Notes

Hannah's register: accumulative and sensory. The chapter should move through the day at day-pace — not rushed, not inflated. The radio is the constant background (always on, always feeding). Hannah's observation of Karl from the kitchen window (too far to read) is a physical fact, not a reflection on their relationship. The father's German sentence at close should be rendered exactly and left unglossed. The ending image: Hannah at the kitchen table, radio low, Karl across the yard in the icehouse — the war in the space between all these things.
