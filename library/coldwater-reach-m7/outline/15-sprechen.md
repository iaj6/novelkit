# Chapter 15 — Sprechen

**One-line summary**: Hannah speaks German to Karl directly, face to face in the barn during three minutes when Lonzo Reed is outside; Karl answers; something shifts that neither of them names.

**POV**: Hannah Pfeiffer
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: The Pfeiffer milking barn; the farm yard; the farmhouse kitchen (evening)
**Time**: April 1944

**Chapter shape**: Multi-scene
**Ending mode**: Resolved beat
**Word target**: ~3,600

---

## Goal / Obstacle

**Goal**: Hannah has been building toward something since December — she does not know what, but April brings a convergence of the spring thaw, the farm work changing, and something that is no longer just curiosity. She wants to speak directly to Karl, in German, about something real.
**Obstacle**: The arrangement — Lonzo Reed present, the farm's surveillance, her father's unspoken claim on the German-language relationship with Karl — does not allow it. It happens anyway.

---

## Scenes

### Scene 1: Before (the spring farm, the arrangement)
April. The mud season: the ice out but the ground waterlogged, the work harder in different ways than winter. The prisoners are on field preparation — draining the low corner, turning the soil. Lonzo Reed is there, but the farm's geography is working against his oversight: the field is large and the four men are spread out. Hannah is doing fencing repairs along the north side, which puts her within a reasonable distance of where Karl is working.

She has Karl's mother's letter still in her notebook. She has carried it since February. She has thought about what Marcus said — *Those who tried to go are gone* — and she has thought about the fence post in December and the ninety seconds of *frost heave*. She has decided something without naming it as a decision. She is going to speak to him in German.

### Scene 2: The barn (the conversation)
It happens on a Wednesday afternoon, not in the field but in the milking barn at the end of the work day. The prisoners come in to move the milk cans before their escort back to the icehouse. Lonzo Reed steps outside briefly — he does this; he knows the layout; he gives himself three minutes of cold air because the barn smells in April in specific ways. The other three prisoners are at the far end. Karl is near the door with the cans.

Hannah is at the stanchions with her back to the main barn entrance. She says, in Bavarian German: "You're from near Rosenheim?"

He stops. He looks at her. He says, in German — not the poor English he's been absorbing from Lonzo but actual German, his German, the German of someone for whom German is the language of thought: "Yes. Near Rosenheim. You speak Bavarian."

She says: "My father's Bavarian." He says: "I know. He told me. We speak sometimes." She says: "I know you do." A pause. He says: "You understand us?" She says: "Some of it."

He picks up the milk can. He says: "It's strange, to hear it here." She says: "I know." Lonzo's boots on the barn door. Karl moves toward the far end with the can. The three minutes are over.

### Scene 3: After (what changed)
She finishes in the barn and goes to the farmhouse. The kitchen: her mother at the stove, working in her silence. Her father's coat on the hook — he's still in the south field. Hannah sits at the table and tries to think about what just happened. She spoke Bavarian German to a German prisoner in her father's barn. He answered. They had a real conversation, in real language, for the first time. The conversation lasted perhaps two minutes and covered approximately forty words.

The forty words: she turns them over. *It's strange, to hear it here. I know.* He said something — she nearly knew all of it — that implied it is strange and also that he is glad. She did not imagine that implication.

### Scene 4: Evening (the recognition)
After supper, her father at the icehouse window. She can hear the German, both of them — her father's, Karl's. The quality of their conversation is different from her two minutes in the barn with Karl; her father's German is the German of a grown man with decades in it, and Karl's German with her father is the German of someone speaking to a man who knows things Karl doesn't. With Hannah, Karl's German was different — more careful, slightly more formal, the German of someone speaking to a stranger who is also from home.

She is not from the home he means. She has never been to Bavaria. She is from Coldwater, Maine. But she was raised on her father's Bavarian and it is as much a part of her as the farm, and Karl knows this now, and she knows Karl knows it.

**Close (resolved beat)**: She goes to bed. She has done the thing. She doesn't know what it means yet — for her, for Karl, for the farm's arrangement, for her parents. But the thing is done. It was two minutes in the barn in April and it was also something she's been moving toward since December, maybe since she found the letter in the hay in February, maybe since the fence post in December, maybe longer. She doesn't know how to locate when it started. She knows it was real and she knows it happened and she goes to sleep.

## Outcome / Resolved Beat

The chapter's central question — will Hannah speak directly to Karl in German? — resolves: she did. The conversation was small and exact and genuine. The resolved beat is earned because the chapter has carried the accumulation of small choices (the fence post, the letter, the German class) through to this moment. Nothing is declared or romanticized. The resolution is not about Karl but about Hannah: she acted from something she doesn't have words for, and it was real, and she knows it.

## New Question Raised

What does the barn conversation change? How will it affect her parents, her sense of her own language, her understanding of what Karl is?

## Continuity Notes

- Hannah speaks Bavarian German (her father's dialect). Karl speaks Bavarian German (from near Rosenheim, same region as Albrecht Pfeiffer's birthplace).
- Karl knows Hannah's father — Albrecht told Karl about his origin, and Karl told Albrecht his. This exchange is established in the characters.md (after Karl names his village, "something shifts").
- Karl's revelation: "We speak sometimes" (about him and Albrecht). Hannah says: "I know." This confirms for the reader that Hannah has been observing the window conversations.
- Hannah's statement: "I know you do" / "I know" — minimal, true, not dramatized.
- Lonzo Reed's brief absence: approximately three minutes. The other three prisoners are at the far end. This is an opportunistic window, not a planned meeting.
- Karl's English from Lonzo Reed vs. his German with Hannah: these are different registers and the scene should make that clear. His German is more articulate than his English.
- This chapter is the longest in the book (~3,600 words) because it is the hinge of Hannah's arc. The extra length goes into the before-and-after, not into the conversation itself, which should remain brief.
- Hannah still has Karl's mother's letter. She does not return it in this chapter. This remains unresolved.
- April 1944: historically accurate farm context (mud season, field preparation, spring thaw).

## Voice Notes

Hannah's register: close to the body, accumulative. The barn conversation itself should be rendered in exactly its length — approximately two minutes, perhaps forty words — without being inflated. The weight is in the before (what she's been carrying) and the after (the recognition at night). German words in Hannah's narration: Bavarian terms where natural. The close must not analyze what the scene meant; it must render what Hannah does (she goes to sleep) and let the accumulation speak.
