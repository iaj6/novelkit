# Chapter 21 — The Second October

**One-line summary**: One year since the body; Hannah is eighteen; Karl is twenty; the farm's strange ordinariness has deepened; her father's face when he sees Hannah and Karl speaking German at the fence.

**POV**: Hannah Pfeiffer
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: The Pfeiffer farm
**Time**: October 1944

**Chapter shape**: Multi-scene
**Ending mode**: Elliptical
**Word target**: ~2,800

---

## Goal / Obstacle

**Goal**: No particular goal — a chapter of accumulation. Hannah is eighteen in October 1944, which is a fact with no ceremony attached to it. The farm continues. The arrangement with Karl has settled into something she doesn't have a word for.
**Obstacle**: Her father's awareness of what is happening at the fence, which he has not commented on and which Hannah cannot read.

---

## Scenes

### Scene 1: October again (the farm)
A year since the body. Hannah notes this in her own mind on October 12 — the date is not publicly marked, no one in town mentions it to her, but she has been aware of the date approaching since September. She walks past the harbor on her Tuesday route to school; the breakwater is there. She looks at it for the usual three seconds and continues.

The farm in October 1944: the harvest mostly done, the cows in the barn more now, the icehouse's inhabitants starting their third autumn there. Karl and the three others have been on the Pfeiffer farm for eighteen months, which is longer than Hannah's parents were in Bavaria when they emigrated. The comparison occurs to her and she doesn't know what to do with it.

She is eighteen. The birthday passed in whatever way birthdays pass in this house — her mother made a cake without speaking; her father said *Herzlichen Glückwunsch*, wished her happy birthday in German, and when she answered in German he looked at her in a way that contained something she couldn't quite read.

### Scene 2: Karl at twenty
Karl is twenty now (birthday sometime in the summer; she doesn't know the exact date; she didn't ask). He has been on this farm eighteen months and speaks serviceable English now — serviceable enough for Lonzo Reed, which is a Maine fisherman's vocabulary; he also speaks a full German to Hannah that has become its own kind of ordinary.

Their exchanges since April: brief, in German, in the barn or at the fence when Lonzo Reed is elsewhere. They have not had another three-minute conversation like the one in April. What they have is something more distributed: a sentence here, a word there, a question about the work. *How many cows? Sixteen. More in winter? No — same, always the same sixteen.* The German is real and ordinary, not charged. This is both less and more than what she expected, if she had known to expect anything.

She has thought about what Karl is, to her. Not in any language she can use yet. She has decided she doesn't need to yet.

### Scene 3: The fence, her father watching
An October Saturday. Hannah at the fence near the north pasture; Karl's work detail on the other side — repairing the stone wall that runs along the upper field, one of the autumn maintenance jobs. She is doing her own work. They exchange a sentence or two in German — something about the wall's mortar, whether it will hold winter. Nothing more.

She looks up and her father is at the field's edge. He is not close enough to have heard — or maybe he is. He is watching. His face has the quality she has been trying to read for two years: not anger, not approval, something more complicated than either, something that belongs to a man who left one world and built another and is watching his daughter do something he recognizes from his first world in his second one.

He doesn't say anything. He goes back to whatever he was doing. She finishes with the fence and goes in.

**Close (elliptical)**: That evening Albrecht is at the icehouse window with Karl, as usual. Hannah sits in the kitchen, as usual, and hears the German through the wall, as usual. The year of October 1944 is the same as the year of October 1943 in its arrangements — same cows, same fence, same window — and it is also entirely different, because she is different, and she knows it, and she doesn't know what it means that her father watched her and Karl at the fence and said nothing. She goes to bed without knowing. The chapter ends on this open not-knowing: what does her father see when he sees them?

## Outcome

A marking chapter: one year in, Hannah is eighteen, Karl is twenty, and what has accumulated is real without being nameable. The chapter's observational quality (no dramatic event; a year's sediment settling) is intentional and correct. The elliptical close is in her father's unreadable face.

What does Albrecht understand about what is happening between Hannah and Karl? Will he speak to her about it? What is the thing he is weighing?
