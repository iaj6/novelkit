# Chapter 09 — Across the Fence

**One-line summary**: Karl speaks to Hannah directly for the first time, across the paddock fence; she replies; contact is made; a small fact is established in the world.

**POV**: Hannah Pfeiffer
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: The Pfeiffer farm — paddock fence, barn yard, farmhouse
**Time**: December 1943

**Chapter shape**: Multi-scene
**Ending mode**: Resolved beat
**Word target**: ~2,800

---

## Goal / Obstacle

**Goal**: Hannah is not pursuing Karl — she is doing the farm work. But she has been watching, accumulating, and something in December shifts the arrangement from observation to contact.
**Obstacle**: The distance is enforced (physically, socially, legally). Lonzo Reed is present. Her father has established a different kind of contact with Karl (through the window, in German) that Hannah has watched but not entered.

---

## Scenes

### Scene 1: Early December (the daily routine)
Winter settling in. The farm in December: the ground frozen, the mud replaced by something worse, the cows restless in the cold. The prisoners' work has shifted with the season — less field work, more barn maintenance, wood-splitting, repairs. Lonzo Reed wears two coats now and has taken to standing next to the barn wall to block the wind. Hannah observes all of this as background, the way she observes everything on the farm — with the accuracy of someone who grew up reading a complicated environment.

She notes: Karl has been picking up English words. Not from instruction but from absorption — from Lonzo's vocabulary, which is the vocabulary of a former fisherman from Maine, so the inheritance is mixed. When she overhears Karl say something in English to one of the other prisoners, it sounds like Lonzo filtered through a Bavarian accent.

### Scene 2: The fence (the contact)
Wednesday morning. Hannah is repairing a section of paddock fence — one post has shifted in the frost heave; she has the post-driver and the staple gun. Karl's work detail is splitting wood twenty yards off. Lonzo has gone to the barn for something; this happens occasionally; he is not reckless but he is sixty years old and the barn is closer to the woodstove.

Karl walks over to the fence. He has a piece of split wood in each hand, as if he came over for a reason. He says, in English: "The post, it is—" He makes a gesture, a tilting motion. She says: "Frost heave. Happens every December." He considers this. He says: "Frost heave." Repeating it like a tool he's picking up. She says: "You drive the post down, tamp it, it'll hold till spring." He nods. He sets one of the wood pieces against the fence post without being asked and braces it while she drives the staples. Lonzo returns from the barn. Karl goes back to the woodpile without hurrying.

That is all. The conversation is perhaps ninety seconds. When Lonzo Reed stamps past, he doesn't seem to have noticed anything particular.

### Scene 3: The evening (the window)
That evening Hannah is doing homework at the kitchen table when her father goes to the window. His low German to Karl, Karl's lower German back. Hannah listens, not to the words specifically, but to the quality of her father's voice — the particular register he uses with Karl, which is not the register he uses with cows and is not the register she last remembers him using with her mother in German, before the war made all their Germans different from each other.

She has been thinking about the ninety seconds at the fence. She doesn't know what she's thinking about it; it's not romantic exactly, or not only that. Karl held the fence post. That's all.

**Close (resolved beat)**: She closes her algebra textbook. She has been in the same town as Karl Reinmar for eight months. He has said, in total, perhaps forty words to her, thirty-two of which were today. The fence post will hold until spring. She has resolved to notice what she notices, without deciding in advance what it means. This is not a dramatic decision. It is the kind of decision that doesn't feel like one. She goes to bed.

## Outcome / Resolved Beat

The chapter's central question — will they speak? — has been answered: they have. The contact is made. It is minimal (ninety seconds, a fence post, a piece of wood), which is exactly the weight it should carry. Nothing is declared or decided. A fact exists in the world that didn't exist before. The resolved beat is genuine and small.
