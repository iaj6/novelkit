# Chapter 29 — Spring Log

**One-line summary**: Late April; the harbor in spring; Eira writes the day's conditions in the log and then, at the end, one more line that is not about tide or weather.

**POV**: Eira Bowman
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: The Coldwater Reach harbor and harbormaster's shack
**Time**: Late April 1945

**Chapter shape**: Multi-scene (one long spring day)
**Ending mode**: Resolved beat
**Word target**: ~2,600

---

## Goal / Obstacle

**Goal**: The harbor in late April — the fishing season in full operation, the boats out, the spring the harbor needs her to be present for. The war in Europe almost over.
**Obstacle**: Not a dramatic obstacle. This is a chapter of arrival — the season, Eira's arc, the harbor in spring. The obstacle, if there is one, is the habit of carrying something for so long that you have to learn to set it down.

---

## Scenes

### Scene 1: The harbor in late April 1945
Late April. The cove open, the lobster boats active, the two draggers running. The dock smells the way the dock smells in spring: diesel and herring bait and cold salt starting to warm. Eira runs her morning routine — tide clock (set Monday), radio check, the log, the boats. Seven lobster boats out. The two draggers.

The news from Europe: the war clearly in its final days. The radio this morning: Soviet forces in Berlin. The harbor radio crackles with it between the regular marine traffic. She logs the marine traffic. The European news is in the air the way it has been in the air for years — the reason the rationing is thin, the reason the men are gone, the reason the dock lights were kept dim in 1942. It is almost over. She does not know what almost over will feel like when it arrives.

### Scene 2: The day's work
A full spring day: three freight manifests, a mooring dispute between Reg Farber and the Pelletier brothers (adjacent berths, a dock-line collision), the Tuesday mail boat with a shipment for the general store. She resolves the Farber-Pelletier dispute with the specific authority she has spent nine years developing: she knows both boats, she knows both men, she assigns Farber the inside berth and the Pelletiers the outside and writes it in the dock register as a seasonal assignment. Neither man argues. This is authority exercised correctly.

The mail boat: no yellow envelopes on the Tuesday boat. She notes this without marking it specially.

### Scene 3: The afternoon — Owen's handwriting
Late afternoon. The shack is warm in the April light; the window that faces the harbor lets in the low sun. She has a few minutes before the evening radio check. She opens the log. She turns, without planning to, to the November 1935 pages — Owen's handwriting, the last entry that stops mid-sentence.

She reads it. *November 12, 1935. Weather deteriorating, wind NNW increasing, barometer 29.1 and falling. Putting out additional mooring lines on —*

She has read this many times. She reads it now with the particular quality of someone who is almost done carrying something and knows it. Owen's handwriting is the harbor's record until a certain date and hers is the harbor's record after it. The log goes back to June 1891. It will go forward after her.

She closes the November 1935 page. She opens to today.

### Scene 4: The log (the last line)
She writes the day: April 25, 1945. Weather: clear, temperature 52°F, wind SE at six knots, barometer 30.2 and rising. Tide: high water 9:14 a.m., 5'2". Traffic: seven lobster boats out, both draggers active, mail boat *Heron* Tuesday arrival, three freight manifests completed. Dock assignment: seasonal berth resolution, Farber–Pelletier, registered. Mail boat: no notifications.

Then she sets the pen down. She picks it up again. She writes — in the same compressed, exact handwriting, as if this is part of the day's operational record:

*Harbor in order. Nine years, two months. All boats accounted for.*

She does not date this separately; it is part of the April 25 entry. She closes the log.

**Close (resolved beat)**: The tide clock on the wall: she checks it. Four minutes fast; she resets it. The radio check — NNW channel, the marine traffic normal, one lobster boat checking in from the outer bar, she responds and logs it. The harbor is there outside the window: the boats, the breakwater with its red fixed light just coming on in the early dusk. The man she saw in October 1943 is in the supplementary record now, and she cannot give him his name, and the harbor is in order. She is the harbormaster. This is the post she holds. She locks up the shack and walks home.

## Outcome / Resolved Beat

Eira's final chapter: her arc resolves in the log entry. "Nine years, two months. All boats accounted for." This is not triumph or closure but accuracy — the exact accounting of what she has done and what she has held, stated in the only language that has ever been fully available to her. The tide clock reset, the radio check done, the harbor in order: Eira goes home.

## Continuity Notes

- Late April 1945: historically, the war in Europe is in its final days (Germany surrendered May 8, 1945 — VE Day, which is after this chapter and the final chapter are set).
- Soviet forces in Berlin: historically, the Battle of Berlin began April 16, 1945; the city fell approximately May 2. Late April 1945 is accurate for the description "Soviet forces in Berlin."
- Eira has been harbormaster since November 1935 — nine years and approximately five to six months as of late April 1945. "Nine years, two months" is approximately accurate from November 1935 to late April 1945 (9 years, 5 months) — the drafter should use the accurate figure: *nine years and some months* or *nine years, five months* as appropriate.
- The log entry "Nine years, two months" — adjust to accurate elapsed time. From November 1935 to April 1945 is approximately nine years and five months. The drafter should verify and use the correct number.
- Owen's last entry: quoted as in Ch 07 — *November 12, 1935. Weather deteriorating, wind NNW increasing, barometer 29.1 and falling. Putting out additional mooring lines on —* The drafter must reproduce this exactly (it is a registered canonical record from Ch 07's continuity notes).
- "All boats accounted for": this phrase appears in Ch 17 (the ice-out chapter) in a log entry and here in the close. The echo is intentional.
- No yellow envelopes on the Tuesday mail: this notation echoes Ch 20 (where Eira notes *Hatch, E.J. — no telegram this Tuesday. The harbor was full*). The practice of private log annotations continues.
- The tide clock: set Mondays, loses four minutes a week (established Ch 01). On late April Tuesday, approximately six days since last set, it would be approximately four minutes fast. The drafter should use this math.

## Voice Notes

The log entry at the close is the chapter's emotional center; it should be written in her exact compressed register.
