# Chapter 16 — The Notebook

**One-line summary**: Marcus writes in his German notebook, assembling what he knows about the body and the Feld letter; the question doesn't answer itself; he closes the notebook without resolution.

**POV**: Dr. Marcus Klein
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: Marcus's room; the Coldwater school (briefly)
**Time**: April–May 1944

**Chapter shape**: Interiority-heavy
**Ending mode**: Elliptical
**Word target**: ~2,800

---

## Goal / Obstacle

**Goal**: Marcus is trying to think his way through what he has assembled — the Feld letter, Hannah's letter, the body on the breakwater — without arriving at a conclusion he hasn't earned.
**Obstacle**: The material doesn't cohere into a solvable problem. He is an eighteenth-century philologist, not an investigator. The skills are related but not identical.

---

## Scenes / Movement

### Scene 1: Late April at the school
The school year approaching its end. His three German students will take no exam — what examination would they take? He has been teaching from primary texts: nineteenth-century letters, simple prose, some Goethe. Hannah Pfeiffer's German has improved over the year in a specific way: she writes more of it, and writes it in the dialect she grew up hearing, which is not what the textbook teaches. He has not corrected this.

After the final class session of the week, he stays in the empty classroom and opens his notebook. He has been avoiding this particular assembly for weeks. He doesn't know why. He is not afraid of what he'll find; he is afraid of what he'll find probable without being able to make it certain.

### Scene 2: The notebook (the assembly)
He writes in German. The chapter renders what he writes in translation — not direct quotation of his notebook but the reader's access to his thinking.

He writes down, in order:
- October 12, 1943: unknown man found at Coldwater breakwater. No papers. No identification. Not from Coldwater. Clothing civilian. Possibly middle-aged.
- December 1943: Feld's letter. Felix K., linguist, formerly Leipzig. Last documented Yarmouth, NS, September 22, 1943. Intended to reach Massachusetts by sea. No word since.
- February 1944: Hannah's letter (from the Pfeiffer barn). *Die die versucht haben zu gehen, sind weg.* The phrase is Bavarian; it refers to people from the mother's community who tried to emigrate before the war closed.
- The calculation: Yarmouth to Maine by motorboat in autumn. Approximately thirteen days at a careful pace. September 28 departure: arrival possibilities along the Maine coast begin in mid-October.

He draws no conclusion in the notebook. He writes: *This is probability, not evidence. One does not publish on probability.* Then, below: *One also does not ignore a probability this high.*

He thinks about Felix K. He tries to remember the 1936 Vienna conference and cannot resolve the half-memory into a face or a full name. He tries to remember what a linguist from Leipzig in the 1930s would have looked like. He cannot.

### Scene 3: The difficulty of this particular uncertainty
Marcus sits with what the notebook contains. He is, by training, someone who lives in archives — primary documents, verifiable chains of evidence, the specific and the recoverable. What he has is not that. What he has is a cluster of probabilities that point to a man who may or may not be Felix K. and who is certainly dead at the end of a breakwater in Coldwater, Maine, and whose name Eira Bowman's records do not contain.

He knows Eira saw something. He doesn't know what. He knows the official record is closed.

He considers going to her with what he knows. He decides not to: he has a theory, not evidence, and the difference matters. If he's wrong he has introduced a painful false story into a woman who is already carrying something. If he's right — if the man is Felix K. — it changes nothing recoverable about what happened on that dock.

He writes: *What do I intend to do with this?* He leaves the question in the notebook unanswered.

### Scene 4: Evening (the shortwave, closing)
Back at the Crow house. Henry at the kitchen table, the same paper he was reading last week, or one that looks like it. Lillian with coffee. Marcus sits with them for twenty minutes, saying nothing much. Henry says: "You look like you've been working on something." Marcus says: "Trying to." Henry nods. He does not ask what.

Marcus goes upstairs. He does not open the shortwave tonight. He sits in the chair by the window and looks at the dark outside — May in Maine, the light coming back, the evenings extending. He thinks about Yarmouth and Boston and the October coast and a man with a shuttered lantern who tied a boat at a dock and walked toward a breakwater. He thinks about *die die versucht haben zu gehen, sind weg.* He thinks about himself, in 1941, emerging from Sherbrooke.

**Close (elliptical)**: He does not write anything more. The notebook is closed on his desk. The question he left in it — *What do I intend to do with this?* — does not answer itself tonight. He goes to bed. He does not know what he intends to do with this.

## Outcome

Marcus has assembled his theory in writing. He has decided not to act on it yet — not because he is cowardly but because he is precise, and the precision requires more before he can justify action. The chapter ends with the question genuinely open: he has assembled the probable picture but has not yet done anything with it.

## New Question Raised

What will push Marcus to take the theory forward? What more could he possibly find — and when?

## Voice Notes

Marcus's chapter, interiority-heavy. His voice in the notebook should feel like a different register from his prose narration — more direct, less hedged, the private academic's shorthand. The notebook entries should be brief and exact, not expansive. The elliptical close is Marcus's mode: the question posed, the question unanswered, the notebook closed. The prose should not tell the reader what he's feeling; it should tell the reader what he's looking at (the dark outside the window, the notebook on the desk) and trust the reader to feel the rest.
