# Chapter 11 — January Harbor

**One-line summary**: The inner cove ices over; Reg Farber mentions conversationally that he saw a boat without lights on the night of October 11; Eira says nothing, and does not know what to do with the fact that she is not alone in having seen it.

**POV**: Eira Bowman
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: The Coldwater Reach harbor; the harbormaster's shack; Merrill's diner
**Time**: January 1944

**Chapter shape**: Multi-scene
**Ending mode**: Elliptical
**Word target**: ~2,600

---

## Goal / Obstacle

**Goal**: Manage the harbor in January — reduced traffic, the inner cove icing, maintenance work, the BC-1206 radio checks.
**Obstacle**: The frozen harbor is its own problem. And then Reg Farber says something at Merrill's that opens a new dimension in what Eira has been carrying.

---

## Scenes

### Scene 1: The cove ices over
By late January the inner cove has iced over — not hard enough to walk on, not open enough for boats. The two draggers are tied up until the thaw; the lobster boats are pulled. The harbor's deep-winter quiet: fewer reasons to be on the dock, longer log entries about conditions, the radio checks reduced to once a day. Eira does her rounds with the particular efficiency of someone with too little to do and too much to think about.

She re-reads the October entries in the log. The entries before October 12: weather, tides, traffic. The entry for October 12: the body, the Coast Guard. The pages that were removed (Ennis took them) leave a faint gap in the binding, as if the ledger knows something is missing.

### Scene 2: Merrill's (Reg Farber speaks)
On a Wednesday afternoon Eira is at Merrill's for coffee — one of the few places in January that is warm and open. Reg Farber comes in and sits at the counter. He is the man whose boy found the body in October; she knows him by his boats as much as by his face.

He mentions the body without preamble — the way people in small towns revisit things when there's nothing else to revisit. He says he's thought about it since October, about who the man could have been. Then, incidentally, as if remembering something that has been in a drawer: "I was up late on the eleventh. Saw a boat come in around midnight — or between eleven and midnight. Small wooden thing, no lights. Tied up at the dock's far end. Gone by morning." He shakes his head. "Never thought much of it until I remembered afterward."

He is not accusing anyone. He is not even sure it's related. He doesn't connect the boat to the body with any certainty. He mentions it as the small-town man's duty to offer the information now that he's had it for three months.

Eira says: "Did you report it to the Coast Guard?" He says: "No — didn't think to. Should I?" She says: "I'll mention it if it's relevant." He says: "Probably isn't." He orders pie.

### Scene 3: After (the long walk home)
Eira walks home from Merrill's in the cold. January: the road frozen, the dark coming at four. What Reg Farber doesn't know: that she saw the same boat. That there is now a second witness to the night boat, and this second witness has offered the information voluntarily and publicly, and she has said she will mention it if it's relevant and she knows she will not.

She walks. She does not run the scenario forward — what would happen if she told the Coast Guard everything, if she filed an amended statement. She has run this scenario and arrived at its conclusions and they are not conclusions she accepts.

What is different now is this: she is no longer the only person who knows the boat was there. She is the person who knows the most about it. These are not the same as being the only person who knows.

**Close (elliptical)**: She goes to bed. The barometer rising; clear weather coming. She does not write anything in the log. The fact of Reg Farber's information sits in her without a place to go. She does not know what to do with it. She does not do anything.

---

## Outcome

The chapter deepens the mystery by confirming the night boat's existence through a second, independent source. Eira is now aware that she is not the only witness, but she is the witness who knows the most — she saw the man, not just the boat — and she continues to say nothing. The elliptical close is appropriate: she has new information and no action.

## New Question Raised

What happens when a second witness's knowledge and Eira's knowledge are both in the town, accumulating, without anyone connecting them?

## Continuity Notes

- The inner cove ices over by late January 1944 (world.md).
- Reg Farber: lobsterman, his boy found the body (Ch 01). He saw the night boat independently, around 11 p.m.–midnight on October 11.
- Farber's account of the boat: small wooden thing, no lights, tied at the dock's far end, gone by morning. This is consistent with what Eira saw (same boat, presumably).
- Eira does not tell the Coast Guard about Farber's sighting. She says she'll mention it "if relevant." This is the record of what she said, not what she intends.
- The pages removed from the log by Lt. Ennis leave a gap in the binding — a physical detail.
- This chapter's function in the mystery arc: Farber's sighting is independent confirmation of the boat's existence. In Ch 26 (the addendum), Eira will formally report what she saw. Farber's account is never formally reported, but Eira carries the knowledge that it exists.

## Voice Notes

Eira's chapter: spare, procedural. The Merrill's scene should be rendered with the casualness of small-town conversation — Farber is not dramatic, he's incidental. Eira's response should be exact and non-committal; the reader should feel the weight of what she's doing without the prose naming it. The walk home: her interiority comes through her relation to the physical world (the frozen road, the barometer, the dark at four), not through named reflection.
