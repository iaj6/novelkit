# Chapter 20 — The Mail Boat

**One-line summary**: Summer in the harbor; casualty telegrams arrive on Tuesday mail; Eira and Marcus exchange a few words at the dock; she writes in the log that evening something that is also something else.

**POV**: Eira Bowman
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: The Coldwater Reach harbor and dock; the harbormaster's shack
**Time**: July–August 1944

**Chapter shape**: Multi-scene
**Ending mode**: Resolved beat
**Word target**: ~2,800

---

## Goal / Obstacle

**Goal**: Manage the summer harbor — peak season for what passes as peak season in a wartime town, the lobster boats active, the two draggers out, the mail boat twice weekly.
**Obstacle**: The summer of 1944 comes with a specific anxiety: the Normandy campaign continuing, the casualty lists. The mail boat brings things Eira cannot prepare for.

---

## Scenes

### Scene 1: The summer harbor
July into August 1944. The harbor at its fullest: seven lobster boats, the two draggers, the occasional pleasure craft up from Portland. Eira's summer is her busiest season in terms of paperwork — dock fees, mooring assignments, freight manifests, the radio checks. She is competent at this at a level beyond competence: she knows which boats will need which berths as the season shifts, which lobstermen run long and which come in early, the pattern of the harbor as a living system.

She has been doing this for nearly nine years. She notes, without dwelling on it, that she knows this harbor better than Owen did when he died. He had been doing it for six years when the November storm took him. She has had three years more. The harbor she runs is partly his and partly not his anymore — it is hers in the ways that matter.

### Scene 2: The casualty telegrams
The mail boat *Heron* arrives Tuesdays and Fridays. In the summer of 1944, with France continuing and the Pacific war still on, the Tuesday boat brings, occasionally, War Department telegrams addressed to town families. The Western Union messenger comes from Rockland on the same boat or the following day.

Eira is not the one who delivers these — that's the Western Union man and then the postmistress and then whoever is closest. But she is at the dock when the *Heron* comes in and she sees the yellow envelopes sorted from the regular mail, and she knows, and the town knows by afternoon.

July: the Hatch boy's telegram. Doris Hatch's older brother. The word is around town by suppertime. The next Tuesday there are no yellow envelopes and everyone notices.

### Scene 3: Marcus at the dock (the exchange)
A Tuesday in August. Marcus is at the dock for a book package from the *Heron*. Eira is running the freight manifest. She sees him before he sees her — his careful coat, wrong for August, which is less wrong in Maine August than in November but still slightly wrong. He signs for his package. He's turning to leave when she says, not loudly: "Dr. Klein." He turns. She says: "No yellow envelopes today." He looks at her. He says: "Good." She says: "So far." She goes back to the manifest.

That's all. Three exchanges. But there is something between them now that wasn't there in November: a shorthand. They know a few things about each other — not the personal history, not the private details, but the quality of precision each one brings to the things they handle. This is, for Eira, something close to trust. She does not name it trust.

### Scene 4: The log, evening
She writes in the log at the end of the day. It is a hot August Tuesday. The entries: weather (hot, clear, wind SW at eight knots), tide, the boats' status, the freight manifest. Then, at the end, after the operational entries, she writes one more line. It is not operational:

*Hatch, E. J. — no telegram this Tuesday. The harbor was full.*

This is not a form of entry the harbor log requires. It is Eira's own form, which she has been developing slowly since October — the annotations that say what the official record doesn't have a place for. She has done this twice before (both times after a Tuesday without yellow envelopes). She does not mark these entries differently from the operational ones.

**Close (resolved beat)**: She sets the pen down. The summer harbor outside the window, still light at seven p.m. She is the harbormaster. The harbor is accounted for. This has resolved, tonight, into something that feels like itself — not the thing she inherited from Owen's death, not the post she was given because no one else could run it, but the harbor that belongs to the work she has done for nine years. A small thing, quiet and permanent. She closes the log.

## Outcome / Resolved Beat

The chapter resolves two things: the summer harbor is managed with competence (the concrete arc of Eira's authority); and the relationship with Marcus has shifted to something that carries the weight of two conversations and a shorthand. The log entry at the close — *The harbor was full* — is the chapter's genuine resolved beat. Not dramatic, not triumphant. Exactly right.

## New Question Raised

What is Eira's private log becoming? The annotations that don't belong to the official record — are they her own record against the day she might need one?

## Voice Notes

Eira's chapter: the harbor is workplace, not metaphor. The casualty-telegram scene should be handled with restraint — she sees the yellow envelopes, she knows what they mean, she is not dramatizing her own reaction to them. The exchange with Marcus: exact, brief, no subtext narrated. The log entry close: operational in form, extraordinary in implication — the drafter should trust this and not add commentary.
