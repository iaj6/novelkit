# Chapter 13 — What the Letter Said

**One-line summary**: Hannah brings the letter to Marcus after German class, asking about one word; he reads more than she intends; what he tells her is true but not everything he hears in it.

**POV**: Dr. Marcus Klein
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: The German classroom, Coldwater school (second floor, east end); the school hallway
**Time**: February–March 1944

**Chapter shape**: Dialogue-led
**Ending mode**: Resolved beat
**Word target**: ~3,000

---

## Goal / Obstacle

**Goal**: Marcus wants to finish the school day and get home. What he gets instead is Hannah's question, which is small and opens something large.
**Obstacle**: Hannah gives him less information than the letter contains. He must decide how much of what he hears to give back.

---

## Action Beats

1. **After class**
   Doris and Louise have gone. Hannah stays — she has stayed before, briefly, to ask grammar questions. She takes a folded page from her notebook and sets it on the desk. She says she found something in German she doesn't entirely understand. Could he look at the underlined phrase?

2. **He takes the letter**
   He picks up the page. The handwriting: a woman's hand, Bavarian dialect, slightly formal. He reads it in full, as he would read anything in full — the letters on either side of the underlined phrase matter for what the phrase means. A domestic letter: the fence, the potatoes, the cousin's wedding. Then: *Die die versucht haben zu gehen, sind weg. Wir beten für sie.*

   The underlined phrase: *die versucht haben zu gehen*. Hannah says: "I know *versucht* and *gehen* — tried and go — but I don't understand the whole phrase."

3. **What he tells her**
   He translates: "Those who tried to go are gone. We pray for them." He says: it's a reference to people who left — emigrated, he thinks, or tried to. It's written in the past tense. It implies they did not all arrive.

   Hannah says: "People leaving Germany?"

   He says: yes. Before the war made it impossible, and then after the war made it dangerous. Some people who tried to leave Germany or Austria or Czechoslovakia in 1939, 1940, '41 — they're gone. Some of them are in places like this. Some of them didn't arrive anywhere.

   He does not say: I am one of them. He does not say: I know what *versucht haben zu gehen* sounds like from the inside, which is different from what it sounds like translated.

4. **What he hears that he doesn't give back**
   He looks at the whole letter again. The name and address are not there — Karl has torn or cut the header from this page; what Marcus has is just the single page. The writing is autumn 1943 or late 1943 — the references to the cousin's October wedding and the onset of winter. *Those who tried to go* — in late 1943, this is a reference to people who tried to leave in 1942, '41, '40. After Kristallnacht and the Anschluss, there were years of attempts. This mother is writing to a son she knows is alive in a POW camp in America, and she is telling him that the people from their community who tried to go are gone.

   And: Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, September 1943. A man trying to reach Boston by sea. The coast of Maine.

   He has not yet allowed himself to connect these two things. He is close to it. The letter is not evidence; it is the echo of a world in which this connection is possible. He folds the letter and hands it back to Hannah.

5. **The exchange shifts**
   Hannah asks: "Were you one of them? The ones who tried to go?" He says: "I am one of the ones who arrived." A pause. Then: "The leaving is not the hard part." He does not say what the hard part is.

   She says: "Where did you find — where did you go, before here?" He says: "Vienna, then Paris, London, Canada, here." She says: "Canada?" He says: "Briefly." She does not ask about briefly. She has lived in a family where you learn which words not to follow. She puts the letter back in her notebook.

6. **Close (resolved beat)**
   Hannah leaves. Marcus sits in the empty classroom for a while. The coal furnace making its inadequate sounds. He thinks about the phrase *die die versucht haben zu gehen* and what it carries — the weight of that construction, the Bavarian inflection, the mother who writes it at a table in a village fifty kilometers from where a girl in his German class grew up speaking her father's language. The world is a specific size. He has known this for six years.

   He picks up his papers and goes. The chapter resolves in the particular way that a scene between two people who have learned to receive each other's partial information can resolve: the thing was offered, the thing was received, the transaction is complete. He has the letter's image in his memory. She has his answer. Both of them are carrying more than they gave.

---

## Outcome / Resolved Beat

The scene's central tension resolves: Hannah has asked her question, received her answer, and left. Marcus has read the letter and given back a true but partial reading. Their relationship has shifted — she is now someone who brings him things; he is now someone who keeps what she gives him. The beat settles, even though it opens new questions.

## Voice Notes

Marcus's chapter, dialogue-led. The scene's texture comes from what is not said: what he hears in the letter beyond what he translates, what Hannah hears in his answer beyond what he says, what both of them know about the etiquette of receiving partial information. The dialogue should be spare — both speakers measure their sentences. Marcus's prose shortens when something moves him (style.md): "The leaving is not the hard part" should arrive briefly and without elaboration. The resolved beat at the close is real — the transaction is complete — but the chapter's final paragraph should carry the weight of what he's still holding.
