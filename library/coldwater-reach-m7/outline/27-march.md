# Chapter 27 — March

**One-line summary**: Karl tells Hannah through the fence that repatriation will begin in phases; she asks if he is afraid to go back; his answer is not a resolution; what she's been holding for eighteen months is now finite.

**POV**: Hannah Pfeiffer
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: The Pfeiffer farm — fence, barn, farmhouse
**Time**: March 1945

**Chapter shape**: Multi-scene
**Ending mode**: Cliffhanger
**Word target**: ~2,400

---

## Goal / Obstacle

**Goal**: No fixed goal — March on the farm, mud season returning, the war in Europe clearly in its final weeks. Hannah is trying to manage what she knows is coming.
**Obstacle**: The news about repatriation has arrived and it is real and it means Karl leaves, and she has not yet decided what she feels about this, which means she is accumulating it without a frame.

---

## Scenes

### Scene 1: March on the farm
Mud season: the ground thawing, the barn work harder in different ways again, the mud season rhythm that Hannah has known all her life. The war in Europe: in March 1945 the Allies are crossing the Rhine. The end is near in ways the radio makes plain. Her father listens, less stunned now, the quality of his radio sitting more like witness than like grief.

The camp administration has circulated information to the prisoner details about the repatriation process: when Germany surrenders, the prisoners will be staged at the primary facility (Camp Houlton) for processing, then shipped back to Germany in phases. This could be weeks away. The farm's arrangement — this strange ordinary thing that has been running since April 1943 — has an end date approaching that no one can specify but everyone can feel.

### Scene 2: Karl at the fence (the conversation)
A Saturday in mid-March. Hannah is repairing a section of the same north-pasture fence she was working when Karl first walked over in December 1943. The fence post has heaved again. Lonzo Reed is somewhere else — he tends to disappear on Saturdays, his most lax day.

Karl comes to the fence. He says, in German: "You know we're leaving." Not a question. She says: "I heard." He says: "It could be a month. It could be two." She says: "When Germany surrenders." He says: "Yes." A pause. She says: *Hast du Angst zurückzugehen?* (Are you afraid to go back?) He looks at her. He is quiet for a long time.

He says: "I don't know what I'm going back to." She says: "You know what you're going back to." He says: "A village and a farm and my mother. Yes." He considers this. "But not what the village is now. Not what the country is now. That — " He stops. "I was twenty-two months ago. I'm twenty now. I don't know what that means for home."

She nods. She says: "We're still here." He says: "You are." He picks up the piece of fencing wire she put down and holds it against the post while she drives the staple. The same motion as December 1943, the fence post, the frost heave. She does not point this out. He may notice it; she doesn't know.

Lonzo Reed comes back. Karl returns to the work detail.

### Scene 3: What she's been holding
That night Hannah goes to her room and gets Karl's mother's letter from inside the school notebook where she has kept it since February 1944 — thirteen months. She unfolds it. The creases are worn now. The phrase: *die die versucht haben zu gehen, sind weg. Wir beten für sie.*

She has been holding this letter for thirteen months. She took it by accident and then kept it by choice. She has shown it to one person (Marcus) and not told anyone else she has it. She has not returned it to Karl.

She is going to return it before he leaves. She has decided this only now, holding it.

She doesn't know when. She knows she will.

**Close (cliffhanger)**: She puts the letter back in the notebook. Outside: the March farm, the icehouse across the yard, the lights out in there already. Karl's last weeks here are finite. She has something that belongs to him. She is going to give it back. She doesn't know what she'll say when she does. The chapter ends here, in the pressure of what she hasn't done yet and knows she will do.

---

## Outcome

The chapter's cliffhanger: Hannah has committed to returning Karl's mother's letter, which she has held for thirteen months. This is unfinished business with a deadline that's approaching. The chapter also deepens Karl's interiority briefly — he doesn't know what he's going back to; the country he left is not the country he'll return to. His fear (unnamed) is real and unresolved, which is the honest ending for this conversation.

---

## New Question Raised

When will Hannah return the letter to Karl? What will she say when she does? And what does Karl mean when he says "you are" in response to "we're still here"?

## Continuity Notes

- March 1945: the Allies are crossing the Rhine (historically, American forces crossed at Remagen on March 7, 1945). Germany's defeat is clearly weeks away.
- Camp Houlton staging: German POWs at satellite work camps like the Pfeiffer detail will be moved to Camp Houlton for processing before repatriation. The timing is uncertain but imminent.
- Karl's letter page: Hannah has kept it in her school notebook since February 1944 (Ch 12). This is thirteen months. She returns it to Karl before he leaves — not in this chapter but in the final chapter (Ch 30, implied, or in the time between Ch 27 and Ch 30).
- *Hast du Angst zurückzugehen?* — "Are you afraid to go back?" — untranslated, available from context.
- The fence post and the staple: Karl holding the post while Hannah drives the staple echoes December 1943 (Ch 09). The drafter may render this as Hannah noticing the echo; Karl's awareness of it is deliberately ambiguous.
- Karl's English is now genuinely serviceable (eighteen months of Lonzo Reed vocabulary); the conversation in this chapter is in German, which remains their primary shared language.
- *Die die versucht haben zu gehen* — the phrase from Karl's mother's letter — when Hannah re-reads it in March 1945, it carries a year's worth of additional resonance. The "ones who tried to go" are now, also, the man on the breakwater.

## Voice Notes

Hannah's register: close, sensory, the farm physical. The fence conversation should be rendered exactly in its length — not long; Karl is not a pourer-out of words. His answer to "are you afraid" is real and uncertain, not a tidy character statement. The close should be the letter in her hand and the icehouse across the yard — the pressure of what she hasn't done yet. No analysis.
