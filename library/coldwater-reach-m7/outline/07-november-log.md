# Chapter 07 — November Log

**One-line summary**: The first hard storm of the season keeps Eira in the harbormaster's shack through a long night; Owen's peacoat, the tide clock, and the log are the chapter's materials; the reader sees the first clear glimpse of what she saw on October 11.

**POV**: Eira Bowman
**Tense**: Third-person limited past
**Setting**: The harbormaster's shack, Coldwater Reach (single location)
**Time**: Mid-November 1943 (a stormy night)

**Chapter shape**: Bottle (one location, near real-time — late evening through early morning)
**Ending mode**: Literary fade
**Word target**: ~3,400

---

## Goal / Obstacle

**Goal**: Keep the harbor safe through a November blow — maintain radio contact with boats that haven't made port, log conditions accurately, keep the dock secure.
**Obstacle**: The storm is moderate but serious; two lobster boats are out late; the inner cove is rough. Also: the storm recalls October, and October is still unprocessed.

---

## Action Beats

1. **Opening: the shack at dusk**
   Eira arrives at the shack at four p.m. when the barometer begins dropping fast. She has done this before — many times, including the November 1935 storm that killed Owen. She does not think about that storm directly. She checks the mooring lines on the two draggers, the dock cleats, the breakwater light (working). She radios the two late lobster boats to confirm their positions and turns them back to the harbor cove.

2. **The peacoat**
   The shack is cold. She puts on Owen's peacoat, which hangs on a hook inside the door. It was too big for her in 1935 and she had it taken in at the shoulders. It fits her now as a coat that has been worn for eight years and adjusted. This is not a meditation; it is a thing she does when she is cold. The prose notes it and moves on.

3. **The log in the storm**
   She writes in the log: weather, barometer, radio contacts, the lobster boats' return times. She writes the way she always writes — compressed, factual, the notation of someone who knows what future readers of a log need. When she opens to today's entry she passes through pages and pages of her own writing, and at the back of her mind is the knowledge that Owen's pages are bound into the ledger at the other end, November 1935 and earlier.

4. **The boats come in**
   Both lobster boats make the harbor by nine p.m. She radios confirmation. She goes out once to check the dock lines in the worst of the blow — twenty minutes in the rain and spray, hands on dock line, the breakwater light visible through the fog. She goes back in.

5. **The quiet hours**
   The storm doesn't break but settles into a steady blow. The radio is quiet. She opens the log to write up the night. On impulse — not dramatic, not a decision she particularly makes — she turns to the back of the ledger, to the November 1935 pages. Owen's handwriting. The last entry in his hand: *November 12, 1935. Weather deteriorating, wind NNW increasing, barometer 29.1 and falling. Putting out additional mooring lines on —* It stops mid-sentence. The entry is unfinished. She has looked at this page perhaps two dozen times in eight years.

   She closes it and opens it to the current month.

6. **The glimpse: October 11**
   She writes the night's conditions. And then she pauses, pen on the page, and thinks about something she has not allowed herself to think directly since October 12.

   On the night of October 11, at approximately 23:00, she was at the dock checking the bilge pump on the *Callie B.* She saw a small wooden vessel come in from the south without running lights. She watched it tie at the far end of the dock — not a regular berth, but the dock's open end near the breakwater steps. It took her a moment to see that there was a person on it. One person. He tied the boat efficiently, in the way of someone who knows boats. He had a lantern, shuttered low. She saw him for perhaps twenty seconds before he moved off the dock toward the breakwater. She watched him go. She was forty feet away. He did not look in her direction. She does not know if he saw her.

   She did not call out to him. She had no official reason to stop him — coming into the dock without filing is irregular but not prohibited, and she was tired, and it was nearly midnight, and whatever the man was doing was his business. She finished with the bilge pump and walked home.

   In the morning he was dead at the end of the breakwater.

   This is the first time the prose gives the reader the full shape of what she saw. It is rendered as memory, in her characteristic short declaratives, without drama or self-indictment. She doesn't linger on it. She writes the night's conditions in the log. She does not write this.

7. **Close (literary fade)**
   The storm easing around two a.m. The radio quiet. Eira checks the dock one more time — one of the mooring cleats needs the line re-knotted; she does it in the dark by feel. Back in the shack, the tide clock: she notes the time, notes the tide, notes the barometer rising. She writes the final entry for the night. Owen's coat is still warm from her body heat. She does not take it off. The lamp low. Outside the rain settling to mist. She sits in the shack until first light before walking home, because that is what the harbor requires of her tonight.

---

## Outcome

The reader now knows what Eira saw. She saw the man arrive. She watched him walk to the breakwater. She did nothing. The chapter makes clear: the reason she withheld this is not malice or guilt but the economy of institutional survival — if she had spoken, she would have had to explain why she didn't engage, and she cannot afford the scrutiny. The prose does not state this. The log entry that says nothing says it.

---

## New Question Raised

What will Eira do with this knowledge she is carrying? Is it enough to know and hold, or will it eventually require action?

## Continuity Notes

- Owen's last log entry: "November 12, 1935. Weather deteriorating, wind NNW increasing, barometer 29.1 and falling. Putting out additional mooring lines on —" (unfinished; the entry stops because this is the storm that killed him).
- The specific memory of October 11: small wooden vessel, no running lights, far end of the dock near the breakwater steps, single occupant, tied efficiently, had a shuttered lantern, walked toward the breakwater. Eira saw him for approximately twenty seconds.
- This is the **canonical account** of what Eira witnessed. Later chapters must not alter these details. The man's face is never described (she didn't see it clearly). His approximate age: she estimated middle-aged or older, but cannot be certain.
- The *Callie B.* bilge pump check is her legitimate business that night (established in Ch 04).
- Owen's peacoat: taken in at the shoulders, fits her now after eight years (characters.md).
- The two lobster boats out in the storm: unnamed (minor logistics; they return safely by nine p.m.).
- This is the longest chapter in the setup phase. Its weight is structural, not dramatic: we now know the outline of the mystery's central fact.

## Voice Notes

Eira's chapter. The bottle shape suits her: one location, work-driven, the harbor as workplace. Owen's log entry should be rendered without commentary — it ends mid-sentence; the prose does not editorialize what this means. The October 11 memory should arrive in the same register as her log entries: factual, compressed, short declaratives. "She watched him go. She was forty feet away. He did not look in her direction." Trust the accumulation. The literary fade at the close is sensory — the tide clock, the barometer rising, the coat still warm — not reflective.
