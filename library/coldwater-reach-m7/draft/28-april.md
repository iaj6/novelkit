# April

The paper came in the school's Wednesday mail. He picked it up from the front office table on his way through — the Rockland Courier, folded once lengthwise as the secretary always left it — and tucked it under his arm. The announcement had already come through the school hallway's radio by nine that morning. He had been at the corridor's edge, between his classroom and the Latin room, when the broadcast ran through. He had stood and listened to the announcer's careful American voice for three or four minutes and then he had walked into his classroom and written *prima declinatio* on the board and taught the lesson.

He carried the paper home under his arm and set it on the desk without opening it.

He graded the Latin exercises. He did this with the paper beside the exercise books, visible but unread, and one part of his mind went through the work — the genitive plural errors, the margin notes, which of the three students had understood the principle and which had guessed correctly without understanding it — and the other part of his mind was somewhere he declined to follow it. He put the exercise books in the pile for morning and set down the pen.

He went downstairs. Lillian had left food in the kitchen. He ate at the small table alone — Henry was still at the school, something with the boiler — and when Henry came in, washing his hands at the sink, they exchanged two or three sentences about the cold for April and then Henry heated what was in the pot and sat down, and they finished the evening together without talking further. This was not unusual. Marcus had stopped noticing it as an accommodation sometime around the second winter, which he understood, when he noticed the stopping, for what it was.

He went upstairs.

He turned on the lamp and sat down at the desk. The Rockland Courier was where he had set it. He picked it up and opened it to the front page. He read the headline and the first three paragraphs of the dispatch. He turned to page two. He looked at page two. He turned to page three.

There were photographs on page three.

He looked at the photographs for perhaps four seconds before he folded the paper closed. He set it face-down on the desk. He sat in the chair for a moment with his hands in his lap.

He had known. This was the thing that required sitting with, in this room, in this chair: he had known. Not in the way of the photographs — not with that specificity, not at that scale, not in the form of the specific architecture the photographs were now showing — but in the form available to a man who had watched Vienna change in 1937 and 1938 and then left it. He had understood the direction. He had followed the logic as far as it went before he chose to stop following it, because the thing you construct in your mind when you follow that logic to its conclusion is a thing you cannot allow to be true, and so you manage the logic at a certain distance, you hold it in the form of structure rather than completion, the way you can know the shape of a building from its foundation plan without having to stand inside it.

The photographs were inside it.

He did not open the paper again.

He thought about the people he knew. Not as a category — specifically, in the way a man thinks about the specific texture of individual encounters across seventeen years in a Viennese academic institution and two years in Paris. Jakob R., who had been his closest colleague in the philology department, who had a particular habit in seminar of beginning his disagreements with a question rather than a rebuttal — arms crossed, leaning back, the question arriving before he had finished forming it — a habit Marcus could still reproduce exactly if he was working to fall asleep and needed something to hold in his mind. They had exchanged letters in 1939, three in 1940, and then the letters stopped. The last one, in November of 1940, had carried a Berlin address. Marcus had not written to a Berlin address after 1941. He had arranged the reasons for this in a form that did not require him to name them.

Grete H., who had been his department's German-language assistant for seven years, whose administrative precision he had depended on in the way one depends on a function that works correctly without one's attention, and who had been in Vienna in 1938 when he left it, and from whom he had received nothing since. He had asked once through the Emergency Committee, in late 1942, and received a letter that said the committee had no current documentation for that name. He had understood, at the time, what current documentation meant, and he had done what he always did with the things he understood in this way, which was to hold them in the form of probably but not confirmed, which was the form available to him, and move on to the next thing that required moving on to.

Two others — names he permitted himself in his mind without writing them anywhere — whose last known addresses had been in Paris in 1940. He had not found them through the Emergency Committee's Paris contacts, who were themselves mostly gone from Paris by the time he was writing letters in 1942 and 1943. He had not let himself complete a thought about them in over two years. He let himself complete one now, briefly, sitting at the desk in the April dark with the folded paper in front of him.

Then he thought about Felix K.

This had a different quality. He had been thinking about Felix K. since January — since the Thursday evening he came back from the harbormaster's shack and spent two hours in this room with the Feld letter and a sheet of working paper before he opened the German notebook and wrote the entry — in a specific register of probability that was not certainty and was not the same as comfort and was not the same as what he was now sitting with about Jakob R. and Grete H. and the others.

Felix K. had tried to go. That was what the evidence said. He had been documented in Yarmouth in September of 1943, heading south by sea, and the sea had brought him as far as Coldwater, and there he had run out of road.

He had not been in one of the camps.

Marcus turned this over in his mind. He had turned it over before — in January, after he assembled the theory, and in February, and in March, on evenings when the European news on the school hallway radio made the structure more visible — and he had not arrived at an answer, and he did not arrive at one now. There was a line of reasoning that said: he died in the Atlantic coast dark, in October, alone in an unlighted boat in cold water, and that was its own fact. There was no architecture behind it, no system, no list on which his name would appear. He had died in the specific and local way, which was the way of weather and the harbor and chance, and no one had done it to him, or it had been done to him by the full weight of what Europe had become for people who needed to leave it and ran out of time, which was a different thing from the same thing, and Marcus had not resolved this distinction and suspected he would not.

He went, Marcus thought. He tried.

After a while he picked up the newspaper and took it downstairs.

---

Henry and Lillian were in the parlor. The Zenith console was on — the same cabinet, the same chairs they had sat in on the evening of June 6 last year when the D-Day news came through and Marcus had translated BBC fragments in a low voice and afterward on the back-street porch neither of them had spoken for twenty minutes. Tonight no translation was needed or useful. The Zenith was carrying the same American voice, or another American voice with the same quality: the calibrated evenness of a man delivering copy that was not written to be delivered, because no one had written this kind of copy before. Lillian had her knitting in her lap. Her hands were very still.

Marcus set the newspaper on the sofa cushion beside him, face-down, and sat.

The Zenith was reporting on Bergen-Belsen tonight. The correspondent described, in the careful language of a wire dispatch, what British soldiers had found at the camp when they arrived. He read numbers. He described the camp's geography — the compound, the structures, the numbers of people found alive and the numbers not. The language grew more careful the more specific it became, in the way of a man who has been asked to describe something he does not have words for and is constructing the words as he goes, with insufficient material.

Marcus listened. He had been in the structure of this knowledge for seven years — since Vienna, since the winter of 1937 when the first changes were visible in the institution and in the streets — and now the structure had a form that the language could reach, barely, in insufficient words read by a correspondent in a careful voice on the Zenith in the Crow house parlor. He sat on the sofa and did not move.

He did not translate. He had enough English for this. He had enough of every language he possessed for this.

After a while — he had stopped tracking time — Lillian said, quietly: "I'm going to bed."

She set her knitting in the basket beside the chair and stood. Before she went she looked at Marcus once, and in that look was something she was measuring against something else, deciding not to try to name. He met her eyes and nodded slightly. She went upstairs. Her footsteps crossed the ceiling above their heads and then stopped.

Henry was in his chair. Marcus was on the sofa.

The Zenith was still on.

---

Henry said: "Is there anyone you're trying to find out about?"

Marcus said: "Several. I don't know how to find out."

Henry said: "There will be lists."

Marcus said: "Yes."

Henry said: "That will take time."

Marcus said: "Yes."

They sat with the Zenith. The broadcast moved from the correspondent's copy to a recorded voice — a soldier, one of the soldiers who had been among the first to enter the camp — describing what he had seen. His voice was not calibrated. It started and found its direction and then lost it and started again. Marcus listened to the difficulty of what the soldier was doing — assembling, in real time, in front of a microphone, a language for something that no language had been built to describe in advance — and understood this from the other direction, because he had been working at this problem from the other direction since 1938. There was no adequate vocabulary. You used the words that existed and they were not sufficient and you kept going.

Henry got up at some point and went to the kitchen. He came back with two cups of coffee, set one down on the low table beside the sofa, and resumed his chair without anything being said. The Crow house coffee was better than anything else available in Coldwater, which Marcus had thought about at various points over three years without ever having remarked on it. He held the cup in both hands. The warmth was specific and useful.

The front-hall clock struck eleven.

Henry reached over and turned the volume down — not off, only down to the register where the Zenith voices were present in the room without demanding it. A small adjustment. He made it without looking at Marcus, who was looking at the floor in front of the sofa.

The clock struck twelve.

Henry stood. He said: "I'll be up."

Marcus said: "I know."

Henry went to the stairs. The third step from the top creaked, the same as it always did — that step in that house in that particular way, which Marcus had known for three years, the same as he knew the specific frequency of the harbor bell on foggy mornings and the boiler's low cycle at the school. Henry's footsteps crossed the upper floor and stopped.

Marcus turned off the Zenith. He sat for a moment in the quiet with the newspaper still face-down on the cushion beside him. Then he picked it up and went upstairs.

---

The lamp on. He pulled the chain and the light came on over the desk, the east window black above it, and he sat down.

The German notebook was on the shelf above the desk, between the English journal and the grammar workbook, the spine written in his English hand. He had last opened it in January — the Thursday evening he came back from the harbormaster's shack, the evening of the fragment and what the fragment said about the letters, the probable name. He had spent two hours working out the arithmetic on a separate sheet first and then he had transcribed the conclusion into the notebook because the notebook was where he kept things that needed to remain true when he looked at them again.

He took it down.

He opened it to the last page of writing. The January entry was there, in his German handwriting, which was not as clear as it had been. His English handwriting had become better than his German handwriting without his having done anything to cause this, which still surprised him when he noticed it, because the tools of his formation were not supposed to rearrange themselves without his knowledge.

*Fe—?—x. Felix K. Yarmouth, September 1943. Coldwater, Oktober 1943. Innenbar, Dezember 1944 geborgen.*

He read it.

He took his pen from the desk tray. He uncapped it.

Below the entry, on the next line, he wrote:

*Die die versucht haben zu gehen.*

He capped the pen. He closed the notebook. He set it back on the shelf.

---

He turned off the lamp.

The room in the dark was the same room it had been in every darkness for thirty-eight months: the east window a shade less dark than the walls surrounding it, the desk a shape he knew, the radiator on the south wall quiet for the night, the slanted ceiling on the north wall where the roofline came down to the plaster. He had arrived in this room in March of 1942 with a cardboard case, two books, and a suit with the shoulders gone loose from a year of ill fitting. He had sat at this desk and looked out this east window in August of that year and in November and in the first winter and in the winter after that and in the spring and the summer and the autumn that followed, through the mornings when Lillian knocked twice on the door frame and through the evenings when Henry's footsteps went past his door and the third step from the top announced him going to bed.

He did not open the shortwave.

He thought, briefly, about what the Europe across the east window consisted of now: what would be in it, who might be in it, what the street he had lived on for fourteen years looked like in April 1945. He had not allowed himself this thought often. He allowed it now for two or three minutes, sitting in the chair in the dark, and the thought did not arrive anywhere comforting and it did not arrive anywhere devastating. It arrived somewhere he did not have a word for.

He would find out. That was all he had.

The notebook was on the shelf. What he had written in it remained.

The harbor was somewhere below the hill to the east, which he knew without seeing it. The ice was out of the inner cove by now. The boats were back on their moorings, the buoys rocking in the April chop, the tide coming in or going out according to its own arithmetic. He had been in this town for thirty-eight months. He had made it to this room in this house with this specific east-facing window. That was where he was.

He sat in the dark for a while. Then he went to bed.
