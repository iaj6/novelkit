# A Letter from New York

The second line of the examination instructions had given him trouble since Sunday evening.

He had written it out in his room the night before — three attempts in the margin of his notebook page, each crossed — and then again this morning on the classroom board before the first class came up the east stair. He stood at the board with the chalk and read back what he had written: *When you do not know a word, consult the sense of the sentence.* The problem was *consult*, which required the student to have an intuition about what consulting a sentence meant, and it was exactly this intuition that he could not assume. He wiped the line.

He tried: *If you do not know a word, work from what the sentence needs it to mean.*

He stood back.

The German classroom occupied the second floor's east end, and in December it received the morning light in a way that was briefly pleasant before the cold coming off the north sill reasserted itself — the cold was better now, since Henry had planed the casement, but there was still a way the December light looked through those windows that was more like winter light pressing against glass than like light in a room. The examination sheets waited on the corner of his desk: three copies, typed himself on the school typewriter, which did not have the characters for *ü* or *ä*, requiring small substitutions he had worked out to preserve the sense if not the precision.

He read the second instruction line back. That would do. He turned to lay the sheets on the desks and the bell from the floor below rang for the first class.

---

Parsons appeared during the interval between classes.

She came with the mail binder under her arm, the Tuesday delivery from the general store, which was also the post office, the mail sorted before school and walked up the hill in the binder. Marcus had Latin in twenty minutes. He knew, in the abstract, that nothing requiring an immediate response was likely to be in the binder, and he opened it anyway, as he always did, with the specific attention that he applied to anything arriving from outside Coldwater: the possibility that it was news.

The envelope from Dr. Feld was fourth from the top.

*Emergency Committee in Aid of Displaced Foreign Scholars.* The letterhead on the envelope was the letterhead he had been receiving, in one form or another, since autumn 1940 — placement confirmation, credential documentation, quarterly notices about the wider network of displaced scholars the Committee maintained. Feld's own letters had been less frequent: two in 1941, one each in 1942 and the early part of 1943. Marcus had sent two postcards in the spring of this year, brief, reporting that his position continued and his address remained the same.

The envelope was thicker than a practical letter.

He opened it and read it standing. He had to sit down and read it again.

Feld was writing about a problem the Committee had encountered in tracking scholars who had been in transit during 1942 and into 1943. Some had arrived at their placements and failed to notify the office; this was not uncommon, and the Committee had means for re-establishing contact in most of those cases. Others were in Europe, unreachable for reasons that Feld described with a care that Marcus read as the careful prose of someone who did not yet know how to describe what was happening in Europe and was not going to guess. The third category was the one Feld lingered on: scholars who appeared to have been in transit and then ceased to be in transit, with no subsequent documentation and no record at any receiving institution.

Marcus knew that phrase. *Keine nachfolgende Dokumentation.* No subsequent documentation. The Emergency Committee used it the way a port authority used *vessel not arrived* — accurately, without affect. The period between his release from the camp and his arrival in Boston in August 1941 had been several weeks in which Marcus Klein existed in no file. The Committee had an address for him — the camp — that was no longer his address, and no forwarding address until he made contact from Boston. For those weeks, he had been a man of whom it could be written: *last documented at.* And then the next address.

The specific case was in the third page of the letter. Feld was careful with surnames, citing Committee privacy practice; he used initials where he could. A Dr. Felix K., he wrote — a linguist of German-Jewish origin, formerly of the University of Leipzig, who had been corresponding with the Committee from somewhere in Canada since early in 1943. His last letter to the office had been postmarked Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, September 22, 1943. In it he had told the Committee of his intention to travel south by sea, to reach Boston by late October; he had a contact in Boston. He had not written since. The Boston contact had heard nothing. The Committee had no other channel.

Feld was writing to Marcus partly because Marcus was placed in Maine — geographically relevant to a route running south from the Nova Scotia coast — and partly because he knew Marcus had been long enough on the Committee's rolls to have some reach into the network. Did Marcus know anything of a Dr. Felix K., Leipzig, a linguist?

Marcus read the letter through twice more.

He had a partial recollection — not a name, but something adjacent to a name, the way a word hovered at the edge of retrieval before either arriving or failing to arrive. A man at a philology conference in Vienna in 1936. Something at the intersection of Germanic philology and historical linguistics; he recalled a corridor in the Anatomiegebäude, the cold institutional light the building always had, two men talking in the specific Leipzig-accented German he had always found recognizable at a distance. He had been introduced. He had not spoken with the man at any length.

The name would not come. He might be constructing the memory from Feld's description rather than from the conference itself.

He set the letter on his desk.

Latin was beginning.

---

The German examination was the following Tuesday.

He read through Hannah Pfeiffer's paper first, which was not his usual order — he customarily worked alphabetically, which put Doris Hatch first and Hannah last — but which he found himself doing anyway. She had translated the passage from Goethe's correspondence correctly, which was not surprising; what stopped him was the word order. The passage was a letter Goethe had written to Schiller in October 1798 about the difficulty of rendering a natural scene in language without losing what had made the scene worth rendering. In the key sentence, the verb fell at the end, as German required. Most students, encountering this, pulled the verb forward; English demanded it, and the instinct was not wrong. Hannah Pfeiffer had left the verb where Goethe had placed it. The English she had produced was technically awkward and philologically correct, a sentence translated in the grammatical sense rather than the idiomatic one — you could see the German inside the English the way you could sometimes see the structure of a building inside the material it was built from.

He marked it correct and moved on to Doris Hatch.

He had not said anything. It was not the examination's occasion, and it was not entirely the German's occasion either — not a conversation he was equipped to have in December with three weeks until Christmas break. He wrote *Good work* on her sheet and the same on Doris's and *This is improving* on Louise Pelletier's and meant all three.

Latin was going well. Caesar's *De Bello Gallico*, the organization of the camp and the construction of the fortifications, twelve students working through the ablative absolutes with the patient incomprehension of people who found something merely difficult.

He encountered Henry Crow on Thursday afternoon in the second-floor hallway, Henry moving past with the supply cart.

"The window," Marcus said.

Henry looked at him.

"The north sill," Marcus said. "The cold isn't coming through the way it was."

"Block plane," Henry said. "And new caulk on the outside. It'll hold as long as the temperature stays above ten degrees. Below ten, the frame tightens." He said, "Might stick closed."

Marcus said he appreciated it.

Henry said it was his job and pushed the cart toward the supply closet at the hallway's end.

---

The school thinned in the last two weeks before Christmas break, the way it thinned every year — boys finding reasons to be on the water or in the timber, girls finding reasons to be needed at home, the teachers accepting these reasons with less scrutiny than they would have applied before the war. The building was quiet in the afternoons. Marcus finished the examination corrections and began preparing the January syllabus, working in the classroom for an hour after four o'clock because his room was colder than the classroom and the classroom had the east light for another forty minutes.

He had read Feld's letter three more times over the week. He had not replied.

---

He read it again that evening in his room, the lamp on, the letter flat on his desk.

Outside the east window: December dark that had been in place since half past four. There was no moon he could see and the cold pressed against the glass. The radiator on the south wall was running well, which it did not always do this early in December, and the room was warm enough that he could work at his desk without his coat.

He set the letter down. He took the German notebook from the shelf above his desk — more than half-full now, the compressed German hand running through its pages from April — and turned to the first blank leaf.

He wrote, in German, what he had:

*Felix K. Linguist. Leipzig. Zuletzt belegt in Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, 22. September 1943. Beabsichtigte, Boston auf dem Seeweg zu erreichen, Ende Oktober.*

He looked at what he had written for a moment and then wrote below it:

*Ich glaube, ihn nicht zu kennen.*

He set down the pen. The 1936 Vienna corridor was still where it had been all week — present, the quality of the light available, the Leipzig-accented German available — and the name was still not there. He might be wrong about the conference. He might have assembled the memory from the description and mistaken the assembly for recollection.

He picked up the pen. Below the previous lines he wrote:

*Yarmouth nach Boston. Die Küste von Maine liegt dazwischen.*

He read the three lines back.

Yarmouth, Nova Scotia. He had not been there. He knew it as a port town on the southwestern coast, a transit point on the sea routes between the Atlantic provinces and New England — knew it in the way he knew most Maritime geography: from a map he had studied when he was trying to understand his own route south, the autumn of 1941. He could bring the coastline's shape from the map. He could not bring the harbor, the town's character, what it looked like from the water in late September.

September 22, 1943. That was barely three weeks before the first heavy weather of the fall. A small vessel could make the run in favorable conditions in two to three days, or longer if the weather turned, or much longer, or not at all if the weather turned badly enough. It was a route that could be done. He did not know whether it had been.

He sat with the notebook open and the lamp on and thought about the phrase *zuletzt belegt in*.

Last documented in. The Emergency Committee's vocabulary for the point on the record where the thread ended. It was precise language; Feld was a precise man. He used the passive construction deliberately — *last documented in* rather than *last seen in* or *last known to be in* — because the documentation was what the Committee could stand behind, and he would not claim more certainty than the record gave him. The documentation ended at Yarmouth on September 22, 1943. What the documentation had been attached to continued, or did not continue, beyond that point, and the file had no way to say which.

He had been *last documented in* a place himself, in the summer of 1941. The documentation had said the camp; the camp had released him; for some weeks after that he had moved south without an address any file could reach him at. He had arrived in Boston, eventually, and written to the Emergency Committee from a boarding house on Commonwealth Avenue, and his file had been updated: Marcus Klein, Coldwater Reach, Maine, March 1942. Everything after that date was documented. Everything before August 1941, going back through the camp and London and Paris to Vienna, was documented also, in one archive or another.

Dr. Felix K. had been last documented in Yarmouth.

He did not write any of this in the notebook.

He put the pen down and opened the shortwave.

The BBC was not finding him tonight — static and the fringe of something that might have been German, or might have been Dutch, sliding in and out at the edge of resolution. He moved down and found an American broadcast, clear and even, a news signal from somewhere in the domestic network. The Pacific: the Marshall Islands, conditions described with the precise optimism of someone who had been given permission to transmit and was being careful with it. He listened to the names of islands he had not known before the war and would not have had occasion to know. Maloelap. Wotje. He did not know where these were on a chart with any precision; he was assembling the Pacific the way he had assembled the Maine coast, location by location, from a distance.

At nine-thirty he turned off the shortwave.

He took the Emergency Committee folder from the shelf between the English journal and the Stifter he had been reading since September. The folder held twelve letters now, going back to 1940, a paper record of his placement and its history. He put Feld's letter in the folder and put the folder back on the shelf.

He closed the German notebook and set it on the shelf above his desk.

Outside the east window: December dark, undiminished. He thought about Yarmouth, which he had not been to and did not know. He thought about the phrase *last documented in* and what came after it in the Committee's language: nothing, and then a date, and then the passive construction that meant someone had stopped being accountable to any record. He had been that. He knew the inside of it.

He turned off the lamp.

The room was dark and cold and smelled of woodsmoke from the furnace two floors below and of the particular density of cold books in a New England December. He did not write anything more. The notebook was on the shelf, the folder was on the shelf, the shortwave sat silent on his desk. He did not move for a while. Outside the window the dark had the quality December dark always had at this latitude: complete, and cold.
