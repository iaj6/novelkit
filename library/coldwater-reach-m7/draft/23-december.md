# December

The radio had been bringing the news since Saturday. German counteroffensive. Belgium. The Ardennes. Three gaps in the Allied line, the communiqué said, the front still moving west. The announcer read the communiqués with the practiced evenness the networks had developed — it gave the information without telling you how to hold it. She had been keeping the radio on since Saturday morning, the volume low, the way she had kept it on through the June weeks when the news was also continuous and also required management. There was nothing to do about the Ardennes from the harbormaster's shack in Coldwater, Maine. She did her work and kept the radio on.

December harbor: four lobster boats at the dock, the Callie B. laid up for her winter haul-out at the south end, two moorings occupied by day-sailers whose owners would not come until April. The mail boat Tuesdays and Fridays, Rockland to Coldwater and back. Since the weekend blow, which had cleared anything still out to tie up or stay out, nothing in or out. The harbor was quiet in the way it was quiet in December, which was different from the way it was quiet in October: in December the quiet was the shape of the season, not an interruption.

Ice: not yet on the inner cove. She had been noting the water temperature dropping faster than last year and had marked it in the weather column without drawing a conclusion she couldn't yet support. In 1943 the cove had iced in the third week of January. She thought it might come sooner this year.

She did the 0700 log entry — wind NNW ten knots, barometer 30.04 and rising, sky overcast, no precipitation — and kept the radio on.

The antenna check: the northwest frequency was crackling. Third consecutive Tuesday. That moved it from coincidence to the antenna wire and she put it on the maintenance list with a note: before January.

The 0900 communiqué ran for six minutes. Road junction in Belgium. German armor advancing on multiple axes. Air support impossible in current weather. She turned the volume down a half-step and went back to the freight manifests.

---

Farber came in around nine.

She heard him on the dock boards before the door: his footfall familiar, heavier on the left side, the right leg working harder than it should because of the knee he did not discuss. He had been running long lines off the inner bar since Thanksgiving week — winter lobstering, the water cold enough now that the lobster were moving deeper, but traps still out. He came in with his catch on the June M. and then he came up to the shack.

He had something tucked under his arm. A piece of tarp, folded over. He set it on her desk. He took his cap off and put it in his coat pocket. He said he had pulled it up in his net that morning, off the inner bar — maybe eighty yards out from the breakwater's cove-side base. It had come up in the trawl with a quarter-crate of rock crab and he had kept it separate. He was looking at the dock through the window while he said this.

She said: what is it.

He said: you should look.

She was not going to ask him why he had brought it to her rather than leaving it on the dock or throwing it back. He was Reg Farber, and this was December 1944, and the inner bar was eighty yards from where the breakwater met the cove bottom, and October 1943 was fourteen months ago. He had brought it to the harbor authority because it had come out of the harbor's floor. He had brought it to her specifically because of October.

She unwrapped the tarp.

---

A wallet. Or what a wallet becomes in fourteen months of saltwater and tidal action: the leather warped and contracted, shrunk into itself, the color bleached and darkened until it was a gray-brown that told you nothing about what it had started as. The stitching on one long side was open, the two layers of leather parted and curling away from each other at the seam. She turned it over in her hands.

Fourteen months, approximately, if it had gone into the water on or near the night of October 11, 1943. The tidal set in the inner cove ran north-northwest on the ebb. The bar was where something would settle, given time and the particular geometry of the cove. Eighty yards was consistent.

She worked it with her hands carefully. The billfold pocket: gone, the seam failed and the pocket open and empty. Water and fourteen months were sufficient for that. The rear face pocket: same — the lower seam failed, nothing remaining. She turned the wallet in her hands and found the center divider pocket on the interior face.

That seam had held. The leather had been pressed into the leather by the compression and the drying, the two layers sealed against each other in something close to adhesion. She worked her thumbnail into the top edge of the seam, a quarter inch at a time, without tearing. She worked it open the full length.

Inside: a piece of paper, folded twice. The paper was heavy stock — heavier than ordinary writing paper, the weight of an envelope's back flap or the card at the front of a formal letter. It had been wet and compressed and partly dried and wet again, and it had come out the other side as something closer to thin board than paper: stiff along the fold lines, slightly bowed. She unfolded it flat on the desk blotter and smoothed the folds with two fingers.

Handwriting. She could see the shape of it before she could see the letters — the slant and press of a careful hand, the ink bled at the edges of each stroke into the paper's grain but held in the center of each line where the pen had pressed hardest. The lower half of the card was gone: a uniform gray-brown wash, the ink dispersed too completely to reconstruct. The upper third was partial — lines, possible shapes, nothing she could resolve into letters. But in the center third, where the outermost fold had held longest and the protected seam had given it the most shelter, three letters were readable. Possibly four; the right edge of the fold had not quite held, and what might have been a fourth letter was present as a partial form.

Handwritten, not printed. A formal script. The ascenders had the backward cant — right to left, the slope reversed from what English cursive did — that she had seen in the typed header on the Yarmouth letter. Two of the three clear letters had that cant. The third, which sat between the two ascenders and was a lower form, had a double-barred crosspiece she had seen Marcus Klein's students copying badly from the board on the two or three occasions she had passed through the school hallway long enough to see anything through the classroom door.

The letters were German. She was certain of that much. She could not read them.

She looked at them for a long time. The radio came on at the half-hour. She did not turn toward it.

---

She folded the card back along its original lines and wrapped the wallet in the tarp. She opened the middle drawer — log pages in their column on the left, freight files for October through December, and at the back behind the January manifests the Yarmouth letter still in its envelope, where she had put it in April — and pressed the bundle to the rear of the drawer, behind the Yarmouth envelope, against the back wall. She shut the drawer. She took the key from the ring and turned it in the lock. The key went back on the ring. The spare was at the house, on the hook inside the kitchen door. No other key.

She said to Farber: I'll look into it.

He said: I figured you would. He paused. He said: I wasn't going to say anything one way or the other.

She said: I know.

He put his cap back on and went back to the June M.

---

The rest of Tuesday was the day's work: the 1300 log entry, wind WNW nine knots, barometer holding, one vessel in from Rockland. The February dock assignments, which had to reach the District office before the last week of December. She ran the 1400 radio check; the northwest frequency was clear. She went out for the afternoon rounds at fifteen hundred and it took twenty-five minutes in the cold, the light already going, and everything was in order.

She did not open the middle drawer.

At five she locked the shack, locked the dock gate, and walked up the hill. December in Coldwater: the harbor smell off the water, herring and diesel and cold salt, the dock lights at their minimum. The inner cove dark and still at the ebb, the bar visible as a faint pale line at the water's surface. The breakwater's red light turning on and off at its interval in the distance.

She went home. She made supper — salt fish, potatoes — and ate standing at the stove because she was tired. She washed the dishes. She banked the coal stove and checked the back door and went to bed.

The wallet was in the drawer. The drawer was locked. She had the key on the ring on the nail by the door. In the morning she would take it and go down to the shack and do the 0700 log and keep the radio on.

She lay in the dark.

She did not tell anyone.
