# June Radio

Four-thirty in June was different from any other four-thirty. The dark was shorter and thinner — already a gray line at the east edge of the fields when she opened the barn door, the horizon somewhere between night and something. The cows were already up. June made them impatient, all that summer weight on them, and they came to the stanchions without needing to be pressed. The hay was warm and dry and the barn's smell this month was not the closed winter smell but something looser, the boards having breathed themselves out all through May.

She moved through the milking the way she always did, can to can, one end of the row to the other. The light over the door came on automatically when she switched the barn circuit; she had not needed the kerosene lamp since April. By the time she finished the second row the gray outside had become something almost blue. By the time she was done and washing down the stanchions a quarter past six, the fields were fully lit.

She came out of the barn. The south field was high and green, the hay cutting maybe a week out. The silo, the icehouse, the farmhouse: the yard in the morning quiet, everything where it was.

Lonzo arrived at eight with the prisoners. He came through the front gate in his patient way with the Springfield on his shoulder and the four of them behind him: Fischer first, then Brandt, then Walther, then Karl last, looking at the fence posts or the middle distance the way he usually did. Lonzo said good morning toward the barn where she was standing. She nodded. They went to the south field for the morning's work and the day looked like a Tuesday in June.

---

The kitchen radio was on at breakfast, as it was every morning now. The habit had formed somewhere in the spring; she couldn't have named the day. Her father held the Rockland paper without reading it. Her mother stood at the stove with her back to the room. The radio cycled through its programs in the ordinary way.

She was pouring coffee into her cup.

The radio said: *Allied forces have landed on the coast of Normandy. The operation is underway.*

She set the coffee pot down. The cup was beside it, she had not poured, she did not remember stopping.

The radio kept talking.

Her father pushed back from the table. He crossed to the radio and turned it louder, both hands on the knob, and came back and sat down. He had his coat on from the morning barn check and he did not take it off. He sat at the table with his coat on.

The radio said: *A massive amphibious operation. Airborne divisions in the early morning hours. The Allies on French soil.*

She sat down. She had her coffee cup in front of her now. She picked it up and held it.

Her mother came in from the garden at quarter past nine and sat down at the table between them and stayed. The three of them in the kitchen with the radio going, eating and not eating. Hannah ate a piece of bread, then set the second piece down. Her father's food sat where her mother had put it. Her mother had her hands folded in front of her and her attention pointed somewhere ahead of herself, all the way into the radio, the way her silence collected itself when it had somewhere to go.

The CBS correspondent said: *Casualties are reported to be significant. The fighting continues at several points along the beachhead.*

The BBC faded in through static at ten-fifteen when the kitchen set picked up the signal — it did this some mornings, caught the BBC clear and others got nothing — and the BBC voice was more formal than CBS but said similar things: *Operation Overlord. The largest amphibious operation in history. Allied forces establishing positions.* She tried to hold the two accounts in sequence and could not. Two voices from two studios in London both saying Normandy, saying beaches, saying dawn.

Her father's hands were flat on the table.

Through the window over the sink she could see the south field.

Four figures out there, far enough that she could not read their faces, not even which one had stopped moving. At the northeast corner, one of them stood briefly apart from the others — she could tell it was Karl by his build, the particular width of his shoulders, the size of him she had been looking at across fences and through glass for more than a year. Then he bent down and went back to the row and he was not distinguishable from the others at that distance.

Camp operations would have told them. She did not know in what form or at what hour — roll call, a guard with a statement, something. But it would be strange if they hadn't known by now.

She took her plate to the counter and washed it and put it in the rack. Her father was still at the table. The radio was still talking.

---

She heated soup at noon and set it beside him. He ate it, the bowl emptying while she ate her own soup standing at the counter. The radio had not been turned off. It said: *The Prime Minister addressed the House of Commons this morning. The King has spoken to the nation.* It said: *Fighting continues at the beachhead.* It said the names of the beaches and she could not retain them in order.

She went out at one o'clock. The north barn needed the afternoon hay brought down from the loft, and the east-pasture gate needed checking after the spring flooding had shifted the post on the hinge side. The farm did not stop for this. She pulled her jacket from the hook by the door and went out.

Outside the day was warm and cloudless and knew nothing about any of it. The fields were the fields. The sky was a June sky. She went to the north barn and spent the better part of an hour on the hay and the gate, and when she came out and crossed the south end of the yard she was taking the shorter way back to the kitchen.

Karl was at the northeast corner, thirty yards from the fence. He was bent at the waist, working along the row, his head down. She could see his face clearly from this distance. It was his working face, the same one every day, and she could not read anything in it from here.

She did not stop.

There was nothing to say today and no place to say it — not with Lonzo twenty-five yards away eating his lunch off the tailgate, not with all of them visible from the farmhouse window, not with the morning still in her head and the radio going in the kitchen and no German that was the right German for the gap between the fence and the row and what this day was. She went back to the kitchen.

Her father was still at the table. He had turned the newspaper face-down at some point, which she had not seen him do.

---

By four o'clock he had moved to the parlor.

The parlor radio was the better set, the signal cleaner and the BBC coming in without the kitchen static. He was in the chair he used only in winter — the corner chair by the window, the one with the gray slipcover her mother put on every November and took off every April. The coat was off now, folded over the arm. He had not called her in.

She passed the doorway twice on errands and saw him there each time in the same position: hands in his lap, the radio on the side table. The quality of his stillness was the same one she had seen in December 1941, that Sunday, when she had been fifteen and had stood in the kitchen doorway not knowing whether to go in.

She went in and sat in the straight chair by the door. He did not look at her. The BBC correspondent was saying that additional forces were moving to reinforce the initial landings, that the situation continued to develop, that the casualties could not yet be fully accounted for. She sat for a while and then stood and went back to the kitchen.

Her mother came through a few minutes later with a cup of coffee and went into the parlor and set it on the side table next to the radio, next to his hand. She said nothing. He reached for the cup without looking up and drank from it and set it back down. Her mother came back to the kitchen.

Hannah watched her come back and turned to the stove and put the kettle on.

---

The prisoners came in from the field at five. She heard them through the open kitchen window — Lonzo's voice carrying across the yard without the words, and then the four of them moving through the yard to the icehouse, and then the icehouse door. Then quiet from that direction.

She made supper. By six her father had come in from the parlor and sat at the table and eaten. He ate all of his supper, carefully, the way he did everything carefully. When he finished he set his fork down and sat for a moment and went back to the parlor.

Hannah washed the dishes. Her mother dried them and put them away. They had been doing this without talking all spring, longer than that, and tonight was the same except that the quiet in the kitchen had the weight of the day behind it. Her mother folded the dish towel and hung it on the hook. She looked at Hannah for a moment — not speaking, never speaking, but something in the look — and then went to bed.

Hannah dried her hands and went to the table.

She had *The Selected Poems of Robert Frost* open in front of her. She'd been working through it since the term ended, not because Marcus had assigned it but because she kept returning to it, kept finding the same pages. She was not reading now. She had the book open and the lamp on and the radio coming low from the parlor, where her father had turned it down after supper — she could hear it the way she heard rain, as a sound that was always there but was not a voice.

He came into the kitchen at half past eight.

She heard him before she saw him, his footsteps crossing the parlor floor. He came through the doorway and crossed the kitchen and stood at the window over the sink. He did not acknowledge her.

Through the window: the yard, half-lit by the kitchen lamp. The icehouse at the far edge of the lamplight, its window dark.

He stood there for a long moment.

He said, to the window, to himself, to no one in the room: "Nun ist es fertig."

He turned from the window and went upstairs. She heard his footsteps on the stairs, even and slow, the familiar weight of him on the second step that always creaked. The door at the top of the stairs opened and then closed.

The house was quiet.

The radio was still on in the parlor, very low, a program that had nothing to do with France — some orchestra in a studio somewhere, the sound barely carrying. She could hear it the way she heard the pump.

She did not move.

The kitchen lamp was on. Through the window the icehouse held no light. Karl was in the icehouse with Fischer and Brandt and Walther, in the dark across the yard, and whatever this day had been for them was behind the dark window and she had no way into it and it was not the night to try.

Her father had said: Nun ist es fertig.

She sat at the table. The fields were moving outside the open window, the long summer grass at the field edge, the nighthawks somewhere over the south field making the sound they made in June. She had heard it every June of her life. She heard it now.

The radio on low from the parlor. Karl in the dark across the yard.

What came next, she did not know.
