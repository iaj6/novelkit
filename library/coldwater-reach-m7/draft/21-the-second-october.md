# The Second October

The Tuesday morning in the second week of October was cold before it was light. Hannah had been up since four-thirty, which was not different from any other morning except for the cold, which said plainly that the season had crossed into a different register in the night and would not go back. She milked with the kerosene lamp because there was not enough daylight yet. Steam came off the nearest cow's back in the lamplight. The barn was warm in the animal way and cold at the boards and at the high window where the October air pressed in.

She had known what date it was since she turned the calendar page in September and saw October laid out in its grid and counted forward to the twelfth. Not marked it anywhere. The twelfth was a Tuesday this year, and on her Tuesdays she walked the inland road to where it met the school road and down the hill, and at the foot of that hill the harbor was visible for approximately forty seconds of walking.

She had been carrying the date since early September, which was less like dread than like the awareness of a fence post that has shifted — not a problem yet, a thing to check on when you came to it. In October 1943 a man had washed up at the end of the breakwater after a three-day blow, and she had been seventeen and had heard about it as town rumor, and the rumor had settled into the year the way certain things settled: not gone, present in a particular way that was different from the presence of things that had been named.

She finished the near stalls and moved to the far side of the barn. The cows were in more often now, which was right for the season. From the far window she could see the icehouse, dark. Lonzo would come at eight.

---

On the Tuesday morning she walked her usual route. Cold, the road hard, the maples mostly bare, the fields gray in the early light, the inland air already different from August. She came down the school-road hill at her usual pace and at the bottom the harbor opened up on the left: the water flat and dark, the dock, the granite breakwater running two hundred yards out, the red light at the end off in the daylight.

She looked at the breakwater for three seconds while walking and then she was past the angle where it was visible and up the hill on the other side and the harbor was behind her.

She was eighteen. The birthday had come one week before the twelfth, and she found herself thinking of the two dates together even though they were not the same date and did not mark the same thing. Eighteen. Her mother had put a cake on the kitchen table without speaking, which was how Magda did everything now, and Hannah had stopped expecting anything different, and the cake had been good regardless because Magda made good cake regardless of what was happening in whatever interior space had gone quiet in her. Her father had come in from the barn and said Herzlichen Glückwunsch. She had said Danke, Vater. He had looked at her then with an expression she had been trying to place for two years: not the expression of a man who was pleased, exactly, and not the expression of a man who was displeased. Something more complicated than either, something that contained its own object and she could not identify the object.

She had answered him in German without deciding to. It had come out of her the way German came out when she was inside it — her father's accent and her father's cadence, the Bavarian vowels that Marcus had noticed in September 1943 in the classroom and had said yes to without saying more. She had been answering her father in the language he had used, which was the language of the house, which was the language inside her in the place where the house was.

He had looked at her, and then looked away, and cut the cake.

---

The prisoners had been on the Pfeiffer farm for eighteen months.

She had counted this in September when she was counting other things, and the number had come out to eighteen months without her having expected it to mean anything, and it had meant something anyway. Her parents had been in Bavaria for three years before they came to Maine — she knew this from her father's brief account, given years ago when she was eleven or twelve, the story of the village and the land prices in Maine and the crossing. Three years in Bavaria. Eighteen months in the icehouse. The arithmetic did not go anywhere useful. She turned it over once and set it aside.

Karl was twenty now. His birthday had been sometime in the summer — July, she thought, or possibly August; she had not asked and did not know. She had learned the date had passed because Fischer said something to Walther in the milking barn one afternoon in early August, something quick in German about the summer and Karl's twentieth, whether anything had been done. Nothing had been done. The farm did not observe German prisoners' birthdays and there was no reason it should, and she had been at the near stall when Fischer said it and had moved to the next stall without giving a sign that she had understood.

His English by October 1944 was serviceable in the way of anything absorbed from Lonzo Reed: practical, coastal, occasionally imprecise in ways that were Lonzo's imprecision rather than Karl's, passed through eighteen months of proximity into what Karl reached for when he needed English quickly. *That post wants setting.* *Come on, old girl, come on.* *Cold moving in, Lonzo says, from the northwest.* She had heard him say variations of each of these at various points since spring, and they were entirely accurate and entirely Lonzo's idiom in Karl's mouth. His German was still his German. When he used both languages in the same hour she could hear the seam between them, where one thing stopped and another started.

Since the barn in April — the three minutes while Lonzo was outside, the forty words in Bavarian German, the confirmation that the language between them was the language between them — their exchanges had settled into something she had not been expecting, though she had not expected anything. What it was: a sentence at the fence when Lonzo was occupied elsewhere. A question about the work or the weather, the kind of question that required an answer and no more than an answer. Wie viele Kühe? Sixteen. Dieselben wie letztes Jahr? The same sixteen as last year, yes. One year's worth of farm, one exchange at a time. The German was ordinary. The conversations were about the farm. Neither of them reached for more than the farm provided.

She had thought more than once about what word applied to what Karl was to her and had not found one that fit. This did not trouble her in the way she thought it might trouble someone who needed words for things. She was eighteen. She had decided she did not need to find the word yet, and she went on milking the cows and doing the fence work and handing Karl the extra wire staples when his supply ran short without having a word for the thing.

---

The second Saturday in October the work detail went to the north pasture's upper wall.

The wall was stone-and-mortar, her grandfather's or before her grandfather's, built sometime when walls like this were built, and it needed pointing before the ground froze or the winter would work the joints open and by spring you'd have a gap. Albrecht had told Lonzo at the start of the week. Lonzo had told the prisoners. Saturday morning: Karl and Fischer with a bucket of mortar and a trowel and a brush, Lonzo twenty feet behind with the Springfield and his bad knee, which had been troubling him since September and which he managed by finding a flat rock to sit on.

Hannah was at the lower fence on the south side of the north pasture, the wire fence that had gone slack over the summer and needed tightening before it became a problem. Her own work, not assigned. She had the wire stretcher and a spool of new wire for the section that had corroded through, and the work required attention because losing tension on a wire stretch meant starting over. She could see the upper wall from where she crouched: the stone line of it along the field's high edge, the work detail there, Karl at the near face with the trowel.

She had been at the fence forty minutes when she said something.

She was not certain afterward whether she had intended to. The wire work was going well and she was focused on it, and there was a long section of the upper wall where the mortar had failed between two large anchor stones, and Karl was packing that section carefully, the mortar pressed into the joint in the right way. Lonzo had shifted further up the field, sitting on a different rock. Fischer was around the wall's far end.

She said: Wird der Mörtel halten, wenn der Boden tief friert?

*Will the mortar hold if the ground freezes deep.*

She said it without raising her voice. She was twenty feet from the wall.

Karl looked up. He had mortar dust on his hands and on the sleeve of the jacket the prisoners wore. He looked at the section he had been working, not at her. He said: Wenn er richtig einzieht. Man muss ihn trocknen lassen.

*If it sets right. You have to let it cure.*

She said: Wie lange.

He said: Zehn Tage. Vielleicht zwei Wochen. Je nach Temperatur.

*Ten days. Maybe two weeks. Depending on the temperature.*

She looked at his work for a moment — the joint well-packed, the mortar even in the gap. He knew how to do this. He had known how to do most of the farm work from the beginning, which made sense given where he was from. She went back to the fence.

Two sentences each. The conversation was about the stone wall's mortar and the time it needed to cure before the ground froze, which was a reasonable thing to know about a wall you were repairing in October. She reached the next post and crouched to check the staples and looked up.

Her father was at the field's edge.

He was standing at the point where the north pasture's fence met the farm road — thirty feet from where she crouched, further from the wall and the work detail. He was not moving. He had his hands in the pockets of his coat and he was standing with the quality of someone who had been standing there for a while, not as if he had just arrived but as if he had been there long enough to have settled into the standing. He had come from the direction of the barn. The barn was behind him and the field was in front of him and he was standing at the boundary between.

His face was toward the field. Toward her and toward the upper wall and Karl and Fischer and Lonzo, though not settling on any one of them from what she could see at this distance. She could not read his expression at thirty feet with the October light behind him. She had been reading his face her whole life — in kitchen light, in barn light, in the particular gray light of a Maine October morning — and from this distance and in this light it was the quality of his stillness she could read rather than the expression itself.

It was the stillness of something working through him that he was not going to say. She had seen it at the kitchen radio in June when the news came in from Normandy. She had seen it in August in the yard when Karl had finished the icehouse roof cladding and Albrecht had come out and stood and looked at the work without saying whether it was good or not. That stillness: a man holding something, not yet released.

He looked at her once. She was certain of this because she was watching him and his gaze came from the wall and moved across the field and stopped at her for a moment before moving on. She did not look away. She did not know what her own face was doing. She held still in the same way he was holding still, each of them at their respective stations in the field.

Then he turned and went back toward the barn. His stride was his usual stride, deliberate, slightly favoring the right knee since early spring. He went around the corner of the barn and was gone.

She finished the section. Coiled the wire stretcher and gathered the remaining staples. On the upper wall the work detail was still going, Lonzo on his rock, Fischer visible at the wall's far end. She did not say anything else. She went in.

---

That evening Albrecht was at the icehouse window.

He was there most evenings when Lonzo had gone home and the farm was down to its nighttime arrangements. She heard it from the kitchen table: her father's voice, low and measured; then Karl's voice answering; then her father again. The German came through the icehouse wall and the kitchen wall between them at the pitch of ordinary conversation, and she could not make out the words from where she sat and had stopped trying months ago. What they said was between them. The making-out of words had never been the point, or it had not been her point, and she had accepted this at some particular moment and could not remember when.

The kitchen was where it had always been. The schoolwork in front of her was the schoolwork. The German through the walls was the German through the walls.

In October 1943 the icehouse had four German prisoners and her father spoke to Karl in the evenings and Lonzo came at eight o'clock and the cows were sixteen and the fence along the north pasture needed maintenance and the breakwater was where it was. She had been seventeen.

In October 1944 the icehouse had four German prisoners and her father spoke to Karl in the evenings and Lonzo came at eight o'clock and the cows were sixteen and the fence along the north pasture needed maintenance and the breakwater was where it was.

She was not the same person inside the list.

Her father had watched her and Karl at the fence. He had looked at her once and walked back to the barn. That evening he was at the icehouse window, as usual, and his voice came through the wall at its usual pitch. She did not know what he had seen when he looked across the field. She did not know what had been in his face at the field's edge — not anger, she thought, with reasonable confidence; not approval, which was its own complicated thing in her father's register. She could describe his face. She could describe the stillness and the hands in the coat pockets and the gaze that came to rest on her for one moment. She could not reach through the description to the thing inside it.

She closed the schoolwork and went upstairs.

The German was still coming through the walls. She did not stand on the stairs to listen. She went to her room and the question of what her father had seen, and what he was doing about it in whatever space behind his face was used for such questions, came with her, and it was still there when she lay down, and she did not reach for it again that night.
