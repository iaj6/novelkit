# At the Harbormaster's

The freight notification had arrived Wednesday on the mail boat from Rockland — a printed form clipped to the corner of his classroom door when he came in that morning, the school's copy of the bill of lading for one crate, forty-three pounds, textbooks, consigned to Coldwater Reach School, language department. He had moved it to his desk and for two days been aware of it in the way he was aware of small formal obligations that required a kind of navigation he was still learning in this place: the combination of institutional procedure and local geography that made even straightforward tasks, in a town of nine hundred people, require some advance calculation about approach.

The form required a counter-signature from the harbormaster's office before the crate could leave the dock. Friday was a mail-boat day; the boat turned around at four-thirty. He left the school at three o'clock with the notification in his breast pocket and the afternoon already going gray.

---

He recognized the preparation now, turning the freight notification in his coat pocket as he walked down from the school.

He knew who Eira Bowman was. Nine hundred people in the town, and he had been here since March of the previous year; she was the harbormaster and had been for eight years, which he had gathered not from any single conversation but from the accumulated texture of a dozen conversations that had been about other things. He had seen her from the ridge road on his morning walks — a figure in a dark peacoat, moving with purpose along the dock's planking — and had not spoken to her beyond the incidental nod of people who have recognized each other without occasion to speak. He was curious about her relation to the body on the breakwater. What he had, concretely, was what the school hallway had given him in October: *no papers, no name, Coast Guard took the log*. The removal of the log was a fact that continued to interest him, in the way that the excision of pages from a manuscript continued to interest a reader who had not been present for the excision. He had turned these two facts over for three weeks without arriving anywhere with them.

The wind at the corner of the harbor road arrived before his coat was ready for it.

At the turn where the road dropped toward the dock, the breakwater was briefly visible through the stripped maples — the granite running out two hundred yards across the inner cove, the seaward-end light not yet lit in the pale afternoon. He looked at it for the seconds the road allowed. Then the seine sheds came between, and he went on.

---

The dock in early November: the planking worn pale at the center where foot traffic ran, darker at the edges, the smell of diesel and herring bait and cold salt arriving as a fact rather than an impression, sharpened by the temperature. The mail boat — the Heron, a white-sided vessel of perhaps sixty feet — was tied at the dock's eastern end, engine silent, flag moving in the wind off the inner cove. Two men in heavy rubber-bottomed boots were transferring freight from her hold to the dock in the methodical manner of people for whom this was not a new activity. One of them gave Marcus the particular look a working dock extends to a person who is evidently not a dock person. Marcus acknowledged this without addressing it and found the harbormaster's office at the head of the dock.

He knocked.

---

The interior was smaller than the exterior suggested. A desk against the wall facing the harbor window. A filing cabinet beside it. The BC-1206 radio set on the south wall, its panel angled slightly toward the desk. A tide clock mounted above the harbor window, its face calibrated in something other than the hour — a wheel rather than a hand, reading the tidal cycle against a scale marked for this harbor specifically. The log ledger lay open on the desk, a pen laid in the binding.

Eira Bowman was at the desk and looked up without appearing to have been waiting or surprised. She was in her early fifties, he thought — harbor-worker's hands visible at the desk's edge, her manner without the performance of welcome or its opposite. The peacoat on the nail behind the door was the coat she actually wore.

He said: Dr. Klein, from the school. He had a freight notification requiring the harbormaster's counter-signature before the school could take possession of a crate.

She said yes, and extended a hand for the form.

He gave her the notification and watched her read it — both hands flat at the form's edges, oriented without needing adjustment, read with the quality of attention that indicated genuine reading. She asked was the crate for the school's general inventory or a specific department. He said it was for the language classroom. She made a note in the margin, produced the freight manifest from the filing cabinet without searching for it, placed it alongside the notification, and read both documents again before handing him a pen with the cap already removed.

He signed on the line she indicated. She countersigned below his name, returned the pen, and told him the crate was on the dock, stenciled with the school's name; the dockhands knew which one.

Four minutes, perhaps. He thanked her.

He did not leave.

---

He turned toward the harbor window and looked out at the dock. The Heron's crew had gone aboard; the dock was quiet. The afternoon was darkening from the east, the overcast settling without urgency. The fixed red light at the breakwater's seaward end was just becoming visible, not quite lit yet but distinguishable against the gray.

He said, without turning from the window: had there been any further word on the man who was found on the breakwater in October.

A pause of approximately two seconds.

The matter had been received by the Coast Guard, Eira Bowman said. The First District office in Portland held the record. There had been no identifying information that she was aware of.

She said this in precisely the register in which she had read the freight form: complete, without inflection, with the care of someone who has established what she will say and verified that what she says is accurate. He had known people who gave testimony on difficult subjects in exactly the register they used to note the hour — scholars, primarily, and certain officials he had encountered in other years in other countries — and he had always found it more informative than the alternative.

He said yes, of course. He was quiet for a moment.

He asked whether there had been any indication from the vessels that put in during the three-day blow — any connection between those arrivals and the individual who was found.

She said: no. The investigation had found no connection between the individual and any vessel that had logged a Coldwater arrival in that period.

He said yes. Of course.

He asked — his hands at his sides, looking at the harbor, in the tone of a man conducting a professional inquiry on a neutral subject — whether it would have been unusual, in that kind of weather, for someone not connected with any particular vessel to come into the dock area after dark.

There was a pause of similar length to the first.

The three-day blow before the twelfth, she said, had pushed a number of boats to seek shelter in the harbor and in the coves east of the breakwater. It was not unusual in that kind of weather for a skipper who knew the channel to come in after dark. The harbor had seen heavier traffic than normal in that period.

He said yes. Of course.

The answer was precise, complete, and had left the space around his actual question entirely intact, in the way a text can be accurate in every particular and still withhold what the reader came to find.

---

He turned from the window and gave the room the attention he brought to documents.

The tide clock above the window: specific, locally calibrated, read correctly only by someone who knew this harbor's tidal pattern. The BC-1206's panel tilted toward the desk in the way of equipment calibrated to a particular user's position over a long period of use. The log ledger open on the desk — he could see the handwriting of the current pages without reading the entries, a compressed and regular hand, and the ledger as a physical object had the accumulated weight and edge-quality of something in continuous use for years: the pages toward the binding thicker, handled more, the spine worn at its lower third where a hand would grip it to open it. If he looked at it as he looked at a manuscript — which was how, after twenty years of professional training, he looked at any document that had accumulated across time — the impression was there of a different hand, earlier in the record. Looser, wider in its letter spacing, not careless but distinct, the letterforms of someone who had learned to write in different conditions from whoever was writing now.

The change of hand in a manuscript was primary evidence.

He said nothing about it.

He said he supposed the Heron would be leaving at four-thirty and he should let her continue.

She said yes.

---

He picked up the freight manifest from the edge of the desk.

She said: that's the school's copy. You can keep it.

He looked at the manifest. Above the freight description — one crate, forty-three pounds, textbooks, Coldwater Reach School — she had written in her compressed hand: *Collected 7 November 1943, 14:10.*

The annotation was not required by the form. The crate was still on the dock; it had not yet been collected. The time was given to the minute, which was a precision the freight procedure did not call for.

He stood with the manifest looking at the line. He could not account for its purpose in any documentation chain he knew. He folded the manifest along its crease and put it into his breast pocket.

He said good afternoon. She said the same. He went out.

---

The dockhand with the school's crate was the one who had not looked at him when he arrived. He thanked the man and took the rope handle and carried the crate up the dock.

Forty-three pounds, and the rope handle was not made for carrying any distance: it cut across the fingers and there was no angle of the wrist that improved matters. He stopped at the top of the dock incline and shifted the weight to the other hand and looked back at the harbor. The Heron's engine had started; the sound of it carried up on the wind, steady and low. The seine sheds stood to the east with their weathered boards and the dark water-stain at their footings. The breakwater ran out across the cove, granite, the tide continuing to fall from it, and the red light at its seaward end had come fully on — fixed, clear, visible from five miles offshore and from the top of a dock incline in a town of nine hundred people on a Friday afternoon in November.

He picked up the crate and walked.

What she had done was not something that clarified itself slowly. It was clear by the time he reached the road. There was only the answer she gave, and around it, in the exact contour of what she had said, the silence of everything she hadn't.

He knew the skill. He had used it — in Vienna, in Paris, in other rooms in other years, in circumstances where imprecision had a cost he had not been in a position to absorb. He had not expected to encounter it here, in a working harbormaster's office on a Maine dock, in a conversation about a freight crate that had lasted seven minutes.

He had the manifest in his pocket. He had the shape of what she had not said. He had, as a matter of habit and of long practice, a tolerance for beginning from incomplete information toward a conclusion that might not arrive for some time.

The road bent at the top of the hill. The harbor went behind the seine sheds and then behind the hill's shoulder, and then it was the road up through the stripped maples and the early dark coming in from the east and the rope handle working at his fingers one by one, and he carried his books the rest of the way.
