# Across the Fence

The ground had frozen in the first week of December and stayed frozen. Not the way mud froze at night and softened by midmorning — a freeze that went deep, that changed the sound underfoot from the clinging pull of October into something brittle and fractured, the top layer of earth heaved into plates by the cold, glittering where the morning light caught it. It made the barn yard alien in a way Hannah had not expected. Eight months ago the paddock had looked like the paddock. Now it looked like something that had been broken and set back down in the shape of itself.

She noticed it every morning on the way out and she noticed it again now, coming on ten o'clock, her breath going gray in front of her, the post-driver heavy under her arm. She had known for two weeks that the southeast corner of the paddock fence had shifted. She could see it from the yard: the post leaning maybe three inches off plumb, the wire sagging with it. But there had been the south field and the window stuffing and three days where the temperature had not come above freezing even at noon. She had not gotten to it. Her father had not said anything about it. He would not. The fence was the fence and the fence required attention and she would give it attention when she could and when she did it would be done.

She hauled the post-driver out from behind the stacked straw and carried it into the paddock. The cows were still in the barn with the morning's hay going; the outer yard was empty. From around the south side of the barn she could hear the sound of splitting wood — the prisoners' work this time of year, the cold driving the field labor out and the barnyard chores in — and once in a while Lonzo Reed's voice carrying a direction in the three German words he had accumulated over six months, none of which she had confirmed for certain.

She set the driver over the post and began.

---

December had changed the work. Not only the ground — the whole farm had shifted with the season the way it always did, the way she had watched it shift every year of her life. The prisoners had been at the farm since April, which meant she had watched them go through summer and into October and now into the cold, and she had watched how the work changed and they changed with it. Less field work now. More of what the barn required: wood-splitting, repairs to the siding on the east wall, the work of keeping the icehouse warm enough and the barn functional. They had become competent at it in a way she could see from across the yard — not performing the work but doing it, a difference that was visible in the back.

Karl had picked up more English than the other three. That was clear by October and clearer now. She had not been close enough to hear much of it directly, but she had heard enough through the barn wall or at the yard's edge to know that what he was working with was a strange inheritance. Lonzo Reed's English was the English of a man who had been on lobster boats for thirty years before the war gave him this particular assignment, and what had passed from Lonzo to Karl had come with all of that still attached. She had heard Karl say something to the quiet prisoner — the one who favored his right shoulder — that had the rhythm and the clipped command of a man giving orders over engine noise, but with the consonants reassembled around a different mouth. Lonzo's vocabulary through a Bavarian throat. She had not said anything about this to anyone.

Lonzo Reed wore two coats now, a canvas work coat over what looked like his old fishing coat, and he had taken to standing with his back against the south barn wall when the wind came off the water, the wall that held afternoon warmth the longest. The Springfield rifle was on a shoulder sling that he had rigged to not interfere with the second coat. He did his work, which was standing there, and the prisoners did their work, which was everything else. He was not unkind. He was in his late fifties and the barn had a woodstove.

Hannah worked the driver down and stopped to feel the resistance. Still loose. The frost heave had pushed the post up a good two inches; she had to get it seated past where the frost had taken it or it would just come back. She lifted the driver and set it again.

---

She did not know which day it had started — Lonzo going into the barn for something in the late mornings. She had seen it happen before this, three or four times over the fall, and it did not surprise her. He was gone seven minutes, maybe ten. The barn had the stove and the stove was the warmest thing within a hundred yards and Lonzo Reed was in his late fifties. The prisoners went on with the woodpile or whatever they had when he went in, and when he came back out they were where he had left them.

So when she looked up from the post and found the yard on the far side of the fence empty of a guard, she did not make anything of it. She fitted the driver over the post again.

She heard him before she saw him — the sound of boots on frozen ground, and then the sound of two pieces of split wood being set on the fence rail. She looked up.

Karl had a piece of split wood in each hand, the way you might carry something if you were crossing toward where a person was working and wanted a reason for the crossing. He set one piece on the top rail. He kept the other.

He looked at the post. He said, in English: "The post, it is—"

He made a gesture with his free hand: palm tilting over, the motion of something going off plumb.

"Frost heave," she said. "Happens every December."

He looked at her a moment and then said it back: "*Frost heave.*" Not mechanically. More the way you repeat something to get its measure before you put it away.

"The ground freezes hard and it pushes everything up," she said. "You drive the post back down past where it was heaved to, tamp it good, it'll hold till spring."

He looked at the ground beside the post. Then he crouched down and set his piece of split wood against the post at the base, bracing it from the outside. She had not asked him to. There was not exactly a need — you could drive the post without it — but the wood would give it something to seat against on the way down, and that was not nothing.

She lifted the driver and brought it down.

He held the brace and she drove it three more times. The post went solid. She checked it with her hand — no give, not even a suggestion of one. She reached into her coat pocket for the staple gun.

He stood and held the second piece of wood and looked out across the frozen paddock while she reattached the wire: three staples, check the tension, check it again. The wire held. The fence was done.

She didn't say thank you. He didn't wait for her to say it. He was already picking up the first piece of wood from the rail when the sound of boots on the barn threshold came from behind her.

Lonzo Reed crossed the yard with his hands in his coat pockets and the rifle sling over his shoulder. His eyes went to the woodpile — the other three were there, the work going — and he came back to his place against the south wall and pulled his collar up.

Karl carried both pieces of wood back to the woodpile and set them on the split stack.

He did not hurry. He did not look back.

---

She went in at noon with the post-driver. Set it in the corner. Washed her hands at the sink and stood at the counter and made herself a plate with bread and the last of the headcheese and ate it looking at the yard through the kitchen window.

The woodpile was where it was. The prisoner detail was working. Lonzo had his back to the barn wall in the afternoon light.

It had been ninety seconds. Less. She put her plate in the sink and went to get her school books.

---

That evening she was at the kitchen table with her algebra when her father came through from the back of the house. He didn't go to the table. He went to the window.

She had seen this enough times to know it without looking up. The particular tread, the small sound of the kitchen window being worked up an inch — it gave a line of sight toward the icehouse from the east corner of the frame. She kept her pencil on the page.

From outside, after a moment, came Karl's voice: low, careful. Her father answered. It was the German he used for these conversations — she had been hearing it since August and had not confused it with any other register he had. Not the barn German, die Kühe and the short working instructions, the language of cattle and what needed doing today. Not the supper German either, the brief declaratives about the south field or the feed schedule. This was different. This had the pace of two people finding what they shared, turning it in their hands, and she could hear in her father's voice something she had not heard from him in the direction of another person in two years: a quality of attention that was present rather than elsewhere.

She worked through the algebra. Page thirty-four, the right column. The equations had a quality she did not usually think about — everything came to a result, and the result was either right or wrong, and you could check it. She moved through the column and filled in the last answer and checked it.

Her father's voice went on, a sentence and then a pause, and Karl's voice answering, the words indistinct through the window and the cold and the twenty yards between the house and the icehouse. She was not trying to hear the words. She was hearing the quality of her father's voice the way you heard the quality of a sound you had learned to read for what it meant rather than what it said: the barn sounds at four-thirty a.m., the cows telling her what they were going to need before she reached them. Her father's voice telling her something she did not have a word for yet.

She put down her pencil and looked at the page.

She had been on the same farm as Karl Reinmar since April. Eight months. Before this morning he had said perhaps eight or ten words to her total — things addressed to the work, or to Lonzo, or to the yard in general, that she happened to be in earshot of. Today he had said perhaps thirty words. Thirty-two. *The post, it is—*. The tilting hand. *Frost heave*, the way he said it back, picking it up the way he picked things up, with the attention he gave to things that were useful.

She was aware of it the way she was aware of changes in the farm's arrangements. When the morning order shifted and the quiet prisoner came out third one day instead of second she had noticed and held it and not named it. That was how the farm worked. You read it accurately and you did not reach ahead of what you could see.

She was not sure what she had been watching for in eight months. She was not sure she had been watching for anything. She had been watching Karl Reinmar the way she watched everything on this farm: with the accuracy of someone who grew up reading a complicated environment.

He had held the fence post. That was all.

The kitchen radio was off. Her mother had gone upstairs before eight. Her father's voice at the window, low and going on, the German in the dark.

She closed the algebra textbook.

The fence post would hold until spring. She had seated it past the frost line, tamped the ground around it with her heel, checked it twice. If February came in unusually hard it might heave again, but probably not. It was solid work and it would hold.

She stacked her books and carried them to the bottom of the stairs.

She went upstairs. The cedar from her mother's linen chest reached her in the hall. She went into her room and wound the alarm to four-fifteen and got into bed.

Her father's voice was still going when she closed her eyes. She couldn't hear Karl's answers from up here. She could hear her father's voice, low and present and in the language she had grown up inside, talking to the person across the wall who was not her, and then she was in the morning and the dark and the alarm doing what it always did.
