# What the Letter Said

Doris and Louise went past the desk in the current of coat-buttoning and the momentum of girls who had decided the afternoon was elsewhere. He heard Louise's laugh in the hallway — specific, uncontainable, the kind of laugh that was a fact about a person rather than a response to anything — and then the stair door, and the building's afternoon quiet.

Three forms of werden remained on the board. The future auxiliary, arranged in a column: wird, werden, werdet. He had written them with more care than the lesson warranted, the chalk letters spaced as though for a class of thirty rather than three, and he went to the board now and took the eraser.

Hannah Pfeiffer had not moved. She was at the middle desk, coat already on, her school bag on the desk before her but not yet buckled. She had stayed after before — once to ask about an irregular verb, and once for a question about the subjunctive in indirect questions that had lengthened into the most useful grammatical conversation he had managed in Coldwater — and she had always had a reason when she stayed, which was not universally true of students who lingered.

He tapped the eraser against the chalk rail and set it down. He went to his desk and opened his lesson folder.

She said: "There's something I don't entirely understand."

He said: "In what?"

She opened not her German grammar but her English literature notebook — the one he had seen her annotating through several weeks of Latin period, which was not his — and she took from between the pages a folded sheet of paper and set it on the front edge of her desk. "German," she said. "A phrase I follow partly."

He came around the desk to look.

---

He picked up the page. The wear at its folds registered before anything else — the paper softened there, the surface slightly fibrous from being taken out and refolded many times.

He had not asked where it came from. He would not ask.

A woman's hand. He understood this from the first line, and then from the particular use of wegen in the subordinate clause — Bavarian, or near enough; a formation that standard written German handled differently, a regional habit — and then from the letterforms themselves: the closing loops, the formation of the capitals, a schoolroom standard drilled in and retained without effort for forty or fifty years. He had been identifying regional written hands for twenty years. This one was not difficult.

He read from the beginning. She had asked about a phrase; he could not read a phrase. The letters on either side of it mattered for what it meant, and the letter's occasion mattered, and the whole domestic organization surrounding whatever had to be said mattered as much as what was said.

A neighbor's fence, replaced in autumn. Some difficulty with the materials, but holding now, better than the old one. Potato prices up. A cousin — Els — who had married in October: a small gathering, family present, weather better than October usually offered. The writer hadn't been able to attend but had sent something with Els's sister. The cellar better than last winter. A dog — the name blurred in the cursive — sick in December, something in the stomach, but recovered and back to his old habits.

He had been reading letters like this all his life — the domestic inventory that organized a world by its surfaces because the surfaces were what held. You said the fence, you said the cousin, you said the dog, and in saying them you maintained the world at its edges, kept the frame. He had received letters like this from Vienna, from Paris, from friends in places he would never return to. The inventory was a kind of loyalty: I am telling you the fence and the cousin and the dog because these things are still here. And sometimes, embedded in the inventory, between a wedding and firewood —

Die die versucht haben zu gehen, sind weg. Wir beten für sie.

He read the sentence. He read it again. He continued to the end of the page, which closed in the middle of a paragraph about the cellar and ended there, no signature.

He looked up.

She had underlined die versucht haben zu gehen. He had read through the underlining without registering it.

He folded the page on its existing creases and set it on the edge of the desk between them.

She said: "Versucht — tried. And zu gehen — to go. But I don't have the whole phrase."

He said: "Those who tried to go are gone."

She was very quiet.

"Wir beten für sie," he said. "We pray for them."

"People who left Germany?" she said.

"People who tried to leave. Germany, or Austria, or Czechoslovakia. In the years before the war made it impossible — 1939, 1940, 1941 — there were people who attempted to emigrate and did not succeed. Who started and were stopped. Or who started and were not heard from again." The coal furnace behind the wall made its sound — something in the metal, a faint contraction against the February cold. "Some of the people who tried to go arrived in places like this. Some of them didn't arrive anywhere."

She said: "She knows particular people."

"Yes. The double relative — die die — gives you a specific group. A real group she knows. The names aren't in the sentence. Just the category: those who tried." He looked at the line. "The placement tells you the rest. Between a cousin's October wedding and the firewood. Past tense — the past perfective. This is something she knows and is passing on. It is already over. The prayer confirms it."

Hannah looked at her hands for a moment. Then: "And the people who tried to go — they were from the same community?"

"From her community. Yes." He paused. "She writes tried, not went. Not left. They attempted and did not complete. That is what the sentence contains."

She sat with it. She had an adolescent's capacity for absorbing a sentence without immediately producing a response to it, which he had noticed before in this room.

He did not say: I can date this letter to autumn 1943. A letter that mentions an October cousin's wedding and the onset of winter was written in late 1943, which meant that the people from their community who had tried to go in '39 or '40 or '41 had been gone for two years or more before she wrote this sentence, and the writing of it was not news but acknowledgment — telling her son in his POW camp in America what had become of the world he had come from. He did not say: the header is missing from this page, and I know what it means when a header is missing, and it tells me something about the path this page traveled to reach this room. He did not say: Yarmouth. He did not say the name from Feld's letter — the German-Jewish philologist, Leipzig, last documented Yarmouth Nova Scotia, September 1943 — and he did not say the coast of Maine between Yarmouth and Boston, and he did not say October.

He was close to saying these things to himself.

He handed the letter back to her.

---

She took it. She held it without immediately folding it, looking at the underlined phrase.

Then she looked up at him with the face that was not her classroom face. The classroom face was attentive and careful and gave nothing away. The other one, which appeared less often, was a girl thinking something and deciding whether to say it.

She said: "Were you one of them? The ones who tried to go?"

He was quiet for a moment.

"I am one of the ones who arrived," he said.

She received that.

"Vienna," he said. "Then Paris. London. Then Canada. Then here."

"Canada," she said.

"Briefly."

She did not ask about briefly. He had said the word with the register of a door that is shut, and she heard the register and left the door where it was. She had developed this particular skill somewhere. He knew where: the same place a girl would develop the skill of hearing her father through a floor, of reading the difference between a word that led somewhere and a word that was simply present in the room.

He said: "The leaving is not the hard part."

He had not intended to say this. He had known it was true for five or six years and had kept it in the notebook rather than giving it to rooms.

She looked at him.

"What is?" she said.

"The arriving," he said.

Which was a simplified version of the truth, close enough to the truth for a classroom in February, and as close as he had managed in language so far — the fuller version remained in the notebook, where he had been working on it since approximately Paris, and he had not yet found the sentence.

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said: "Yes."

Not I understand or I see. Just yes. Which was the right word, because it didn't claim more than she had.

She put the letter back between the annotation pages in the English literature notebook, closed it, buckled her school bag, and stood.

He said: "Good afternoon, Miss Pfeiffer."

She said: "Good afternoon." Then, at the door: "Thank you, Dr. Klein."

The door. The hallway. The stair door below. The building settling around its quiet.

---

He sat down at his desk.

The classroom arranged itself without him: the erased board, the three desks at their slight angles from the day's use, the chalk dust in the late February light. The coal furnace pressing its inadequate heat against the cold of the room, which absorbed it and remained cold. The east windows held February — condensation on the oldest frames, where the glass was thinnest and the fit loosest, the pines on the upper hill with their snow, the road below going toward four o'clock's early gray.

He reached for the letter from the edge of his desk and then stopped, because the letter was not there. She had taken it in her bag.

He put his hand flat on the surface.

Die die versucht haben zu gehen, sind weg. He held the construction. In German — in the Bavarian formation, specifically — the double relative enacted the group before naming what happened to the group. Who they were first, before the fact of their going. The past perfective: had tried. Had been in the act of trying, had continued trying, had completed the trying, and it had not been enough. Sind weg: are gone. Not fled, not departed. Gone. Three words. Bavarian restraint doing the work of whatever the full account would require.

He thought about the woman who had written it. At a kitchen table somewhere, in a village whose letterforms he could place to within thirty or forty kilometers on the evidence of her handwriting — a village that lay within fifty kilometers of where a girl in his German class had grown up hearing her father's language, the dialect this woman wrote in, the register Hannah Pfeiffer's ear had been trained to by means she had never chosen. The world had a size. He had been learning this size since 1938, the same lesson in different rooms, in different languages, arriving each time as if for the first time. A mother at a table in Bavaria writing to her son in a POW icehouse in Maine, and the sentence she put between the cousin's wedding and the firewood, and the page separated from its header traveling from that table to this desk, and the girl who brought it to him, who carried in her speech the same dialect the letter was written in without knowing it — or without having said it.

He had told her the truth. What the phrase contained, what the construction meant, what kind of people the prayer was for. He had told her as much as was his to give from the letter she had given him, which was not the same as everything he had heard.

He held these things alongside each other and did not yet move them into sequence. They were adjacencies: real, probably connected, not yet proven. He had a habit of not closing adjacencies too quickly, a habit he had developed in the years when closing them too quickly had led to bad decisions in train stations and border offices. You looked at what you had; you wrote it down; you saw what it was.

Tonight he would write something in the notebook. He would look at what it looked like written down and decide from there.

He picked up his lesson folder and put it in his bag. His hat from the chair beside the desk. One of the student desks had drifted slightly from its usual position — the middle one, where she sat — and he moved it back, more carefully than necessary.

The world was a particular size. He had been learning this for six years.

He went out and closed the door.
