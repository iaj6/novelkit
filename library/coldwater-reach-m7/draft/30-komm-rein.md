# Komm Rein

The barn in late April was the same barn it had been in every October of the last two years and every month between them: the same smell of hay and cattle and morning damp in the boards, the same gray light through the high eastern windows coming in at an angle that moved six inches across the floor between the first milking and the second, the same sounds of the cows pulling at the water troughs and shifting in the stanchions before full light. Outside it was April, the field mud beginning to firm and the maples at the south bend of the road putting on leaves, but in here it was the same barn it had been in October 1943 when the icehouse was new and the arrangement was new and everything about it was new. Two years and the barn had absorbed all of it into its ordinary sounds. There were four mornings left.

The spring light on the hay in the loft was different from November — not the blue-white of January, not the flat gray of mud season, but a yellow that reached the far wall and made the dust visible in it. The smell of the barn in April was the smell of a winter's enclosure not yet fully aired: the cows inside all winter, the spring cleaning not done, the high windows still latched against the last cold.

The morning routine had been what it always was: the six o'clock milking, the clean-up, the first feeding, the water troughs. She had done these things alongside the four prisoners and Lonzo Reed for twenty-four months and they had all learned the barn's particular sequence so well that nobody had to say which task came next; you simply arrived at the next thing and did it. This morning was the same, except that it was the last one.

She gave Karl the letter on the morning before the truck was due.

Lonzo Reed stepped outside through the side door for his cigarette at the same point in the early routine he always did — for the cigarette, or because twenty-four months of the same hours had made one minute out of the barn's particular air necessary. The door shut and she heard his boots on the gravel and then nothing.

She had carried the letter in her coat pocket since March, since Karl told her through the fence that repatriation would begin in weeks. She had known since then what she was going to do with it and had not done it, and now there was one morning left.

She crossed the barn to where Karl was at the far stanchion.

She said: Ich habe das gefunden. In der Scheune. Im Februar, letztes Jahr.

She held out the envelope. He looked at it before he took it — the look you give a thing you had stopped expecting to see again. The paper had gone soft at the crease lines from handling, and the upper left corner where the return address had been was stained pale brown from the hay it had lain in. His mother's name on the front in the postal officer's hand. He took the envelope from her and turned it over once.

He said: Ich hab mich gefragt, wo es hingegangen ist.

She said: Es tut mir leid.

He said: Nein.

He held the envelope a moment without opening it. Then: Du konntest es lesen.

She said: Ja.

He nodded once and put the letter in the inside pocket of his jacket, the pocket over his chest, and pressed it flat with his palm.

He said: Deine Mutter — beginning the sentence slowly, feeling for the path through the words. He stopped there.

She said: Wir beten für sie.

He looked at her. His face was the face it had been for twenty-four months — different from October 1943 in the ways that farm work and two winters make a face different, the jaw steadier, the skin rougher, the eyes accustomed. He said: Ich glaube, es geht ihr gut. Ich hoffe es.

She said: I hope so too.

He turned back to the stanchion. Lonzo's boots sounded on the gravel outside and the side door opened and the barn returned to itself.

---

The truck came at mid-morning. She heard the engine on the road before it turned in through the gate — the camp administration truck from Houlton, olive drab, the same model that had come with supply deliveries occasionally in the past two years. Two camp soldiers got down from the cab without hurry, one carrying a brown folder with the transfer paperwork.

Lonzo Reed was in the yard with his Springfield at his side and his winter coat on, the collar turned up against the April wind — the same coat he had worn all winter, the loose button on the left front still loose the way it had been since January. He walked over and took the folder and read the paperwork the way he read all paperwork: steadily, without hurry, nodding at the relevant parts. He signed with the pencil stub from his shirt pocket and handed the folder back.

The four prisoners came out of the icehouse with their things. There was not much — twenty-four months had accumulated some quantity of objects for each man but not much: a bag or a folded parcel, some letters, whatever the camp stores had issued over two winters. One of the three men Hannah had never learned to say right carried a folded blanket under his arm. Karl came out third. He had his jacket buttoned, his bag in one hand, and the inside pocket of his jacket lay flat against his chest where the letter was.

When they came out he did not look toward the porch.

Then, once, he did. She looked back. His face did not change and hers did not either, and then his head turned to follow the motion of the tailgate going down, and that was all. The four men loaded into the truck bed. The tailgate went up.

Lonzo shook hands with one of the camp soldiers — brief, two pumps, the handshake of strangers finishing the same piece of work. He stepped back to the yard's edge. The second soldier got in the cab. The engine turned over.

---

She was on the porch. She did not remember going up the steps. Her father was beside her at the railing, both hands around his coffee cup, already there the way he was sometimes simply there when something was starting in the yard without him. She had not heard him come out. Her mother had not come to the door.

They stood at the rail and watched the truck maneuver. The yard was not large and the turn was tight for the loaded truck and the engine labored while the front wheels found their angle. Beside her her father was breathing — she could hear him in the quiet of the yard — and he had not spoken since they came to the porch. He stood the way he stood at the icehouse window in the evenings, or at the edge of the south field when the weather was coming in from the northwest, weight settled into his feet, watching something he had no way to change.

The truck found the lane and moved forward. It went slowly down toward the road, the ruts filling with pale water behind the tires as the weight passed over them. At the end of the lane it turned south.

It went past the fence line along the south field. Past the heaved second post, the staple she had driven while Karl held the wire. Into the maple stand at the south bend and gone.

She counted without intending to. Under thirty seconds and the engine sound was gone south too, toward the highway, toward Houlton.

Lonzo Reed was still in the yard. He stood looking at where the truck had been, the Springfield at his side, his coat hem loose in the wind. Then he looked toward the porch. He lifted his chin once and turned and walked down the lane. He went around the south bend.

The yard was empty.

Her father had not moved beside her. The tire tracks of the truck were filling slowly with the pale water that rose through this clay when you pressed it. The icehouse door was standing open at the back of the yard — left open when the four men came out and no one had gone back to close it. She could see the dark opening from the porch, the empty room inside, the rough walls the renovation had put up two years ago. The first time the icehouse had stood empty since April of 1943. The door open to the cold April air for the first time in two years.

From inside the barn came the ordinary sounds of the cows in the stanchions, indifferent to the hour. Forty-five cows. The south field across the fence, too soft to turn yet — another week, maybe two. The mud of the yard going still in the mid-morning light.

---

Her father put his hand on her shoulder.

The weight of his hand was specific — the farmer's hand, wide and dry and heavy, the hand that had been milking cows and turning hay and holding fence posts on this farm for twenty-two years. She could feel it through the wool of her coat. He did not grip or press. He set his hand on her shoulder the way you set something down carefully on a surface you want to stay level.

He said two words. She heard them in both languages he had. Komm rein.

She looked at the south bend of the road where the maple stand closed over it. The leaves were not yet full — late April and the canopy still thin enough for the light to come through in pieces. The road was empty.

Then she turned.

---

The kitchen was warm. The stove was going with the draft set low for the middle of the day, the room holding the warmth of the morning's bread and the coffee already on the table in three cups. Magda was at the stove with her back to them, setting the pot back on the burner. She did not turn around.

Hannah sat down.

Her father sat across from her.

The table held what it always held: the cups, the pot, the sugar bowl with the chip on the rim that had been there as long as Hannah could remember, the dish her mother set the stirring spoon on. The window over the counter with the view of the barn roof and past it the south field and the maple stand at the south bend where the road disappeared.

She put her hands around her cup and let the warmth come through. The coffee was what it always was. Through the window the late-morning light was the thin April light that made everything look like it could become something else in a week, the fields not yet what they would be and already past what they had been.

The radio on the counter was off. She had not turned it on this morning and her father had not turned it on and the kitchen held its own quiet — not the silence of the last two years, not her mother's closed silence and her father's German and the icehouse full at the back of the yard, but the ordinary quiet of a room with the stove going and the coffee on the table. In the radio, if she turned it on, she would find the war in the quality it had had all spring: the careful evenness of the announcer's voice, the names of places in Germany spoken in a changed tense, the armies moving through a country that was finishing itself place by place. She could reach out and turn the dial.

She did not.

Her mother came from the stove and set the pot on the table. She put her hand flat on the surface once — the gesture Hannah had been reading for two years. Then she sat down.

The three of them were at the table.

Her father drank his coffee. Her mother held her cup with both hands. Outside the window the barn roof was the same barn roof it had always been. The south field would tell her when it was ready — she would look at it this afternoon and the soil would give her what she needed to know. The icehouse door was still open at the back of the yard; she could not see it from this window but she knew it was open, the darkness inside it now the darkness of a room no longer in use.

The man on the breakwater would stay in the supplementary file in Portland, without a name.

Somewhere on a road south, the truck.

Her father set his cup down. Her mother refilled it from the pot, without being asked, without looking up. The farm was what it was and required what it required: the south field in another week, the forty-five cows waiting for the afternoon milking, the spring work ahead of all of it. The icehouse empty.

The war was in the radio. It was not yet over. It would be.

The farm was here.
