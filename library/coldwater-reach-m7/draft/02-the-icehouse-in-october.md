# The Icehouse in October

The alarm was the four-fifteen, which she had moved back in September because the dark came earlier now and earlier meant harder. When it went she lay for a moment adding up the warmth she had against the warmth she would need: the cold of the kitchen floor, the cold of the yard, the barn and its better warmth that smelled of forty-five cows and hay and the steam of animals who had been working through their own slow processes since midnight. She added these in order and then she got up.

Her boots were by the door, laces already loosened from the night before. She dressed in the kitchen without lighting a lamp — she knew every nail and hook in her own kitchen in the dark — and took the lantern off the shelf and went out.

The yard in October. The dark was total until the lantern made it particular: the pump handle, the corner of the silo, the low fence of the kitchen garden with its dead stalks. The hayfield to the east gave off cold in a way the harbor-cold didn't, a cold from the earth rather than the water, dense and close to the ground. She was halfway to the barn before she could hear the cows clearly over the wind. Forty-five animals telling her what she already knew.

Inside she hung the lantern on the iron hook by the door and the light went orange in the warmth. The smell hit before the warmth did: hay and manure, the sweet-sharp smell of milk, the wet-wood smell of the stalls that had been wet since October and wouldn't dry until May. Above everything the animal steam, the breath and bodies of forty-five cows giving off heat into an enclosed space. She had breathed this smell every morning of her life.

She started at the near end. Heide shifted left when she heard the bucket and had to be corrected — not hard, just redirected. Brunhild was easy. The red one they called Rote was easy. She worked down the stalls in the order her father had taught her when she was small enough that the cows had seemed very large, and her hands did it while the rest of her was still waking up, and the barn was warm and loud in the particular way of barns at four-thirty in the morning, which is not warm the way a room is warm, not loud the way a room is loud, but warm and loud in the way of forty-five large animals being specific about what they needed.

She was on the third bucket when the far door opened and her father came in.

He went directly to the east side, which was his, and he spoke to the first stall cow without stopping — die Kühe and their names and what he needed them to do, in the barn German that was older than the other German and didn't belong to the war. She had heard it her whole life. It was a different instrument than the German he used in the kitchen now, or the German she heard in the evenings through the wall. The barn German was working language, specific, not addressed to anyone she could hear.

He came along the aisle behind her toward the center where their rows met. She was reaching forward to adjust the bucket when he passed.

He said: Morgen.

He said it the way he had always said *good morning* — flatly, without looking up, a fact already established that required only acknowledgment. He had stopped saying *good morning* in the kitchen, at the table, when he stopped speaking English at home, but in the barn he had kept *good morning* and then at some point it became *Morgen*. She was not certain when. She hadn't been paying close enough attention to mark the exact day.

She said: Morgen.

She had started saying it back in August. She hadn't decided to. It was simply the response that required the least choosing, when the alternative was standing on the English side of it and making something of that. He had always said good morning; she had always said good morning back.

He moved on without responding to her response, which she hadn't expected. Beyond her, toward his side, and his German came back in fragments down the length of the stalls — a cow's name, an instruction. The cows responded in their own register, which was not interested in the language being used.

---

At half past eight she heard the truck.

Lonzo Reed came through the gate with his Springfield over his right shoulder and his collar turned up against a cold that was October cold, not yet November cold, but Lonzo addressed all inconveniences at the level of their theoretical maximum. He had been a fisherman before the war and he moved with a fisherman's deliberateness — not slow, simply finished with haste as a method. He walked to the icehouse, knocked twice on the door, and pulled the latch.

The four men came out.

There was no drama in it. They had done this every morning since April: they emerged, they put their coats on in the yard, they separated to their assigned tasks without conference or complaint. Ernst — she thought his name was Ernst, she'd heard it once in June and had not confirmed it — came first. Then the heavyset one whose name she had never caught. Then the quiet one who favored his right shoulder but never mentioned it to Lonzo, because mentioning it to Lonzo would have required language neither of them had. Karl came last.

He came out pulling his jacket sleeve over his right wrist and saying something to the heavyset one in German that made the heavyset one tilt his head slightly. Then he saw that Lonzo was already making his perimeter check and he stopped saying it and looked at what needed doing.

He was nineteen. Lonzo had said so in July, in the tone of a man grateful the situation wasn't worse: *the youngest one's just nineteen.* As if the age were something in his favor in a reckoning Hannah had not been asked to make. Karl was from somewhere in Bavaria, fifty kilometers from where her father was born, which she had thought about more than once since they arrived in April.

He moved the way boys moved — not clumsily but with something in reserve, energy not yet fully organized into efficiency. She noticed this the way she noticed whether the cows were favoring one side or the other: not as a judgment, as a reading.

Lonzo said his three German words and the four men spread to their tasks.

Karl went to the woodpile. He picked up the maul and turned it once in his hands before he set the first round on the block — this was always his habit, measuring first, then splitting. She'd watched it enough times to know. He'd been good at it since June and was better now. The quarter-round came off clean on the first stroke, the log falling to both sides evenly, and she turned away before he could notice her watching.

In front of Lonzo, Karl and her father were simply two people in the same yard. They didn't look at each other any differently than either of them looked at the fence or the barn.

Lonzo stamped his feet and watched his breath go white and disperse. The morning kept on being the morning.

---

The feed store closed at five on Wednesdays. She took the Ford after school let out and drove the three miles of unpaved inland road — the truck pulling right the way it always did, the heater requiring the knob held at an angle slightly left of center, the Bangor station coming in on the radio with enough static that she kept it on anyway because it was better than the absence of it.

From the ridge above town the harbor was visible for a moment through the October haze: a strip of gray water, the breakwater a thin dark line extending from the dock, the fixed red light at its seaward end. She didn't look at it for longer than the road allowed.

She had heard it before she saw the water. Ellis Carver, in the hall outside Klein's classroom Tuesday morning: a man on the breakwater, the Coast Guard there and gone by ten, no one from Coldwater missing, no name and no papers and Reg Farber's youngest had found him before breakfast. She'd heard it again at lunch, from two other girls, with different details. She'd heard it from the Dunham boy in Latin class, who'd added additional details that contradicted Ellis Carver's details without either of them seeming to notice.

The general store was also the post office, which meant it was where information arrived without anyone needing to carry it. Mabel Trask was behind the counter and the Morrissette brothers were in the seed display for reasons that had nothing to do with seeds. They were there because Mabel knew.

What Mabel knew: not a fisherman, not anyone from around here. The Farber boy had thought he was sleeping. He was not sleeping. The Coast Guard took the body and took the log pages with it, which Mabel found notable and said so. The man had no identification. No one was looking for anyone from Coldwater.

The Morrissette brothers added commentary about a man they had once seen in Rockland who had struck them as the type, though they acknowledged it might not be the same man. Hannah listened. She bought her four bags of dairy meal — the good kind, not the cut kind, because the cows had been producing well and her father's rule was that good cows got good meal — and she heaved them over the tailgate one at a time with her left hip against the lip, the method her father had taught her, which left the small of the back out of it. Four bags, four thumps. She tied the tailgate and drove home.

The heater knob at its angle. The radio in fragments. The man was in her mind — not sharply, just present — his absence of name, the city shoes that Mabel had mentioned in passing. She had seen men in city shoes in Rockland, men who had not dressed for the coast. The shoes alone said something.

She drove the inland road with the harbor behind her and the farmland opening up on both sides, the shadow of the silo already stretched halfway across the yard when she turned up the farm road. The light had gone orange on the eastern fields. The cows would be wanting their evening feed.

---

After supper she heard her father's voice through the kitchen wall.

The east side of the house shared its partition with the narrow yard hallway, and from the window above the dry sink she could hear things in the yard that would not be audible from anywhere else in the house. She had found this out by accident years ago and had used it since.

Magda was at the table with the hip seam in Albrecht's work pants. The right hip seam opened every October when he pulled the heavier work from the trunk — it had opened so reliably for so many years that she thought of it as a fall event, like the first hard frost, like the leaves off the maple by the kitchen door. Her mother's needle moved at the pace she worked at everything: exactly as fast as the thing required. She had not spoken in six months. The doctor in Rockland had a word for it that Hannah had not written down, a long word, functional at the beginning, meaning: not injury, not illness, but something the body had decided. Hannah's own theory was different and she kept it to herself.

The kitchen was quiet enough that she heard her father's voice through the wall — not the words, just the low movement of it — and she went to the window.

The icehouse window was lit. The kerosene lamp, which Lonzo had authorized in June when the wood stove started drawing properly: yellow and steady from outside, the window cracked its two or three inches that she had come to associate with the evenings since August. Her father stood in the yard with his back to her. She could see his work coat, the white of his breath.

Karl's voice came through the window. He was asking something. Then her father's voice: lower, going on past where a simple answer would have stopped.

She had never been close enough in the evenings to hear the words, and she had not gone closer on purpose. What she could hear was the quality of it — the rhythm of two people talking about something that warranted the talking. The barn German was not this. The barn German was a tool, simple, used for specific animals in a specific order. This moved through the air differently. It had the cadence of things being described or asked about in earnest, of a question that expected a real answer.

Her father's shoulders in his work coat were not the shoulders she had been watching for two years. The particular set they'd had — since the telegram, she thought, or before the telegram, since December 1941 when the radio news came in and he had sat very still for a long time and she had been sixteen and had not known what she was seeing — that set was not there now. He was not relaxed; he was standing in the cold at an icehouse window and relaxed was not the situation. Present in one place, not carrying the elsewhere that had been in him for so long she had almost forgotten what his shoulders looked like without it.

The lamp inside shifted — someone moving near it — and the window brightened briefly and settled. Her father said something. Karl answered.

She stepped back from the window.

Her mother's needle moved in the hip seam. In the kitchen the only sound was the creak of the chair and the pull of thread and her own breathing, which she had not noticed until now.

---

She went upstairs.

The man on the breakwater was still with her — no name, city shoes, not from anywhere local.

Underneath the man on the breakwater was the sound of her father's voice at the window. The length of it. Karl on the other side of the glass, Karl's question receiving an answer that was longer than the question. She did not know what they had been talking about. She had not decided to try to find out.

The house made its sounds around her — the pipes settling, the boards in the corner of her room that spoke once every night, the cold working at the seams of the window glass. She lay in the dark and the dark of thinking and the dark before sleep had no clear boundary between them, and eventually she was on the far side of that boundary without having crossed it.
