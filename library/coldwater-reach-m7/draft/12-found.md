# Found

The cows moved before she got the barn door open — a shifting, the soft thud of hooves on packed earth, a low sound one of them made to another in the dark. She had been hearing this for six years, since the first winter she was old enough to do the milking alone, and she still stopped for a moment when it happened: the sound of the herd gathering itself in the dark, the way they knew someone was at the door before the door opened.

February cold had drawn itself into the boards overnight. Her breath showed when she stepped through, and then the cows' breath showed too, a white fog rising from the near stalls in the lantern light, and she stood a moment in the smell of it — wet hay and grain, the close animal heat, the February cold that lived at shin level and in the beam joints and did not warm off entirely even when the herd had been standing in it for hours. The lantern made a small yellow circle on the earthen floor. Above it, darkness.

Four-thirty. She went to work.

Her section was the six Guernseys on the near side. She had been milking them, or cows like them, since she was eleven and her hands knew the work without asking her permission. The one who kicked left. The two who leaned into the pail. The last one, who needed settling before she would let you near her, who had been that way for two years and would probably always be that way. Hannah moved through them in the order she always moved through them, unhurried, the pail finding its place, her fingers working.

Her father was in the far stalls with his Holsteins, and she could hear the rhythm of his milking through the divider — pull and ring, pull and ring — as steady as it had been for as long as she had known what milking was. They had not spoken during morning milking in as long as she could remember. The barn had its own kind of quiet and they both kept it. This had been true before his English went away, which made it a barn thing and not a silence thing.

February dark outside the barn door was total. She had been standing on the back step before she came down, her coat half-buttoned, and the hill above the property had been just a darker shape against a dark sky and the fields were nothing at all. July dark wanted something from you; it had insects in it, motion, the rustling of things that had business to do. February dark had no business. It settled in and waited.

She finished her third Guernsey, moved to the fourth.

Somewhere around five, the wind came over the roof peak and she heard a nail pull free — a ping against one of the far boards — and she looked over at the dark end of the barn where the sound had been, and couldn't see anything that had changed. She would check it in daylight. There was always something in February that needed checking in daylight.

---

At six, Lonzo Reed came with the four men.

She heard the keys first, always. Then the footsteps crossing the barnyard, the particular sound of four people being walked somewhere at six in the morning in cold they had not chosen. Then the drag of the door against the ice that had been building at the sill for ten days. Lonzo came through first with the Springfield, and then Werner, and Gerd, and the quiet one whose name she could never remember even though she heard it every morning — some difficulty between the German and the Maine of it — and then Karl last.

Lonzo had fallen in the first week of the month. He'd gone down hard on the icy patch near the barn door, the Springfield striking the ground before he did, and after that he walked from the icehouse to the barn with the deliberateness of an old man testing ice, each step settled before the next. He was not old, exactly, but he walked old on ice now.

The prisoners had not fallen. Karl had learned to read the ice sometime in January; she had noticed it: the widening of his step on the gray patches, the way his weight shifted back. He was nineteen and his body took what the ground taught it quickly. The other three were older and slower to adjust.

She did not look up when they came in.

The February arrangement was the same as January: the prisoners took the far stalls for mucking and for the cleanup work while she and her father managed the milking, and then when the Guernseys were done Karl and Werner carried the cans to the cooling tank. Her father worked the Holstein end. Lonzo Reed stood near the center beam with his rifle and his cold, stamping his feet occasionally on the earthen floor.

She finished her fifth Guernsey and moved to the last.

Karl came across the barn with a can in each hand, moving at the pace she had watched him find over the winter — not rushing, not dawdling, the workman's pace that got things done. He was aimed for the door. The icy patch near the sill was the same patch Lonzo had gone down on: the temperature dropped overnight and the melt off their boots refroze by morning and the ash she spread kept being absorbed into the gray surface and needing spreading again. She saw Karl hit the edge of it. His right foot went sideways, the can in his left hand knocked outward, and he caught himself in a single half-step — no drama in it, the body's fast knowledge — and set his feet right and kept going through the door.

The right can sloshed. He had not dropped it.

She was back at the last Guernsey, moving the pail, when she saw the paper on the floor.

---

It lay near the icy patch, close to where his foot had turned. A folded page, doubled twice. She could see from where she stood the wear at the folds — the paper darkened there, soft with handling, the kind of wear that came from something carried and taken out and folded back again, many times.

She looked at the door. It was closed.

She went to the page and picked it up.

There was a wooden ledge beside the door post, where people set things down when their hands were full — a glove, a harness strap. She could put the page there, where Karl would see it when he came back for the second pass on the cans. She held the page.

She looked at the far stalls. Werner and the others working, the sound of it regular. Her father's voice, once, in German, to one of his Holsteins. Lonzo Reed had gone out with Karl.

She unfolded the page.

A woman's handwriting — that was what she understood first, before the language sorted itself. Careful, narrow, slightly formal, the kind of handwriting that was tidy from intention and not from ease. The kind of handwriting that had been drilled into a girl and had stayed. The page had been folded and unfolded enough times that the creases held their shape even flat.

German. She understood this a moment after the handwriting, the way you caught the voice before you caught the words. And then, inside the German: Bavarian. Her ear for it was her father's ear — she had not learned it, had not been taught it, had absorbed it the way she had absorbed the sound of cows at four-thirty, from before she was old enough to know what learning was. This was not Dr. Klein's classroom German and not the German she had found in books. It was the German of home, some home she had never seen, the same dialect she had been hearing through the floor and through the icehouse window since April.

She read.

A neighbor's fence, replaced that autumn. Some difficulty with the materials, a delay, but it was finished and holding now, better than the old one. Potatoes more expensive than last year, as everything was. A cousin — her name appeared to be Els — had married in October: a small gathering, quietly done, the family had been there, the weather better than you would expect for October. The writer hadn't been able to come but had sent something with Els's sister. The cellar was holding better this winter than the winter before. The dog — a name she couldn't parse from the cursive — had been sick in December, something in the stomach, but had come back to himself; he was back to his old habits.

She read slowly.

Then, at the end of the paragraph about the cousin's wedding, this:

Die die versucht haben zu gehen, sind weg. Wir beten für sie.

She read it again.

Those who tried to go are gone. We pray for them.

The sentence was between a line about the October weather and a line about the cellar and it did not explain itself. The writer moved on after it to something about firewood. The war was not named in the letter, not once in the whole page, and yet versucht haben zu gehen was not a sentence about the cellar. Sind weg was not the dog being sick in December.

She didn't know who the people were. She had enough German to know that the sentence was specific — a real attempt, past tense, over; particular people this woman knew; a prayer for them by name, or rather for them in the place of their names, which were not given. She did not know where they had tried to go or what had become of them when they went.

The door scraped.

She folded the page on its creases and put it in her coat pocket and went back to the last Guernsey.

---

Karl came in again with Werner for the second pass on the cans. Lonzo Reed came through behind them, saying something about the ash and whether she had more of it. She told him where the bucket was.

She finished the last cow and carried her pail to the cooling tank. Her father came out of the far stalls and they moved the last of the milk across the barnyard to the cellar, two trips, without speaking. The cold that early in February cut straight down through the collar. The sky above the hill was just starting to separate itself from the darkness, gray coming up from the east.

She heard Lonzo lock the barn behind them.

---

That afternoon she sat at the kitchen table with her school notebook open.

She took the letter out of her coat pocket and unfolded it.

The handwriting first, again. Careful and narrow. In the kitchen light — the wood stove going, the gray February afternoon outside the window above the sink — the handwriting looked like someone who had sat down to write this with an intention to do it well, to send her son something careful. The page had been read smooth. He had been carrying it in his jacket pocket since whenever it arrived, October maybe or November, through the ice and the cold and the early mornings and the icy patch near the barn door, through whatever November and December and January were like in an icehouse in Maine.

She read it from the beginning.

The fence. The potatoes. Cousin Els. The cellar. The dog.

Die die versucht haben zu gehen, sind weg. Wir beten für sie.

She sat with it.

She could ask Dr. Klein. She had thought of him by name in the barn. He was the only person in Coldwater who would know both the German and enough of the world beyond Coldwater to know what a sentence like that was the end of. But asking Dr. Klein meant taking the letter out of her pocket in front of someone, meant explaining where it had come from, meant saying what she had done with it when she found it.

She had not decided to do that yet.

She put the letter between the pages of her English literature notes — a Whitman passage she had been annotating, the words barely visible through the folded page — and closed the notebook and set it in her school bag.

The fire in the stove was going. She could hear her mother in the back room moving about, the particular footstep that said she was sweeping, and outside the February light doing what it always did at three-thirty, which was give up.

She pulled her arithmetic toward her and began.
