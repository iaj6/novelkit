# The Fragment

The note was waiting for him at the school office on Tuesday morning, the first Tuesday of January, slipped through the door grid of his mail slot between the week's attendance forms and the District office circular about spring scheduling. He found it when he came for the day's mail before first period: a folded half-sheet, his name in ink on the front fold. The handwriting was compressed and exact.

*Dr. Klein — If it is not inconvenient, could you stop at the harbormaster's office at your convenience this week. I have a document in German I would like you to look at, briefly. — E. Bowman.*

He stood in the office doorway with the note in his hand. The morning bell had not yet rung. Through the window above the secretary's desk he could see the schoolyard: frost on the packed ground, the elm at the corner of Henry's lot bare against the January sky, two students moving quickly across the yard in the cold.

He read the note again. Then once more.

*A document in German.* He was not certain what to make of that, in this place, in this January, in the third full winter of his knowledge of Coldwater Reach and the harbormaster who ran it with the careful neutrality of a person working inside a chosen boundary. He was available Thursday afternoon; there was nothing after four o'clock, and the walk to the harbor shack was eight minutes from the school's back steps, or nine in January when the footing was uncertain. He put the note in his coat pocket. He picked up his attendance forms and went upstairs.

He came on Thursday.

---

The harbor in January was a different harbor than in any other month — not merely colder or emptier but quieter in a way that was more total, as if the season had removed something structural and left only the essential geometry. Three lobster boats at the east face of the dock, nothing moored, the Callie B. laid up at the south end, the water in the inner cove nearly still at the low tide and reflecting the sky's flat gray. Marcus came down the hill from the school's back street and onto the dock road with his collar up and his hands in his pockets and the cold finding the gaps at his wrists and behind his ears with the thoroughness he had come to expect of it. The dock boards were clear of ice; someone had sanded them. The shack at the dock's head had its light on inside.

He knocked twice.

She opened the door immediately. She said: come in and shut it.

He shut it. The interior: the radio set on the left wall, the tide clock on the right, the desk in the center with the log ledger open to the current week's entries. She had moved a chair from the corner and positioned it beside the desk's near edge. He sat. She sat. The cold he had brought in with him dispersed quickly in the shack's heat; the coal stove in the corner was burning low and steady. The harbor sound outside was muffled by the closed door and the winter-weight windows — or not muffled exactly, but held at a distance.

She did not speak at once. She pulled open the desk's middle drawer and lifted out something wrapped in a piece of dark tarp — a flat bundle the size of a wallet, which it became, a moment later, when she set it on the desk blotter and began folding the tarp back.

She said: it came out of Farber's net off the inner bar in December. Fourteen months in the water. She said: could you look at the paper inside.

The wallet: leather, badly deteriorated, warped to the shape of whatever it had settled against below the bar. One long seam was open, the leather parted and curling. She opened the wallet along its length and folded the front face back, and he could see the interior: the pocket seams failed, the compartments empty. But the center divider had held. She had already worked it open — her thumbnail along the seam's edge, he guessed, a quarter-inch at a time — and from the interior face she drew a folded piece of paper, stiff now as thin card, and laid it flat on the blotter.

He leaned forward.

He did not touch it yet. He looked at the handwriting first: a formal Continental script, the kind taught in German-language schools from the 1880s through to the 1920s and perhaps beyond, with the characteristic backward cant of the ascenders — the tall letters sloping right to left, opposite to the English habit. The ink had bled into the paper's grain at the stroke edges but held in the center of each mark where the pen's pressure had been greatest. The lower portion of the card was gone to a uniform gray-brown wash. The upper third was partial, lines visible in outline but not legible. In the center third, where the outermost fold had held longest against the shelter of the sealed divider, three letters were readable. Possibly the beginning of a fourth, at the fold's right edge, where the protection had been incomplete.

He looked at them for a long time.

The first two letters were clear. *F* and *e*. The *F* had the diagonal crossbar of the German formal hand; the *e* was open at its top in the continental manner. The third character was ambiguous: the ink's ghost suggested the loop of an *l* or the closed form of an *i*, but the damage at the stroke's center made certainty impossible. The fourth, if there was a fourth, was a partial arc at the edge of the fold's protection — the upper curve of something. It might be *x*. It might be *k*. It might be a letter he could not reconstruct from so partial a form.

He sat back.

He said: I can make out part of what may be a name. The first two letters are *F* and *e*. He said: the subsequent letters are damaged. He paused. He asked: where did you say this was found?

She said: Farber's net. The inner bar. He brought it in on the nineteenth of December.

He looked at the fragment again without touching it. He said: may I look at it more carefully this evening? Under better light. I would return it in the morning.

She looked at him, and it was not the look of someone suspicious of him so much as the look of someone who was deciding what kind of person he was — taking a measurement she had been in the process of taking for some time. He held the measurement. She said: yes. She refolded the card along its existing lines and wrapped the wallet in the tarp and held it across the desk. He took it.

She said: I'll expect it before school, then.

He said: before eight.

She pulled the middle drawer open and shut it again, the particular gesture of someone verifying that something remains where it has been put. He stood and put on his coat. The tarp bundle went into the breast pocket, where the note had been on Tuesday morning. He went out.

The cold came back immediately, specific and total.

---

His room at half past nine: the radiator on the south wall making its mid-winter noise — the metal's expansion against the heat, a ticking that was at first, when he arrived in Coldwater, an irritation, and was now something he rarely heard except in moments of particular attention. The desk lamp on. The tarp unwrapped and the wallet open on the blotter with the fragment flat on its surface, held at the top edge by two books pressed together so that they weighted the card without creasing it.

He found the Emergency Committee folder on the shelf above the desk, behind the Stifter volume and ahead of the District correspondence box. He had not opened it since spring. He opened it now and found Dr. Feld's December 1943 letter in the second section, filed deliberately behind his own New York correspondence, because that was where a letter that might require future action belonged. He drew it out and read it in full in his accustomed way — sequentially, not skipping to the part he was looking for, because skipping was a practice that caused him to miss things, and he did not want to miss anything tonight.

The paragraph was in the letter's third section. *Felix K., linguist, formerly of Leipzig. Last documented Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, September 22, 1943. Intended to travel south by sea. No further word as of this date.*

He set the letter on the blotter, to the left of the fragment, so that both were visible under the lamp at once.

*Fe.*

*Felix.*

He sat with the two objects in front of him and did not move for a little while.

The probability was very high — the fragment on the right, the letter on the left, the lamp between them casting the precise light of the thing. The first two letters matched. The ambiguous third was consistent with *l*. The incomplete fourth was consistent with *x*: the terminal letter of *Felix*, if you were reading from the German, if you were a linguist from Leipzig trained in the formal hand of the late century, if you had been last documented in Yarmouth on the twenty-second of September 1943 and had been intending, from there, to travel south by sea.

The inner bar lay eighty yards from the breakwater's cove-side base. She had said so. Fourteen months of tidal action in the inner cove: a body and its effects settling toward the bar on the ebb, shifting and settling again, small objects moving in the specific geometry of the Reach's bottom currents until they rested. He had not needed to work the arithmetic because he had already worked it, in a different form, in the notebook in April, with the autumn weather record and the crossing time for a small motorboat out of Yarmouth running south.

He got the notebook from its place between the English journal and the grammar workbook.

He wrote the date at the top of a fresh page. Then in the private shorthand he kept in the German, the notation stripped of its qualifying architecture — bare sequences of fact, the record pared to what resisted argument — he wrote:

*Fe — l or i — x or k. Fragment, wallet, inner bar. Recovered December 1944. Fourteen months in the water.*

*Felix K. Linguist, Leipzig. Yarmouth NS, September 22, 1943. Coldwater, October 1943.*

He drew a line.

*If this is correct: Felix K. is the man on the breakwater. No one has known his name for fourteen months.*

He drew another line.

*I do not know for certain. The fragment is not sufficient.*

He put the pen down. He looked at what he had written for a moment — at the last two lines in particular. They were accurate. They remained.

He put the fragment back in the tarp. He put the Feld letter in the folder and returned the folder to the shelf, to its position behind the Stifter volume. He closed the notebook and put it back in its place.

He would return the fragment before eight. He would tell her what he could honestly tell her: that the legible letters were *F* and *e*, consistent with the beginning of a German-language name; that the subsequent letters were too damaged for certain identification; that this was the limit of what the fragment permitted him to say.

He would not tell her about the Feld letter.

He turned the reasons over and they remained reasons. He had a theory, not a confirmed name. A theory offered to Eira Bowman — a probable name for the man on her breakwater, the man she had watched walk out of the dock's darkness fourteen months ago in October, whatever she had been carrying from that night — was not information she could use. A theory she could not verify was weight with no purchase. And if he was wrong, the wrong name in the mind of a woman already holding what she held was a harm he would not be able to undo.

This was the argument. He believed it.

He was not entirely certain it was the only reason.

He did not open the shortwave. He went to bed.

The name lay where he had put it, in the notebook, in the German, on the shelf under the Stifter volume, in the dark. *Felix K.* He looked at the ceiling's familiar shape — the north side's slant where the roofline came down to meet the plaster — and the question he had written in the notebook's margin that April evening in the empty classroom, *What do I intend to do with this?*, had finally obtained an answer of a kind: for now, nothing. Return the fragment. Say what the evidence permits. Hold the rest.

*For now* was a qualified phrase. He went to sleep with it in the sentence.
