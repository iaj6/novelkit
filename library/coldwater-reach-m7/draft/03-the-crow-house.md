# The Crow House

The passage on the board was Lessing — the opening scene of *Minna von Barnhelm*, Act One, which he had chosen because it was military and therefore timely and had the further advantage of containing enough grammatical difficulty in the first page to occupy two students for the duration of the period without leaving them time to consider what they were actually translating. He had written it out in his German hand — the one that was smaller than his English hand and still leaned rightward in the way he had been taught at eight years old — and now he stood with his back to the room and looked at what he'd written while the two girls worked.

He could hear Doris Hatch on the second sentence by her breathing. She was the kind of student who held her breath when reading in a second language, as though the effort of comprehension required the body to suspend its ordinary functions until it could be resolved. Louise Pelletier had moved through the second sentence and arrived at the third, which contained a dative of purpose he had not glossed in the notes he had put on their desks, and he was waiting, without appearing to wait, to see what she would do with it.

The third chair — at the left-hand desk, second row from the window — was empty.

Pfeiffer. The information had not arrived as a deliberate perception but as an alteration in the room's ordinary texture, the way a room registers an absence not because one has looked for it but because the space that is usually occupied is not: a gap that does not announce itself but is there. Hannah Pfeiffer was the only one of the three who came to a German passage knowing, in some preliminary way, what the words contained before she construed them. She had her father's Bavarian dialect in her phrasing — recognizable even in the small German she produced in class, a particular shaping of the back vowels, a rhythm to the subordinate clause that was distinct from anything she might have learned here — and this told him things about the household she came from that he had not asked her to tell him and that she had not, so far, offered.

"Hatch," he said.

Doris looked up with the expression of a student hoping to remain unnoticed a moment longer. She read the second sentence aloud. The German was accurate; the accent was northern American in the particular way that Maine is northern American, a distinct phonological territory, and the meaning came through intact, which was the purpose of translation class and sufficient. He corrected the long *u* once, without comment, and moved to Louise Pelletier, who had navigated the dative independently and correctly, a small specific satisfaction — the kind that belongs to teaching rather than scholarship, less clean but present in its way — and he told her so. He wrote the dative construction on the board and would set it again in two weeks to see whether the lesson held.

Below the Lessing, he wrote: *Wohin?*

Beneath it, in English: *Where to?*

He left both on the board without explaining them. The word was in the passage; he had no other reason to put it there that he would have been able to articulate in terms the girls would find useful. Doris Hatch looked at the board and then at her notebook. Louise Pelletier looked at the board and then out the window, where the October trees had gone to the plain brown of late October, the last week before November reduced them further. The room smelled of chalk and damp wool and the coal furnace's particular brand of inadequacy, which he had been smelling since March and had accepted as characteristic. 

He stood with the chalk in his hand and the chalked German of a playwright dead a hundred and sixty years on the board behind him, and through the east window the afternoon light came in at the angle it came in at in mid-October at this latitude, low and already beginning its withdrawal, and the chair at the left-hand desk stayed empty, and he set them both to the fourth sentence and waited.

---

He heard his name not said.

The supply room at the end of the second-floor hall had a door that did not close flush with the frame, and over a year and a half he had learned to stand slightly back from it when he was collecting chalk or examination paper, because from that position he could hear, or could not hear, what was happening in the hallway in a way that was occasionally informative. This was not a habit he would have thought to develop in Vienna. It had developed, in stages, through Paris and London and afterward.

He was putting chalk in his coat pocket when Hendricks from mathematics — and the woman whose name he had not yet retained, who taught something in the girls' physical program and something else he could not recall — came around the corner from the stairs. He heard them come to the particular pause that conversations came to when he entered a range. He walked at the pace he had learned, which was the pace of a man with nothing in particular he was doing or listening for, and came out of the supply room and went along the hall.

He heard: *no papers. No name.* He heard: *Coast Guard took the log pages, which Mabel thought was notable.* He heard: *not from Coldwater, not from anywhere anyone can—* and then he was past the point where it carried.

This was sufficient. He had been a philologist for twenty-three years. The body on the breakwater: no papers, no name, not from here. A man without documentation, found at the edge of the sea, in clothing that suggested somewhere else. He put the fragment where he put things he had not yet finished examining, and went down the stairs into the afternoon.

---

The walk from the school to the Crow house took nine minutes at his ordinary pace. Where the road bent upward at the ridge, before the elms closed in above it, the harbor was briefly visible through the October-stripped trees: a strip of gray water, the breakwater a dark granite line extending from the dock's end, the fixed red light at its seaward terminus not yet lit in the afternoon. He stopped for a moment — not for any reason that required stopping, but because the view had precise edges and he had discovered, in his months here, that he found a particular comfort in things with precise edges. Then he went on.

The Crow house was white clapboard, second-from-the-end on Elm Street, with a modest front yard and a porch that in warmer months Henry occupied in the last of the afternoon light. This was not a habit anyone had pointed out to Marcus; it was something he had understood, early in his time here, as the portion of the day Henry reserved for his own thinking, and he had taken some care not to arrive at that hour in a way that required Henry to acknowledge him. In October the porch was not useful. Henry was inside.

Marcus let himself in with his key.

The hallway was warm, not aggressively but sufficiently — the warmth of a house that had been maintained with care over a long time. He heard the Rockland paper's particular rustle from the kitchen, which meant Henry at the table. From the back of the house, coffee: the real kind, not the grain substitute, which had a flavor he had become too well acquainted with in his first months here before he understood that the Crow house was somehow an exception to this. He had arrived at the correct position about this fact, which was that it was not his to investigate and that the correct response to it was gratitude without commentary.

He hung his coat on the peg.

In the kitchen Lillian was at the stove with her back to him. She said, without turning: "Marcus. Coat."

"Already," he said.

She glanced back briefly — a look that was not a look of doubt. Henry was at the table with the Rockland paper and looked up from it in his way, which was not all the way up but enough, and Marcus sat.

The kitchen table had three chairs. His was the one that faced the east window, through which the back yard was visible, and past the yard the hillside, and above the hillside the gray sky of October pressing down in a way that was not unpleasant but was entirely earnest. Henry's chair faced the door. Lillian's could see both. This arrangement had established itself in the first week of March and had not been discussed.

There was a quality to this kitchen that the rest of Coldwater, in general, did not have in his presence — a quality he had spent some months attempting to name and had eventually settled for calling the absence of management. Elsewhere in town he was, in some ongoing functional sense, a matter that required decisions: the credentials requiring periodic validation, the provenance requiring explanation when it came up, the presence requiring an account of itself in a community that had not anticipated it and had made what room it had made in the spirit of pragmatic necessity rather than affirmative welcome. He had learned to live within this without complaint, which was easier than it sounded and also harder than it sounded. Here the management was absent. Lillian had written, in her letter through the school board the previous winter, that he was welcome, and the word had meant what it said.

He folded his hands on the table and then unfolded them, because folded hands was a classroom posture and he was not in the classroom.

"There was a man found on the breakwater," Marcus said. "Tuesday morning. I understand he had no papers and no name."

Henry did not respond immediately. He set the paper down, flat, without marking his place — he kept the page in his head — and turned the sentence over with the unhurried attention of a man who had decided that responses given before they were ready had a quality of incompleteness that was not worth the saved time. Then he said: "Found him Tuesday?"

"That is what I have been given to understand."

Henry nodded once, picked up the paper, and returned his attention to it. This gesture, which Marcus had initially read incorrectly in his first weeks here, meant that the information was received and placed and required no further handling at this moment — that there were categories of information which arrived complete and which adding to served nothing. It was not incuriosity; it was the practiced discipline of a man who had learned precisely which things warranted a response and which things warranted only acknowledgment, and who did not waste the one on occasions that called for the other. He was still in the process of learning it.

Lillian set the cups down and sat and poured. She asked after the school furnace, which had been running cold on the second floor since September and which the building staff had so far addressed without result, and Marcus told her what he knew, and Henry's expression did not change, and Lillian said she would have a word.

They drank their coffee. The Rockland paper reported what it reported. Outside, Elm Street darkened toward five o'clock in the way October darkened it: not gradually but in steps, the light changing its angle and then abandoning the angle entirely and leaving only the flat gray that preceded the dark. They sat in it, in the kitchen, and Marcus held the fragment — no papers, no name, not from around here — at the edge of his attention where he had put it, and where it stayed.

---

His room was on the second floor, east-facing, with a slanted ceiling on the north wall where the roofline came down to meet the plaster, which gave it the shape of a room in which one moved with a working knowledge of the available space. He had acquired this knowledge in the first days of March and had arranged the room accordingly: the desk under the east window, the single wooden chair beside the bookcase, the narrow bed along the south wall near the radiator, about which he had not yet asked Henry, understanding the asking to require a confidence in his own domestic standing that he was still, in certain particulars, assembling, a year and a half on.

The shortwave was on the desk: a Zenith portable, not the console that sat in the Crows' parlor, but a smaller set he had acquired in Bangor in the spring of his arrival with money he had needed for other things and had spent on this instead. The dial glowed amber when the set was warm and the tuning knob required a light touch and something he had too much time to develop.

He switched it on and waited.

The BBC World Service came in on 9.41 megacycles, or had come in there last Thursday; the frequencies shifted and he had to find them freshly each time, which was an inconvenience he had come to regard as appropriate, or at least as not inappropriate. He tuned.

Static. Through it, something: a woman's voice reading figures he couldn't resolve before the signal dropped. He tuned past. A military frequency in English, procedural, not meant for him. Past. At 9.38 the BBC identification signal arrived — four notes, the motif that had acquired, over three years of hearing it in conditions of varying comfort and uncertainty, a significance — and then the signal faltered and faded before the bulletin began.

He sat with the dial and waited.

It came back. The Italian campaign: not going quickly, but going. The Pacific: performing better than the assessments of late 1942 had suggested. The convoy situation in the North Atlantic: improved, the broadcaster said, in the careful syntax of someone managing what could not be entirely managed, and Marcus listened with the full attention he gave to news from Europe.

The man on the breakwater was there at the edge of his attention, where he had put the fragment.

He did not write in his German notebook tonight. He had taken it out and set it on the desk, which had been his intention, and the intention had not carried through.

The BBC signal faded. He tuned along the dial slowly — a quarter-degree at a time, the method that had a better yield than the broader sweeps he had tried in the early months — and found, in order: a fragment of French, gone before he could determine the source; something that might have been Brazilian, the geography of radio being less intuitive than one assumed; a clear voice in American English for thirty seconds on the subject of soybean prices in the Midwest; and then the static, full and thorough.

He sat with the static.

No papers. No name. A man dressed carefully for something and arrived at the wrong end of it, at the edge of a sea he had apparently tried to cross. 

He held the fragment the way one holds a word at the edge of recall — not pressing on it, because pressure did not help.

He had possessed, in August of 1941, a suit that did not fit him. The chest had been correct; the shoulders had gone loose in the way garments go loose when the body inside them has reduced, over some months, to less than the garment expected. He had possessed, in August of 1941, a document describing him in English as a resident alien enemy — *enemy* the substantive noun, *alien* and *resident* the qualifying adjectives that determined what could legally be done with him — and this had been the document he had when he stepped off the train in Boston with his cardboard case and the two books he had been allowed to bring with him when he left. The man who stepped off the train did not correspond, in any precise sense, to the man who had boarded one in Vienna three years earlier. He had spent the months following the arrival determining the nature of the correspondence, and the question had not been fully resolved, though he had made progress.

He did not know why the man on the breakwater interested him with this particular quality of attention. He turned the tuning dial a quarter-degree and found static of a slightly different texture and went on sitting with the thought he was not quite pursuing, because the pursuit would not help.

The BBC came back for a moment — shipyards, the Pacific, a name he didn't catch — and dissolved again into the static.

On the shelf above the desk, in the amber dial-glow, his notebooks stood in the order he maintained: the English journal, the grammar workbook from his first months at the school, the current German one. He could see, from his chair, that the spine of the German notebook was written in his English hand.

This had been happening for some months. When he needed to write something quickly — a word, a heading, a note in the margin of something — his English hand came first, without direction. Not because his German hand had failed him; when he turned to it deliberately it was there, the *Kurrent* script and the Roman print both intact, present and correct. But the English arrived first, without the interval of retrieval that German now required. The English was, in some working sense, more available. It was the closer thing.

He had not expected this. He had expected, if anything, the reverse to be permanent: that German would remain primary in the way a native language remains primary, the English always a skill rather than a reflex, the boundary maintained even when other things were not. The boundary had not maintained itself. He still noticed it when he noticed it, the way one notices a change that has been gradual enough to arrive without announcement.

He reached over and switched off the set.

---

The room without the radio found its other sounds: the north wall settling, cold working at the seams of the window glass, the boards making their adjustments with the night temperature. The harbor was somewhere down the hill in the dark beyond the east windows, not visible, present in the way of things one knows to be there without seeing them. In whatever building across town contained the information — whatever file or desk or locked cabinet — a dead man's absence of papers and name continued to be a fact that waited for whatever facts waited for.

Marcus took off his glasses and set them on the desk. The room went soft at its edges: the desk, the chair, the bookcase with its spines, the shelf above the desk with the notebooks — shapes he knew and knew where they were, imprecise now but present. The east window, dark. The harbor's direction.

The room was dark and cold and was, for the moment, where he lived.
