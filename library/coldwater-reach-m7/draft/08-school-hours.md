# School Hours

The back door of the Coldwater school used a skeleton key and required a lift on the handle before the lock would turn. Henry had learned this in his first week and it was still true eleven years later. He came through it at five-twenty and pulled the light chain at the bottom of the basement stairs.

The furnace was a Vulcan No. 6, coal-fired, installed 1921. In cold weather below twenty degrees it ran lean on the right side near the upper draft plate. Not a defect. A characteristic. He adjusted for it the same way every time: two scoops from the left bunker, one from the right, the draft plate turned clockwise a half-turn from where he'd left it Friday. He stood and listened. The furnace settled the way it should. He replaced the scuttle and went upstairs.

Before anyone arrived the building made its own sounds. The pipes coming up with the heat. The windows ticking in their frames. The particular creak of the second-floor hallway where the building had settled eastward over the years, the floorboards riding the angle. Henry had heard these sounds enough times that they weren't sounds anymore. They were the building telling him it was all right.

He had been in this building eleven years. He knew it in November: which windows swelled in their frames overnight, which stair nosings worked loose before Christmas, how much heat the east corridor lost between seven and noon when the wind came off the water from the north. He had a route, the same every day, adjusted for what the building needed.

The enrollment was down since spring. About fifteen students now, where there had been twenty-two.

By seven-thirty he had the hallways swept. The chalk dust came off Marcus Klein's end of the hall in considerable measure — the man wrote everything twice on the board, the original text and then the English below it, all of it in a small precise hand that required more erasing than you might expect. Henry cleared the ledges and went downstairs and had the supply closet in order before the students arrived at eight.

They came through the front doors at eight. About fifteen of them. He heard them without needing to see them.

The building's social weather he read the same way he read the physical building — its pressures, what moved and what didn't. Mrs. Morse and Miss Tatum said good morning when they came by him in the hall. Mr. Reedy did not, which had been the case since September 1932, and Henry didn't expect it to change. He held the mop and let Reedy pass.

---

Mid-morning, carrying the repair kit from the supply closet to the east stairwell, Henry came around the corner as Marcus turned out of the German room. Marcus had a roll of papers under one arm — the Latin texts, from the look of them.

"The window," Marcus said.

"Still sticking?"

"The cold comes in on the north side."

"I'll get to it this afternoon," Henry said.

Marcus said that was fine and went left down the hall toward Latin. Henry watched him go — the slight figure, the coat a little wrong for the Maine weather, the careful step — and then went downstairs to put the kit away.

---

Around two o'clock he went to the teachers' room to collect the recycled paper. Mrs. Morse and Miss Tatum were there. He heard the word "breakwater" and then something about what a shame and whether the Coast Guard had ever found anything out.

He took the paper box and went.

The body had come in off the water five weeks ago. The Coast Guard had come and gone. The town had not stopped talking about it. He heard it most days, coming through rooms with the mop and the repair kit — the breakwater, the body, what the Coast Guard might or might not know. He expected to keep hearing it until something replaced it, and nothing had.

---

At three-thirty he stopped at the school office about the supply order. The chalk stock was running low and the coal bunker needed a second delivery before December. Parsons had the forms ready and they went through them. When they were done she kept him a moment.

"Mr. Crow," she said. She stopped. Started again. "There was a letter, early October. From a parent." Her eyes were on the wall beside his shoulder. "They wrote to the board — asking whether the school had properly considered Dr. Klein's background. Whether a German teacher was appropriate, given the times."

Henry waited.

"I wrote back myself. That his credentials were confirmed by the Emergency Committee and that the board had reviewed the matter thoroughly before his hire." She straightened the folder she was holding. "Nothing formal came of it. I kept the letter in the file. I thought you should know."

"I appreciate you telling me," Henry said.

She nodded and went back to her desk.

He went upstairs.

The German classroom was empty. Marcus had left at four, the way Marcus left every day at four. Henry opened the repair kit on the windowsill and took out the block plane. The casement had swollen in the frame — the putty on the north side gone brittle in October, the wood moving with the temperature. He shaved a quarter-inch off the casement edge, worked the sash until it seated right, and checked that the latch caught. Cold would still come in on the north side in any real wind. There was a limit to what a block plane could do about November.

He tested the window twice and closed the classroom door behind him. The hallway was empty.

He walked home.

---

Lillian had soup on the stove. Henry hung his coat on the peg by the kitchen door and washed his hands at the sink. The bowls were already out.

"How was today?" she asked.

"Fine," he said.

She set the bread on the table. They ate. After a while she said: "Is Marcus still running the shortwave every night?"

"I expect so," Henry said.

She got up and refilled his bowl without asking. He thanked her. They ate the rest of it and she washed the dishes and he dried them and put them away, and then she went upstairs. He heard her on the stairs — the third step from the top, the one that always spoke — and then the floor above him and then nothing.

He sat down at the kitchen table with the Rockland paper.

---

He had already read most of it. He opened it to the shipping notices anyway.

The furnace was running below. The window over the sink showed nothing but the dark outside and his own dim shape when he looked up. The house settled in the cold — the boards in the west wall moved once, then a second time, then stopped.

He didn't read the notices.

Parsons had the letter in her file. Nothing formal had come of it, and if something formal did come, he would know before it reached anywhere that could do damage. In eleven years he had become the person in this building that people told things to when they needed to set something down — Parsons tonight, others before her, others probably still to come. He had it now. He had fixed the window. The window was fixed.

He folded the paper and turned off the kitchen lamp and went upstairs in the dark. He knew each stair. He got into bed without waking Lillian and lay still until he was warm.
