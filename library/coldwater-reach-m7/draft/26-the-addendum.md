# The Addendum

The February log ran clean for seventeen days before anything happened worth noting, and even then what happened was routine: a dock cleat worked loose in the fourteenth's blow, and she spent an afternoon rethreading it through gusts that averaged thirty-eight knots and peaked at forty-one. That went in the log. Tide, barometer, wind direction — those went in every morning. The harbor radio gave her the European news at low volume and she let it run. The Third Army was at the Rhine. The Russians had crossed the Oder in force. She was aware of these things the way she was aware of the tide schedule: as facts she needed to know and that shaped what she could expect.

The inner cove had been locked under ice since mid-January. Two lobster boats were still working the outer bar — the *Maisie K.* and Farber's *June M.* — and they came to the dock every third day or so to unload and ice down, and she wrote their arrivals and departures in the log, and they went back out again, and the cove stayed still and gray-white under the overcast that had been sitting over the coast for most of the month. The mail boat came Tuesdays and Fridays. She was at the dock to log it and to take the harbor's share of the freight, which in February was modest.

The middle drawer was locked. The key was on the ring above the door, where it always was.

She had known since December what she was going to do. Not in the way of a decision — she had not sat down and decided. It had come in the way things come when they are correct: over a succession of ordinary mornings, arriving in segments, settling into place without ceremony. By January it was there, occupying the same part of her thinking as the seam she needed to re-pitch on the float's east fender plank: a thing that would be done. The ice-out came the same way. You did not wait for a moment. You felt the particular quality of the late-winter light and you looked at the cove, still as a plate of glass, and you understood that it was the last state, not the permanent one. You wrote the ice-over in the log and you got on with the work.

On a Wednesday morning in late February, the tide in from the southeast, the barometer holding at 30.2, the harbor with nothing on it that needed her right then, she got out the harbormaster's stationery.

---

The form was pale green stock, the port seal in the upper left corner. The heading lines were set for date, subject, and reference. She dated it February 28, 1945. She wrote *Supplementary Statement — Addendum to Record, Re: Incident of 12 October 1943* on the subject line and the reference to Lieutenant Ennis and the First District file below it. Then she wrote the body.

She did not draft it first. She had known what it would say since sometime in December. She wrote it once, in the harbormaster's hand she had been using in this office for nine years — the hand that was hers now in a way it had started out not being; she had consciously copied Owen's script in the first months, believing consistency in the log mattered enough to justify it, and had only gradually stopped.

---

*Supplementary Statement — Addendum to Record*
*Re: Incident of 12 October 1943, Coldwater Reach, Maine*
*Reference: Statement of 12 October 1943, USCG First District, Portland — Lt. R. Ennis, case officer*

*I, Eira J. Bowman, Harbormaster, Port of Coldwater Reach, Maine, submit the following supplementary material in addition to my statement of October 12, 1943, filed with the United States Coast Guard, First District, Portland.*

*On the night of October 11, 1943, at approximately 23:00 hours, I was present at the Coldwater Reach dock performing routine inspection of the F/V* Callie B. *(owner: R. Farber), moored at the second slip. During this inspection, I observed a small wooden motorboat, running without navigation lights, approach from the south and tie at the dock's eastern end, near the breakwater access. I observed a single occupant — male, of approximately middle age — who left the vessel on foot and proceeded in the direction of the breakwater. I did not engage with the individual. I observed him for less than one minute. I was unable to identify the individual or to determine the vessel's name or registration.*

*When I returned to the dock the following morning, October 12, 1943, the vessel was absent.*

*I did not include this observation in my statement of October 12, 1943, to Lieutenant R. Ennis. My reason for the omission: I judged the observation insufficiently documented and the identification of the individual uncertain.*

*I submit this account now as supplementary material to the above-referenced case file, for the record.*

*Eira J. Bowman*
*Harbormaster, Port of Coldwater Reach*
*February 28, 1945*

---

She set down the pen. She read it over once, from the heading to her name. Nothing to change.

The reason she had given for the omission was true. The observation had been at a distance, in the dark, for less than a minute. She had not been able to see his face. She had not known the man's name and did not know it now. She had judged it insufficiently documented and she had been right. These things were true. They were not everything. The official record would hold what it could hold.

---

The Yarmouth reply took less time. She had been composing it, off and on, for months. She wrote it to the Harbour Authority at Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, in response to their inquiry dated March 19, 1944 — filed in the back of the middle drawer for eleven months. She directed them to the USCG First District case file in Portland, Maine, under the October 12, 1943 incident at Coldwater Reach. She noted that supplementary material bearing on the inquiry had been submitted to the same file that morning, February 28, 1945. She did not speculate about what the material might indicate. She did not name anyone.

She signed it, dated it February 28, 1945, and addressed the envelope.

Both documents went into a manila folder. She wrote the date on the tab and set it on the corner of the desk.

---

She put on Owen's peacoat and her hat and went up the hill.

It was a cold morning, overcast, temperature in the low twenties with no wind to speak of. The road from the dock to the main street was glazed from the overnight freeze, and she took the pace she always took on ice — heels down, weight back, steps shorter than usual. The hardware store was open. The diner's windows were fogged from the breakfast crowd, and from inside came the particular radio-noise of a morning when someone had turned it up louder than the room required. She passed both without stopping. Nine minutes to the auxiliary station.

The Coast Guard auxiliary station sat above the main street on a short rise, set back behind a parking apron with a flagpole. The flag was up and moving slightly in what wind there was off the harbor. She had been here four times in her nine-plus years as harbormaster: twice with emergency weather reports during the bad winters, once to register the dock's navigation-light upgrade under the blackout protocol, and once in November 1943, ten days after the October inquiry was closed, when she had come to give her formal statement to Ennis's district clerk. She had been here that time and said what she had said and signed where they told her to sign and come back down the hill and written the day's weather in the log. The visit was in the log as a notation. Everything was in the log.

The petty officer on duty was a young man she had seen twice but didn't know well — Kerrigan, she thought, transferred down from Bath in September. He was at the counter when she came in, working through a maintenance form, and he looked up.

She said she had documents to submit for forwarding to the First District office in Portland, relating to the October 1943 incident at Coldwater Reach.

He set down the form and held out a hand for the folder.

He opened it and read both documents through, working at a steady rate, starting with the addendum and then moving to the Yarmouth reply. She stood at the counter and waited. The station had a coal stove in the near corner and she felt the warmth of it through the peacoat after the cold outside. The harbor radio was running in the back room on the same frequency she kept the shack radio, and the correspondents were giving the morning news at higher volume, and it was the same news she'd had at low volume in the shack.

He said he would log the receipt and forward both documents to Portland with the district courier. The addendum would go to the existing case file under the October 1943 incident reference number. The Yarmouth reply would go with it as associated correspondence. He gave her the copies — one each — and wrote the log entry. She watched him write it. He had small, neat handwriting, the kind that gets used to forms.

He pushed the copies across the counter. She folded them once and put them in her inside coat pocket.

He asked if there was anything else.

She said: has anything come in on the October 1943 inquiry. From the First District, or from anywhere. Any response on record.

He went to the logbooks behind the counter. He pulled 1944 and ran a finger down the index, turned to October, went through it. He turned forward to November and December. He pulled 1943 and worked backwards from December. Then he set both books back in place.

Nothing in the record from Portland, he said. No response on record from any source.

She said: thank you. She buttoned the coat and went back out into the cold.

---

She went down the hill at the same pace she had come up. The road still glazed. The diner windows still fogged. The harbor was where she had left it.

She went into the shack and sat down at the desk.

The manila folder was gone from the corner. That was how it felt — not lighter in the room, just accurate, the way a room feels when a thing that should not be on the desk is no longer on the desk. The middle drawer was still there. She sat for a moment and looked at the harbor through the window without opening it.

The breakwater ran out to its fixed red light. The inner cove was still locked, same as this morning, the same as yesterday and the day before. A single herring gull was working the water off the end of the dock float, circling and dipping and circling again, its pattern unchanged by what had or hadn't been submitted to the Coast Guard auxiliary station twenty minutes ago.

The Yarmouth letter was gone from the drawer. The addendum was filed. What remained: the wallet, still wrapped in Farber's tarp in the back of the middle section. And inside the wallet, compressed between the seam and the stiff fold of card stock, the partial script that Marcus Klein had read under the lamp in January and returned to her before eight o'clock the following morning, carrying it up the hill in his briefcase in the cold. She had put it back in the wallet and put the wallet back in the drawer and locked the drawer and said nothing to anyone.

She had not submitted these objects because they were physical property — they belonged to the dead man or to his estate, such as it was, wherever it was — and they belonged in the official file, and she was not ready to give them up.

She was not sure why. Not exactly.

She put the submission copies in the front section of the drawer's ordinary papers and locked the drawer again and hung the key on the ring on the nail above the door. The ring was heavier than it needed to be — four keys on it, the drawer key and the dock locker and the supply cabinet and the shack's main door, and she had never taken any of them off because the ring had been that way since Owen had it and it had stayed that way.

The harbor was outside the window. Late February. The overcast sat flat and high over the cove. Somewhere off the breakwater's east face the number-four bell buoy was ringing in the long swell — its clapper worn, the tone a half-step flat from its rated note, a thing she had stopped noticing years ago except when she made herself notice it. She had logged it three times for service and service had been twice deferred and the buoy was still half a step flat and it rang in the swell the same as always.

She did not feel better. She did not feel worse. What she felt was something she did not try to name. It was in the category of: done.

The harbor did not need the rest of it.

---

She opened the log and wrote the day's conditions.

*Feb. 28. Overcast. Temp. 22°F at 0800, rising slowly. Wind NW at 8 kts. Barometer 30.2, steady. Tide: high 0643, low 1301. Inner cove: ice holds. No arrivals. Buoy no. 4 clapper: third request for service, log this date.*

Below the weather entry, in the same column and the same hand and the same black ink:

*Supplementary statement re: incident of 12 October 1943 submitted this date to USCG Aux. Station, Coldwater Reach, for forwarding to First District, Portland. Concurrent reply to Yarmouth NS inquiry (received April 1944) submitted. Copies held.*

She closed the log. She was still harbormaster. The harbor was there outside the window. She went back to work.
