# The Notebook

The three girls had gone at four o'clock. He stayed.

He had been carrying the German notebook since morning, in the inside pocket of his coat, and had not opened it. He set the exercise books on the teaching desk and took out his pen and sat for a moment in the empty classroom, looking at nothing in particular — the east window, the flat gray light coming through it, the chalk ledge where he had written today's conjugation exercise, which he now went up and erased. He cleaned the eraser against the ledge and the chalk dust fell. He sat back down.

The three girls: Priscilla Mack, Doris Farber, Hannah Pfeiffer. German enrollment for the current year and, he suspected, for the foreseeable future. There was no exam at the end of the year — he had asked the administration in September and they had looked at him as if he had inquired about something beside the point, which was perhaps accurate. He had been teaching from primary texts instead: nineteenth-century letters, simple prose, a few pages of Goethe in the original that he had typed out himself because there was no text available and he was not prepared to assign Faust. In the autumn the lesson that most occupied the three of them was the grammatical relationship between direct and indirect object; by February it had become the subjunctive; now, in late April, with the school year approaching its last five weeks, they were reading a short story about a journey by train, and the discussion of vocabulary had drifted twice into territory that had nothing to do with the train.

He read the exercise books in his usual order. Hannah Pfeiffer's was on top.

She had written about the farm in early May — the seed orders, the garden beds being turned, the smell of the earth before planting. He had assigned a composition requiring specific description rather than general: something familiar, but named precisely. She had done this, and what she had named precisely was Bavarian in its vocabulary and in a dozen small matters of grammar. The verb placement in two of her sentences was the placement her father used at home, not the placement the textbook taught, which were not the same position. He had been correcting exactly this for seven months — the small marks in the margin, the notation of standard form beside the regional form she had grown up on. He did not make the corrections this time. He wrote at the bottom of her composition: *Gut. Weiter so.* He set it aside.

He sat for a moment with his pen in his hand.

Her German had not become more standard over the year. It had become more specific — more precise in the register her father had given her, more accurate to the dialect her father had brought from Bavaria to Coldwater in 1922. When she wrote about the farm she wrote it in the vocabulary of the farm she had grown up on, which was the vocabulary of a farm fifty kilometers east of Munich, transplanted and maintained through two decades of Maine winters. He had been trying to correct this in the direction of the textbook. He was not sure, this evening, why.

He set the other two exercise books to the side and took out the notebook.

---

He had started the current volume in the previous spring. More than half the pages were filled — closer to two-thirds now, he estimated, riffling through without reading — and the writing was denser in the most recent quarter of the book than in the earlier sections, as if the record had been crowding itself. He had not noticed this before.

He wrote the date at the top of a fresh page.

Then, in the private shorthand he had kept since his years as a junior lecturer in Vienna — subordination stripped away, bare sequences of fact, the record pared to what could not be argued with — he wrote what he had been assembling since December and had not yet assembled in one place.

*October 12, 1943. Unknown man, found at the Coldwater breakwater. No papers. No identification. Not from Coldwater. Clothing civilian: city coat, leather shoes. Approximate age: fifty, or a hard forty.*

He had known this since October. He had not written it here, in this form, until now. He was aware of the delay and did not intend to explain it, even in the private shorthand.

*December 1943. Letter from Dr. Feld, Emergency Committee. Felix K., linguist, formerly Leipzig. Last documented Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, September 22, 1943. Intended to reach Massachusetts by sea. No further word.*

He looked at that entry for a moment, then wrote the third.

*February 1944. Letter, Pfeiffer barn. Die die versucht haben zu gehen, sind weg. Bavarian. Refers to people from the mother's community who attempted to emigrate before the war closed. Letter written late 1943.*

He put the pen down and worked the arithmetic.

Yarmouth, Nova Scotia to the Maine coast by small motorboat, autumn 1943. He had checked the coastal weather record in the Courier archives in January: a northeast blow between the twenty-seventh of September and the fourth of October, sustained, the kind of system that would have kept a boat of eighteen feet close to shore or laid up in shelter for some portion of the crossing. Last documented Yarmouth, September 22. Departure: certainly after that date — by some days, perhaps a week, to arrange the boat, watch the weather, do what a man preparing to cross an open coast does. Call it late September. Add ten to fourteen days of careful navigation southward, hugging the coast in the way a man would who did not know this coast and was alone on it. Arrival possibilities along the Maine coast: mid-October.

He wrote it: *Departure Yarmouth: late September, estimated. Crossing: ten to fourteen days, careful. Arrival along the Maine coast: mid-October.*

He looked at the page.

Below the arithmetic, without the qualifying architecture that attended everything he wrote for any other reader — without the subordinate clauses, without the hedging — he wrote two lines:

*This is probability, not evidence. One does not publish on probability.*

And then, below that:

*One also does not ignore a probability this high.*

He sat with it. The classroom was quiet; the building had gone still around him the way buildings went still on Friday afternoons, as if it had been waiting for the week to end. Outside the east window the spruce line at the back of the schoolyard held against the overcast sky.

He tried to remember the 1936 philology conference in Vienna. He had been there; he had attended the third session, eighteenth-century correspondence, which was close to his own work. He reconstructed the corridor on the second day, the height of the ceiling, the sound of a specific door with a heavy hinge. He had spoken with a man whose affiliation was Leipzig, or possibly Halle — a linguist, or someone adjacent to language history, which was adjacent enough to have been at the same session. The man's face would not come. He had met perhaps a hundred people over three days, and retained perhaps twenty of them fully, and the man from Leipzig or Halle was somewhere in the remainder: present as a sense rather than a face, a handshake that had or had not made an impression, possibly a name on a program card he had long since lost. He tried to imagine what a linguist from Leipzig in the 1930s would have looked like. He produced several faces, none of which he trusted. It was possible he was misremembering the affiliation entirely. It was possible he had never spoken to Felix K. at all.

He wrote: *1936 Vienna conference: possible meeting. Not certain.*

He closed the notebook.

---

He sat in the empty classroom with the notebook closed on the desk before him and let the problem sit, which was his practice. He had a problem; he had assembled it; he did not have its resolution.

He knew Eira Bowman held something he did not have. He had known this since November — since seven minutes at the harbormaster's shack when she had attended to something she had decided not to raise, with the careful neutrality of a person working inside a chosen boundary. He recognized that quality; he had been practicing it himself, in several languages, for some years.

He had been considering, for several weeks, whether to bring what he had written to her.

The argument for it was not difficult. She had the official record, or what remained of it after the Coast Guard's October visit. She had whatever she had personally observed that had not gone into her statement, which — on the evidence of those seven minutes — was not nothing. If what he had assembled in the notebook was in any sense accurate, her information and his were adjacent to the same object, and the sum of the two might be more useful than either alone.

The argument against it took longer, because it was better.

He had a theory. He did not have evidence. That was not a technicality. It was the difference between the verifiable and the merely plausible, and in his experience — in the twenty years of his scholarship that had preceded the word scholarship ceasing to apply to him — the damage done by confusing the two was not recoverable. If he was wrong, if the man on the breakwater was someone with entirely different origins, then he had introduced a false story into the account of a woman already carrying something whose weight he could only estimate. A wrong theory was not a neutral addition to what she held. It was a second weight, and the wrong kind.

If he was right — if the man was Felix K., linguist of Leipzig, last documented Yarmouth on September 22 — then being right changed nothing that was still recoverable. The man was dead. His name was not in any official record and would not be restored to one by Marcus's theory. Going to Eira Bowman with a probable name was not the same thing as providing her with one.

He took the notebook out and opened it to the last page. Below the two lines about probability, he wrote a question in the private shorthand, without qualifying architecture:

*What do I intend to do with this?*

He left the question on the page. He put the notebook in his coat pocket, gathered his papers, and went out.

---

The walk back took nine minutes, as it always did. Down the school's back steps, through the alley past the hardware yard, up the hill to the elm at the corner of Henry's lot. The elm had leaves now — small, still partly furled, the pale green of the first weeks. The afternoon light was later than it had been a month ago. He did not linger under it.

Henry was at the kitchen table with the Rockland paper spread before him, open to a section that might have been the same section he had been reading since Thursday, or one that looked like it. The cup was at the far edge. Lillian was at the stove. There was a cup at Marcus's usual place; he sat down and wrapped both hands around it.

The kitchen had its sounds. The stove, the settling of the house, something outside — a screen door, two houses down — that opened and closed twice and then stopped. He sat and did not say anything particular, and nothing particular was said to him.

After a while Henry looked up from the paper and said: "You look like you've been working on something."

"Trying to," Marcus said.

Henry nodded. He looked at Marcus for a moment with the even and un-pressing regard he gave to most things, and returned to the paper.

Marcus sat for twenty minutes. Lillian mentioned that the sugar ration was short again and she was going to ask about the April allocation. He said he could stop in Monday. She said she would ask herself. The coffee was good. He drank it.

He said good night at half past five and went upstairs.

---

His room. The desk under the east window; the shortwave on the shelf above; the notebook's usual place between the English journal and the grammar workbook, where it was not because it was still in his coat pocket. He took it out and set it on the desk.

He sat in the chair by the window.

He did not turn on the shortwave. On Friday evenings he usually listened — the BBC at nine, CBS, whatever the coastal atmosphere permitted — but tonight he did not want more arriving from outside.

The last light was going. The spruce line at the back of the schoolyard was visible, then less visible, then a shape against a marginally lighter dark. The season's lengthening: the evenings extending by a minute or two every night, accumulating. He had been noticing this change in the light at Coldwater for two springs now and had not yet decided whether he noticed it because he had finally started paying attention to this place, or because the place had become available to him in some way it had not been before, or whether there was a distinction between those two things.

He thought about Yarmouth. A harbor in Nova Scotia in late September, built from what he knew of harbors in general and of this coast's autumn weather: the gray-green chop, the cold in the water before the cold reached the air. A man borrowing a small motorboat. The open coast running south, the shoreline kept in sight. Boston at the far end of it — Massachusetts, a name and an address, whatever document or institution or Emergency Committee contact was there to receive him. Ten days on the coast. Fourteen. A lantern carried for the compass work and shuttered before the last approach, because there were places on this coast that were blacked out and he would not know which until he was close.

He thought about what a man does when he has found the dock in the dark and tied the boat and walked to the end of a breakwater at the end of a very long transit. He thought about sitting down. The specific quality of having stopped. What the back of a light standard's pedestal would feel like against a man's back if he sat down with his legs extended before him and let himself be still.

He thought about *die die versucht haben zu gehen, sind weg.* He had been holding the phrase since February, since the afternoon Hannah Pfeiffer had set the folded page on his desk and he had read what the mother had placed between the cousin's October wedding and the firewood paragraph. The Bavarian past perfect, which was also a way of saying: irreversibly, without return. Gone into the category of the gone. The mother at a kitchen table in Bavaria writing this to a son in an American prisoner-of-war camp, not saying what she was saying, saying it anyway.

He thought, briefly — not about the camp at Sherbrooke, he did not go there directly, had learned in the subsequent years not to go there directly — but about a morning in August 1941 when he had come out of a place with a document in his coat pocket classifying him as a resident alien enemy, which was accurate and was not the full picture of what he was, and had stood on a street in the same summer air as the previous day and the day before that, only now he was outside in it. He had spent the following months learning the difference between outside and somewhere. By the time he arrived in Coldwater he had begun to understand that the difference was other people, and a desk under a window, and a kitchen where someone put a cup at your place before you sat down.

He thought about Felix K. He thought about whether Felix K., at the end of the breakwater, had believed he was nearly arrived, or had understood that he was not going to arrive at all. He thought about whether it mattered which of those two things Felix K. had believed, in October, in the dark, at the end of a granite breakwater on a coast he had not known long enough to name.

He did not know whether it mattered.

He did not know whether the man on the breakwater was Felix K.

The notebook was on the desk. The question he had left in it — *What do I intend to do with this?* — did not answer itself in the dark. It would be there in the morning. It would be there next week. It would be there until he found something more to write below it.

He went to bed.
