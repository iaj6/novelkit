# Zuhause

The cows were restless through most of December. It was the dark, or the cold, or the particular quality of deep winter that animals seemed to register before people did and which manifested in the barn as a low-grade generalized unease — not sickness, nothing clinical, just the small signs of discomfort she had learned to read in the years she'd been doing the four-thirty milking. Hannah talked to them through the work the same as always: the low reassuring murmur that barn work required, not words exactly, just sound, the human voice as its own kind of calm. She had been doing this since she was old enough to carry a pail. She would probably do it when she was fifty.

The radio was on before she got back to the kitchen. It had been on since December sixteenth, which was the morning she'd heard the word Ardennes over breakfast and watched her father set down his coffee and not pick it up again. The Ardennes was a place she knew from Harton's atlas — a forest on the German border with Belgium, which had nothing to do with Maine, which was therefore precisely the place her father would listen to for twelve hours straight. The German army had pushed through it, or was still pushing, the news confused in the first days, the correspondents using a kind of controlled uncertainty that told you the situation on the ground was more uncertain than the broadcasts could sustain.

Her father was at the kitchen set in the mornings and the parlor set in the evenings, and on the twenty-second he had both going at once, a thing she had not seen since the summer of 1942 when the tankers were burning off Cape Cod. The Ardennes was different from that. The tankers were the war coming here — a thing to be frightened of in the specific way of physical nearness, the fire visible on the horizon, the smell of it. The Ardennes was the war going there: to the country he had left in 1922, to the fields that were not Maine fields, to whatever was or wasn't in the Bavarian farmland he had not heard from in two years, perhaps longer. She had lost count of when the letters had stopped and could not remember if he had ever told her exactly.

She made breakfast and brought him coffee. He said danke and went back to listening.

Karl came to the barn willingly, the same as always. He moved through the milking stalls with the economy of someone who had worked animals since before he could read. Hannah moved through them the same way, on the opposite side, and the work got done in the barn's usual quiet. They had not spoken much since October. No particular thing had been said to stop it — the winter had done it, the way cold does, slowing things to stillness and leaving them to start again when the air changed. She knew where it had been and she knew it was still there.

She went to school on the days she went, which in December was most of them and in January was four out of five — the road iced over on Tuesdays and Wednesdays of the second week, and she stayed to do the morning work and then the evening, and the school day was somewhere else, occurring without her. Marcus Klein would have set her the reading. He did not make a particular occasion of her absences, which she appreciated.

---

The Bulge resolved in the Allies' favor by the end of January. The papers said it and the radio said it and Harton's geography students moved the pins back across the map in the school hallway, east and east again until the line was approximately where it had been before December sixteenth. Hannah watched this happen over three mornings on her way to German class. The resolution was accurate. The satisfaction it produced in her was not equal to the relief she had expected and she did not know what to do with that, so she left it.

Her father was not better.

She had let herself believe that the Bulge resolving would move something in him the way the tides moved things. In and then out, the particular pressure going and the next thing arriving. But the Bulge going had left him quieter, not more open, and the quality of his stillness had changed in a way she had to look at carefully to name. He was not sad, or not only sad. He was the face of a man who had handed something away and was now in the absence of the thing. She did not have a German word for it. She did not have an English one either.

He sat at the radio less. Her mother moved through the kitchen with the efficiency she had maintained across two years of silence: bringing him coffee in the parlor without being asked, the way she communicated everything now, by action without word. Hannah had learned to read the quality of her mother's movements the same way she had learned to read her father's face — it had simply taken different practice.

What Hannah wanted to say to her father was not a question.

She had questions. She had accumulated them for three years of living inside a house that had reorganized itself around two different silences. Whether there was family still in Bavaria that she'd been told about too young to remember. What he heard when he listened to the radio and what it cost him to hear it. What the German word was for whatever had happened to him when the war started and then went on and then the letters stopped. These were questions and she had given up expecting answers to them — not in despair, just as a practical matter, the same way you gave up expecting a certain kind of weather in February and instead worked with what February brought.

What she wanted to say was not a question.

She wanted to say: she knew about him and Karl. The icehouse window, the evenings, the particular register she had heard in her father's voice — the third register, neither barn German nor supper German but something slower, with more room in it, the pace of two people finding shared ground. She had known since spring and had said nothing because she had not known how to say it without seeming to offer something she was not offering. She did not want to make it strange, the way saying the name of something ordinary can suddenly make it strange.

She wanted to say: she spoke German now. Not Morgen, not the supper-table yes-father words. She had spoken German to Karl in the barn in April, forty words, and he had answered, and she had understood, and she had not been afraid of the language. She had been afraid of other things — whether Lonzo would come in, what her father's face would do if he saw — but not of the language itself. She had been speaking it at the fence through the fall, the ordinary German of farm questions and weather, the language settling into her the same way the barn language had settled in when she was three and the cow language when she was four. It was hers now. It had always been hers.

She had not said this to anyone in the farmhouse. She had walked through this house for eight months with the language in her and had not brought it into the same room as her father's language, as if they were two things that should be kept separate, as if the fence that kept Karl in the paddock also ran through the kitchen. By mid-January she recognized this as a kind of cowardice she had dressed in the wrong name. She had been calling it caution. It was not caution.

---

On a Thursday evening in late January she came in from the barn and stopped in the mudroom. She still had her coat on. The lantern was in her left hand. Outside the mudroom window the cold was absolute, the temperature well below what it had been when she went out, the sky clear and indifferent. She had not planned Thursday. Thursday had simply arrived at the end of enough other Thursdays.

The kitchen was lit by the overhead and the stove's firebox glow. Her father was at the table. Not beside the radio — the radio was on the counter, its dial barely amber, turned so low it was barely registering, just the habit of having it on that he had not fully let go since December. He was sitting with both hands around his coffee cup. He was looking at the table's grain. She had seen this posture many times in the last year: turned inward toward whatever country existed behind his eyes. Not empty. He was never empty. Somewhere.

Her mother was at the far end of the table with her darning. The sock on the needle was Lonzo Reed's — she had been mending the farm socks since November when Lonzo held out a heel with a hole in it and Magda had taken it from him and nodded. The darning gave her hands something. Hannah thought of it as that, and perhaps her mother did too.

Hannah set the lantern on its peg. She came into the kitchen. She sat down across from her father.

She said, in German — his German, the Bavarian she had grown up hearing through the floor and at the supper table and in the barn when she was close enough to catch it: Papa. Ich muss dir was sagen.

Her father looked up.

He had not heard her speak German to him — deliberately, directly, aimed at him across a table — since before the war. Since she was fourteen at the oldest and it was still the house's ordinary language, before the ordinary had ended. He looked up and his face was the face she had been learning to read. She thought she was closer to it now than she had ever been, which was different from having it fully.

He said: Dann sag es.

She said: she knew about him and Karl. The window. The evenings. That Karl was from fifty kilometers from where he was born, that she knew that and had known it, and that she knew it was not nothing. She said: I speak German now. Not the words I grew up with. I speak it. I have been speaking it for eight months and I was afraid to say so to you, and I'm not afraid anymore.

He was quiet.

She let him be quiet. The stove worked. At the far end of the table her mother went on with the sock, the needle moving in the same small arc, steady. The clock on the shelf above the icebox ticked through the room — the clock with the white face and the brass pendulum that her father wound every Sunday morning the same time as the barn, the first task of the week. She could hear it clearly now.

He said, in German, more slowly than his German usually moved, as if he were finding each word before he let it into the sentence: Du hast das von mir.

She said: Ja.

He said: Ich weiß das.

A pause. She had learned, in three years of this house, that some pauses were meant to be filled and some were themselves the conversation — the space the other person needed to breathe in. She held this one.

Then, quietly — in English, the language he had not spoken to her, not once, not even in passing, in more than two years — he said: "Hannah."

Just her name.

Her mother did not look up from the darning. But her hands were still.

---

Hannah went to bed.

She lay in the dark and the farm settled around her the way it settled in deep winter: the boards in the cold, the cows in the barn making the low sustained sound they made at night, the clock sending its voice through two walls and a ceiling. She had been falling asleep in this room her whole life and had never heard the clock so clearly, and she could not say why tonight was different except that it was.

She did not know what her father's name in English meant. She knew too many things it might mean, and too many things it couldn't mean, and she could not sort the combinations in a single night. She did not try very hard. She was eighteen and tired from the barn and from what she had done at the kitchen table, and the tiredness was not unpleasant.

She knew this: he had said it. He had found the language he had set down two years ago and picked it back up long enough to say her name. He had not offered a reason or a gesture toward reason. He had just said her name, in English, at the kitchen table in January, and the English was real — not a slip — real and deliberate and directed at her specifically, which was the person it had always been for.

She thought about the word zuhause.

She had been trying for years to know what it meant in this house. Her father's German and her mother's silence and Karl through the icehouse window speaking to her father in the register of a young man learning from an older one, and the school German that was Hochdeutsch when she expected Bavarian, and the barn German that was in between, and the language at the fence with Karl that had gradually stopped being between anything and started being its own thing. She had been looking for the word that covered all of it and finding that no single word did, that what fit Bavaria didn't fit Maine and what fit Maine didn't fit the kitchen in the evenings when everything was German and she was standing at the edge of it.

Tonight it meant the kitchen in January. The stove's glow. The clock that her father wound every Sunday and which kept time the way it kept time, neither fast nor slow, steady. Her mother's hands going still over the sock. Her father at the table, both hands around his coffee, looking up at her with the face she was learning to read — and then the English word, her name, the one he had always known and had just now said again.

Zuhause.

She went to sleep.
