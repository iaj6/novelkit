# March

The frost went out of the ground in stages, which meant the farm got worse before it got better. The barn lane turned to mud first — knee-deep over frozen sub-soil, the upper layer thawing while the ground beneath stayed hard. You came back from the morning milking with your boots caked to the calf and the cows' hooves thick with it and the whole yard smelling of what came up when the earth loosened. The fence posts heaved. They did this every year, the same posts in the same corners, lifted half an inch or more out of true by the freeze and what came after it. She knocked them back down with the mallet and drove new staples into the wire and they would heave again the following year. That was mud season. It had been mud season before she could name it and it would be mud season after.

The east pasture wall had pushed out a corner stone that her father reset on a March morning before anyone was up. She found it back in place when she came out for the milking — the mud around the base still disturbed, the stone sitting level the way it had sat for twenty years. She did not mention it to him and he would not have said anything either.

The radio ran most mornings. It had run through the whole war, kitchen radio and then the parlor radio too during the worst of the Bulge, but the way her father sat with it had changed. Through the Bulge he had sat at the radio the way you sit at a thing you cannot put down and cannot bear to hold. Now he sat the way you sit beside something you have decided to witness. In early March — a Tuesday, she was in the middle of the milking — she had heard through the window he had cracked despite the cold that the First Army had crossed the Rhine at Remagen. The end was approaching. Not here. Somewhere behind all the silence the war had made.

Some evenings he sat for the German-language broadcast. He sat for it the way he would sit for a letter from a person he had given up expecting a letter from.

The icehouse had been occupied since April 1943. Twenty-three months, now in its twenty-fourth. She had stopped counting somewhere in the fall.

Lonzo Reed had come out on a Thursday with papers from Camp Houlton — the directive from the War Department about the repatriation process, forwarded through the duty officer at the primary facility. She had watched him explain it to her father in the yard: Lonzo with his sheaf of forms, using the patient over-loud English he used when he thought he was being understood, her father nodding slowly with no expression. After Lonzo left her father stood in the yard for a moment and looked at the icehouse and then at the barn and then at the house. Then he went inside. She had the milk pails to scrub and she went back to them.

She knew what the papers said. When Germany surrendered, the satellite details would reassemble at Camp Houlton for processing and then be staged for transport back to Germany in phases. Timing unspecified. Weeks, maybe. Not months.

---

Hannah was alone at the north-pasture fence with the mallet and a coffee can of staples and the wire stretcher. The second post in from the corner. It had heaved last November and last February and now it had heaved again — the same six-inch lift, the same wire gone slack between it and its neighbor, the same ground doing what ground does.

She had knocked it back down and was setting the first staple when she heard him.

"Du weißt, dass wir gehen."

Not a question.

She set the staple first. Two strokes of the mallet, the wire seated, and then she straightened and looked at him across the fence.

He was standing on the pasture side with his hands in his coat pockets, not close to the wire, not leaning on it. The same wool coat he had come in with, re-stitched at the collar where it had separated sometime in the second winter. He had been eighteen when he arrived. He was twenty now. She could see it — something settled in the way he held himself that had not been there at the start.

She said: "Ich hab's gehört."

He nodded. He said: a month, maybe. Maybe two. No one knows the exact date. But it's coming.

She turned back to the fence. She worked the wire stretcher on the next section and felt the wire tighten against the cold wood. Behind him across the field the icehouse was visible — the south corner, the stovepipe, the thin column of woodsmoke that bent east in the wind off the pasture. She moved her hands along the wire to the next staple position.

He crossed to the fence and picked up the piece of fencing wire she had set down on the post rail and held it against the staple position, without her asking him to.

She looked at his hands on the wire. She looked at the post.

She said: "Hast du Angst zurückzugehen?"

He was quiet for a while. Long enough that she drove the staple and moved to the next section before he answered. The field behind him was still brown from winter, the grass flat and pale, the mud season not yet reached here at the pasture's edge where the drainage was better.

He said: I don't know what I'm going back to.

She said: you know what you're going back to. She caught her own English and left it.

He said: a village and a farm and my mother. Yes. And then: but not what the village is now. Not what the country is now. I was — he stopped. He started again. I was eighteen twenty-three months ago. I'm twenty now. He stopped there.

She waited.

She moved to the next staple position and he moved the wire with her, holding it against the post, his hands on one side of the fence, her hands on the other.

She said: "Wir sind noch hier."

He said: "Du bist."

They worked the last stretch without talking. She set the staples and he held the wire and she drove each one home. Two strokes, clean. The same motion — the wire against the post, her hand with the mallet. She watched the work and thought about nothing in particular and the cold was in her hands and it was March and he was leaving.

Then Lonzo came around the icehouse corner with his collar turned up, heading toward the barn, and he saw them at the fence and nodded the nod he used for everything he had decided to accept without comment. Karl stepped back from the wire.

She said: "Auf Wiedersehen."

He said: "Ja." And then he walked back across the brown field and met Lonzo at the barn door.

She picked up the coffee can and the wire stretcher and the mallet and carried them back toward the barn.

---

That evening her mother went to bed at eight, the same as most evenings since October. Her father sat with the kitchen radio until past nine, the volume low enough that she could not hear the words from the table, only the shape of the broadcast — the rhythm and register of German, the particular cadence of a voice reading prepared text in that language in 1945. Then he turned it off and set it back on the shelf and went upstairs. The house around her went quiet in the way a farmhouse in March goes quiet: the barn settling, the cows moving in their sleep, the wind off the east field, and the stove ticking as it cooled.

Hannah was at the kitchen table with her school notebook. She had carried it down thinking she would get through the Latin — three weeks of declension exercises she had been meaning to do since January — but instead she opened it to the Whitman annotation section, which was where she kept the work that was not Latin.

Between the last annotation page and the notebook's back cover. Folded in thirds. She had put it there in February of the previous year.

She took it out and set it on the table. She did not unfold it right away. She looked at the kitchen instead: the clock above the stove, the shelf where the radio had been until her father moved it toward the table and then moved it back again, the jar of wooden spoons her mother had placed in the empty space. The hanging lamp put a circle of warm light over the table and the notebook. Outside the window, the yard was dark.

She unfolded the letter.

The paper had changed from being held often — not worn through, just altered at the creases, the fold lines soft now in a way they had not been when she found it in the hay in February 1944. Fourteen months ago. She smoothed it against the table.

The handwriting ran in close lines. She had looked at it often enough to know its shapes: the way the G opened at the top, the r that crossed almost into an n, the period pressed hard into the paper at each sentence's end.

*die die versucht haben zu gehen, sind weg. Wir beten für sie.*

She read it. Those words on that paper in this kitchen with the stove going cool. Marcus had given her what it said in English, in January of last year, and she had known most of it before then — her father's German at the supper table had been reaching her for seventeen years. Those who tried to go are gone. We pray for them.

She had held this letter for fourteen months. She had taken it from the hay by accident and then continued taking it by choice. She had told Marcus she had it. She had not told her father. She had not told Karl.

But Karl was going. A month, maybe. Two.

She was going to give it back to him before the truck came. She had not known until tonight, sitting here with the letter on the table, that this was already the decision.

She didn't know what she would say when she handed it to him. She would find out. Maybe she would say only: this is yours.

She folded the letter along its old lines — the paper fell into them without resistance — and put it back between the annotation pages and closed the notebook.

The clock ticked. The stove had gone cold enough to bank for the night. She should get up and do that. She sat for a moment longer.
