# The Zenith

The Crow house was quiet when Marcus turned onto the back street at twenty minutes past four, the same time he always turned onto it on a Tuesday in June. He had stayed after the last Latin class to review a student's Cicero translation — the girl had mistaken a subjunctive for an indicative and the meaning had shifted in a way that was grammatically precise and entirely wrong — and he had marked it with a note instead of a mark, because at a certain level of wrongness the mark would not tell her anything useful about what she had misread and the note might. He was still thinking about this, about the difference between correcting a mistake and explaining its interior logic, when he heard it.

The parlor window was open two inches and through it the Zenith's voice reached him across the front yard and the porch: not music, but a man speaking carefully and without pause, in the register that radio voices took on when the news was large. He had learned this register since December 1941 — the slight slowing, the particular economy between phrases, as if the voice understood what it was carrying and was choosing to carry it with a certain care.

He came up the porch steps. He could hear the words now.

*Allied forces have landed on the coast of France. Units of the American, British, and Canadian armies, under the supreme command of General Dwight Eisenhower, have this morning begun the liberation of Western Europe.*

He opened the door and stood in the hall.

Through the parlor doorway: Henry was in his chair, still in his work clothes, his cap on the side table beside him. Lillian was in the doorway between the kitchen and the parlor with a dish towel held in both hands in front of her in the manner of something forgotten.

Neither of them looked at him.

Marcus set his briefcase on the floor beside the umbrella stand. He crossed the hall. He sat on the parlor sofa without taking off his coat.

---

The CBS broadcast ran through five and into six. At some point Marcus stood and turned the dial, past a farm report from Portland and two stations of static, until the BBC came in — clear and precise, the presenter carrying his characteristic quality of British reserve in the service of very large information. He went back to the sofa. From here he could manage the dial with a single step; he reached over several times during the six o'clock hour to correct for drift, the BBC signal restless on its frequency.

Lillian brought supper at half past six. She had made a pot roast with carrots and there were biscuits, and she carried everything in on a tray and set it on the low parlor table without asking whether anyone wanted to eat in the parlor. She had moved the table from its usual position against the wall and put two chairs alongside the sofa, so all three of them could sit without being far from the radio. This seemed to Marcus an act of considerable domestic intelligence.

He ate without being particularly aware of it.

They ate at the parlor table, the Zenith between them and the wall, the broadcasters alternating their accounts of the morning in France. The CBS man had a tendency toward summary; the BBC presenter organized by geography, moving from beach to beach along the coast in a way that Marcus found he could almost follow on the map he held in his head, though the map had gaps. He had never been to Normandy. He knew the coastline by its name and its latitude and the general shape of it on paper, but he could not place himself in it, could not see the particular quality of the sand or the height of the sea cliffs or the angle of the morning light coming in from the east.

He tried to imagine it and got only the radio's language.

Lillian refilled his coffee cup at seven o'clock without asking — real coffee, not the substitute that the ration book pushed you toward, the kind she made on evenings when something required it. He accepted it and set it on the table and forgot about it until it was cold and drank it cold. It was still good.

---

He found it by accident at nine o'clock, turning the dial during a CBS break.

He was looking for the BBC — it had drifted during the eight o'clock hour and he had been tracking it incrementally, degree by degree of the dial — when he passed through a frequency and heard something under the English signal. Not alongside it. Under it, as if two broadcasts occupied nearly the same position and one of them was losing ground.

German.

He rotated the dial back slowly until both were audible: the English on top, the German below it, the two signals in an involuntary conversation. The BBC European service — or something like it — the transmission aimed east, toward the occupied territories, designed for listeners with clandestine receivers hidden in farmhouse cellars and back-room closets, who were listening in a way that had consequences if they were caught listening. The German was formal and deliberate, the register of a language designed to be received once by someone in a dangerous position and understood immediately.

He listened.

The sentences were complete, clear, not overlapped by the English signal in any way that made them inaudible to someone who knew the language.

Henry said, "What's that other voice?"

"A German broadcast," Marcus said. "The BBC. For occupied Europe."

Lillian had come back from the kitchen with a dish towel, which she set on the arm of Henry's chair. She said nothing.

The German ran alongside the English for another thirty seconds — close enough on the dial that moving away from one would lose the other — and then the English signal gathered itself and the German reduced to something barely present. In the time it was clear:

Henry said, "What are they saying?"

Marcus translated without looking away from the radio. "They are saying the Allied landing was conducted at multiple points along the coast in the early morning hours, before the German commanders understood what was happening. They are saying the initial German assessment was that the landing was a diversion, not the primary operation." He stopped, listening. "They are saying the element of surprise —"

The broadcast shifted. The CBS signal reasserted itself and the German was gone; what remained was the American presenter saying something about airborne divisions and the pre-dawn hours and the fields behind the beaches. Marcus released the dial. He sat back on the sofa.

Lillian sat down in the armchair by the lamp, which she had turned low at seven when the evening had settled into itself. Henry was in his chair, his hands in his lap, his expression the one Marcus had come to understand as Henry's form of total attention — it was not demonstrative and could be read, by someone who didn't know him, as merely neutral; it was the furthest thing from neutral.

The three of them sat in the low lamplight with the Zenith speaking at the wall.

---

He had not been trying to think about Paris.

His job in this room was to track the dial, to translate if there was anything that needed translating, to be present to what was happening on the radio in the way that this household had decided to be present to it together rather than alone. He was not trying to think about Paris. But Paris was present in the way that certain things were present on such evenings: not summoned, not avoidable, simply there.

He had been in Paris for twenty-four months, from the spring of 1938 until May 1940. He had left before the Germans came — made the calculation in May and acted on it, not knowing at the time whether the calculation was right, not knowing it was right until several weeks afterward when the information began arriving about the people who had not made the same calculation. So Paris for him was not June 1940. He had not seen the flags in the Place de la Concorde or the soldiers coming down the Champs-Élysées. He had the twenty-four months before it: the reading room at the Bibliothèque nationale, where a reader's card was the only requirement and a man could sit from nine in the morning until five in the afternoon and the staff required nothing further. The Sainte-Chapelle in November. A café near the Odéon where he had spent several evenings working on a chapter about Klopstock, the coffee good and the other patrons uninterested in him, which was its own form of grace.

The Louvre. He had been to the Louvre more times in twenty-four months than he had been to the Kunsthistorisches Museum in ten years in Vienna — something he had not anticipated of himself, that temporary residence in a city made him attend to it more carefully than permanent residence, as if the known deadline of departure organized the seeing.

German soldiers had been in the Louvre for four years.

He was hearing, on the Zenith, that this might be beginning to change — that the soldiers might leave the streets of Paris if the battle at Normandy moved in the direction the announcer's careful optimism implied. He listened to the optimism as a document: the radio presenter knew, tonight, which things he was authorized to say and which he was not, and so the shape of the optimism told him something about the shape of the uncertainty. The optimism was real but measured. The uncertainty was real and larger.

A colleague from the École pratique des hautes études who had gone to London in the spring of 1940, whose current address Marcus did not have and had not had for three years. A street that someone's cousin had known, the name of which Marcus had written on a piece of paper once and then lost. The apartment in Vienna that had been taken over by whoever took over such apartments, where a monograph on Klopstock sat in a drawer, or had been turned to other uses, or had been thrown away.

He had been not-knowing about the colleagues for four years. The not-knowing had accumulated a specific gravity over that time — different from the not-knowing of the early months, when not-knowing still felt like a condition that would resolve. It had not resolved. It had become the form in which he held the people he had known before 1938: probable but unverifiable, present in the room as a weight rather than as a fact.

He turned the dial. Found the BBC. Left it there.

Henry reached forward and adjusted the lamp. The room settled.

---

At ten o'clock the news became a different kind of news.

In the first hours, information had been arriving as it was established — each fact landing with the specific weight of something genuinely new, the picture building in real time, the uncertainty alive. At ten o'clock the uncertainty had not resolved but the shape of the day had solidified, and what the radio was doing now was the work of assembly: the same facts fitted into a chronology, the event having happened, the urgency dropping slightly and in its place something more like record. Still important. Not the same kind of important.

Lillian gathered the cups and the last of the supper things and took them to the kitchen. She came back and stood in the parlor doorway for a moment.

"I'm going up," she said.

"All right," Henry said.

She looked at Marcus in the particular way she looked at him on evenings when she was concerned. "Get some sleep, Marcus," she said.

"I will," he said. "Thank you, Lillian."

She went upstairs. They listened to the sounds of her on the second floor: the hallway, the bathroom, the bedroom door, the familiar sequence of the house settling itself.

Henry reached over and turned the Zenith down to just above silence. The announcer continued at that volume without mass, a presence in the room rather than an account. The lamp was still on but low. The chairs were in something near their usual positions. The supper things were gone.

They sat.

---

Henry said, "I'm going to sit out on the porch a while."

He stood and went to the door. Marcus got his coat from the sofa arm and followed.

The night was warm in the way that June nights in Maine were rarely warm — warm meaning simply that it did not require defending against, that the air lay around you without urgency. The back street ran dark between the houses under the elm canopy; above it the sky held its midsummer blue even at this hour, more blue than black, some reserve of daylight still in the horizon to the north that the season refused to entirely give up.

Mrs. Pelletier's lamp was on across the way. Otherwise the street was quiet.

Henry sat in the left chair. Marcus sat in the right. It was the arrangement they had, established without discussion. He had been in this house for two years and three months and this porch was one of the things in Coldwater that had become, in a precise and undramatic way, his.

The elm leaves were still. Down the block a screen door opened and closed.

They were quiet for some time.

Henry said: "You have people there?"

Marcus considered this. He said: "I did. I don't know."

Henry nodded. He didn't follow it with another question.

The screen door was quiet. In the elm's upper branches, some movement of air too light to reach the porch shifted the leaves briefly and then stopped.

Marcus said, after a while: "It's a long way to go."

Henry said: "It is."

He was talking about the invasion — the distance the soldiers had crossed in the dark before dawn, the Channel, the boats, the beach they had crossed on foot. Marcus was talking about that too. He was also talking about his particular relation to the distance between here and France, between this porch and a city he had lived in and left before it became something other than itself. And he was aware, as he said the words, that Henry's relationship to the distance between here and somewhere else was not the same as his — that Norfolk, Virginia, and the distance between Coldwater Reach and there, and whatever lay in the country between those two coordinates, was a different distance from Marcus's distance even when they arrived at similar things: the not-knowing about people, the specific quality of being somewhere that required managing, the particular alertness one developed in a town that was one's only within certain limits.

Another quarter-hour. The elm leaves. The street quiet under the midsummer sky.

---

He went back inside at midnight.

The Zenith was still on in the parlor, turned to its lowest setting, the announcer's voice carrying across the room without weight. Marcus turned it off on his way through. It seemed wrong to leave it talking to an empty room when what it had to say tonight had already been said and what remained was only the saying of it again.

The house was dark. Henry had come in a few minutes before him and the downstairs was settled.

He went upstairs.

His room was the way he had left it in the morning: the chair by the east window, the books on the desk arranged in the order he had put them in March and not disturbed since, the shortwave on the corner table with its dial dark. He had not turned it on all evening. What would it find that the Zenith had not already given him — what frequency between here and Europe would carry something the CBS presenter had not already said, that the BBC hadn't organized into its careful English, that the German signal, briefly audible under the English, had not already delivered in the register designed for people in danger?

He sat in the chair by the window without turning on the lamp.

Through the glass: the back street below, the elm canopy, and above it the sky, which even at this hour was holding its blue. He had not expected this of Maine when he arrived — that the sky refused its full dark for most of June, that the light had a longer argument to make than it did in Vienna or Paris or London, though London was different because of the blackout, because the dark in London had been enforced rather than natural, a different kind of dark altogether.

The window faced east.

He was aware of this in a way he was not usually aware of it: east across the town and east across the harbor and east across the Atlantic was France, and the men on the beach at Normandy, and the German soldiers withdrawing from certain positions, and somewhere beyond that, Paris — the reading room, the café near the Odéon, the colleagues whose current situations he did not know. He had been not-knowing about them for four years, and the quality of the not-knowing had changed as it accumulated, had become something he carried differently than he had carried it in 1940 or 1941 or 1942. He was not sure whether hearing that this might be ending would change the quality of the not-knowing, or whether it would only change the shape of what he did not know, which was not the same as knowing.

He did not open the notebook.

What was in the notebook was already there — the entries in shorthand, the arithmetic, the question at the page's bottom that had been there since April and was not going to answer itself tonight.

He sat in the chair until the cold came in through the old window frame — June cold, not significant, but enough at two in the morning to make itself present. He stood. He went to the bed and lay down in the room's dark without the lamp, without the shortwave, without the notebook.
