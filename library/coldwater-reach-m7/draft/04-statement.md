# Statement

The auxiliary station had left a note.

Lt. R. Ennis, USCG, would attend the harbormaster's station on Wednesday the twentieth at nine a.m., regarding the formal statement in the matter of the body found October 12. It was written in pencil on a half-sheet with the station address at the top. The signature at the bottom belonged to someone named Osgood.

She read it Monday morning when she came in and set it on the corner of the desk.

The radio check at eight came in clear. She logged the conditions — wind WNW eleven knots, barometer 30.1 rising, tide low at six-forty-two — and went out to walk the dock in Owen's peacoat, which was enough coat for October and only just. The fog hadn't lifted; the breakwater showed the first hundred yards and then grayed out, the fixed light not yet switched on for the day. She walked dock head to end, checked the line on Pellerin's lobster boat, which had been sitting since Thursday, and found it holding. The fenders needed adjusting; she adjusted them. Through the *Callie B.*'s wheelhouse window she read the fuel gauge at three-quarters and wrote it in the log. At the far end of the dock, in the shallows near the seine sheds, the inner cove had put down a skin of ice overnight — thin and gray, early for October, the kind that a shift in wind would break before noon. She checked the cleat at the dock's end, which had been working loose since the blow, and found it had tightened on its own. She wrote that too, and went back inside.

The *Mabel Lena*'s winter registration was due November fifteenth and Clarence Pelletier hadn't come in to sign. She wrote a reminder. There was a discrepancy in the September fuel-allocation figures she needed to correct before the Portland form arrived; she worked through the log and found the error and noted the correction in the margin. She ran the noon check. She went out to re-seat the anemometer mount at the dock's end, which had been reading low since the blow preceding the twelfth, and came back in and made the entry: *anemometer mount re-seated; estimated variance in readings Oct 10–17 not to exceed 1.5 knots.* She dated it.

The note sat on the corner of the desk.

On Tuesday, Farber appeared around eight-thirty, coming down to check on the *Callie B.* before the next weather system moved back in. He thought he might run out Thursday if it held. She told him the pump was running clean, that she'd checked it herself Monday. He stood with his hand on the rail and said he'd heard the Coast Guard was coming Wednesday for her statement, about that business on the breakwater. She said yes, Wednesday morning. He said it was a peculiar thing, a man like that, no name or nothing. She said yes. He waited a moment, and then nodded and went down to his boat.

She did the Tuesday items. Tide charts through the end of October. The freight manifest for Friday's mail boat. The second fuel-log discrepancy, different from Monday's, which she tracked back through three weeks of entries to find its source.

On Tuesday afternoon she glanced at the note on the corner of the desk.

Wednesday morning, before she cleared the papers, she glanced at it a second time.

---

Wednesday she was at the shack by seven. The morning was cold and clear, the fog gone overnight, the breakwater showing its full length against a gray sky with the fixed light off in the daylight, the granite dark with the tide. She ran the eight o'clock check and logged conditions — wind WNW eight knots, barometer 30.2 steady, tide high ten thirty-four — and noted that the skin ice on the inner cove had persisted through Tuesday and thickened slightly overnight. She cleared the papers from the desk and moved the harbor log to the windowsill.

At nine she heard boots on the planking.

Ennis came in with a yeoman — a young man of about twenty-two with a Remington portable in a case — and the shack became a different size than it had been. The yeoman set the Remington on the desk, which took the left half of the work surface, and there was no useful place for anything else on it, so the harbor log stayed on the windowsill. The yeoman sat in the chair near the radio set, put paper in the machine, and waited. Ennis stood near the window with his clipboard. Eira stood near the stove. The arrangement sorted itself without comment.

Ennis was in his mid-forties, clean-shaven, in uniform with the rank on his collar. He had the look of a man who had made the drive down from Portland and intended to complete the task and make the drive back. He was not hostile. He was, in the particular manner of a man from a larger office with a form to complete, correct. He explained the process: statement on the events of October eleventh and the morning of the twelfth, typed as they went, reviewed and signed before departure. He looked at the clipboard while he spoke.

She said that was fine.

---

He began at the beginning.

What time had she arrived at the dock the evening of October eleventh.

Twenty-two forty-five, approximately.

Her purpose for being at the dock at that hour.

She had come to check the bilge pump on the *Callie B.*, registered to Reg Farber, second slip. A slow leak at the port quarter since early October; she had been monitoring it. She had checked it on the eighth and again on the eleventh.

Had the pump been running.

Running clean.

How long had she remained at the dock.

Until twenty-three thirty, approximately.

What had she observed during that time on the dock or on the breakwater.

She had not observed any individual on the dock or on the breakwater.

Had she observed any vessel not previously seen in the harbor.

She had not.

The yeoman typed. Each answer went in, the carriage returned, and the shack was quiet again until Ennis found the next item on the form.

Had she logged the dock visit.

No. A bilge-pump check on a vessel already in the harbor log didn't require a separate entry.

Was there any entry in the harbor log for the evening of October eleventh indicating unusual activity.

No.

Had she observed any unusual activity in the harbor between approximately twenty-two hundred hours and midnight.

No.

Had any other personnel been present on the dock during that time, to her knowledge.

No.

Was the visibility sufficient, given the conditions that evening, to observe the full length of the dock.

The fog had been moderate and the dock lights were on. She could see to the head of the breakwater steps from the *Callie B.*'s position at the second slip.

Ennis made a note on the form and looked at the next section.

He asked whether the dock lighting at the far end — near the breakwater steps — would have made it difficult to observe activity in that area.

She said yes. The lights were kept low.

Was that standard practice.

Standard practice since the summer of 1942, she said, per First District guidance. The dock lights at the seaward end had been reduced to fifteen watts. The breakwater's fixed light was on its regular schedule.

He wrote something. He was not looking at her while he wrote — his attention was at the form, at the space allocated for the answer. She watched him make the check beside the item. She heard the tide clock on the shelf and she didn't need to look at it but she looked at it anyway. Nine-seventeen. He moved to the next section.

---

He said he wanted to share what the investigation had developed, as a matter of courtesy to the harbormaster's office.

She said to go ahead.

The man had no papers, Ennis said. No wallet. No identification of any kind — nothing military, nothing naval, nothing issued by any civilian authority they had been able to identify. His clothing was civilian: a dark wool overcoat, a dress shirt. Nothing to indicate a branch or a service. The overcoat was of a quality suggesting a city rather than a working coast; the shirt collar had been intact, which was notable given the overall condition. He appeared to be in his forties or older, medium build. He was not, by any information the Coast Guard or the local inquiry had produced, a man from Coldwater or from the neighboring towns. He had been in the water or in severe weather conditions for some considerable time before reaching the breakwater — the physical evidence was consistent with exposure over several days, corresponding to the storm pattern in the days preceding October twelfth. The investigation was not formally closed, but there was at present no identifying information sufficient to support active inquiry. The record would be held at Portland. If she came across any additional information in her capacity as harbormaster, she should contact the auxiliary station.

She said she understood.

She did not ask how far such a man might have come in three days of weather, or what was on this stretch of coast if you worked it backward from Coldwater. It was not her investigation. The record was in Portland.

---

The yeoman pulled the form from the typewriter — three carbons — and set the original and one copy on the edge of the desk. Ennis put his clipboard on the windowsill beside the harbor log.

She read the typed statement. Her name, her position, her years of appointment. Wednesday, October twentieth, nineteen forty-three. The events of October eleventh and twelfth as she had given them: the dock, the bilge pump, the time arrived, the time departed, nothing unusual observed. She read each line for correspondence between what was written and what was known to be true. There was no line that was inaccurate. Each statement was a statement she had made and each corresponded to an event that had occurred. She signed the original and the carbon and set the pen down.

Ennis thanked her. He shook her hand — the grip of a man who had processed a task — and nodded to the yeoman, who was already putting the Remington in its case.

She held the door.

Ennis went down the dock at the pace of a man with a drive ahead of him. The yeoman, slower with the typewriter case, went past her with a nod that meant nothing particular. She watched them reach the dock end and turn toward the road and go from sight around the corner of the seine sheds.

---

The shack was the shack again. The radio set on the south wall. The harbor log on the windowsill. The tide clock on the shelf, reading past where it should read — she hadn't reset it Monday, she saw now, and hadn't reset it the Monday before either; the clock had been gaining since October twelfth, since before October twelfth, since the Monday when Farber's boy had come at a run and the morning had become something else. It had been gaining since then and she had not stopped to set it.

She moved the harbor log back to the desk.

The time was nine forty-three. The interview had run forty-three minutes.

She looked out the east window. The breakwater ran its full two hundred yards in the gray morning, the fixed light off in the daylight, the granite dark at the waterline with the incoming tide. A lobster boat was coming in through the outer channel with its running lights still on, the green on the starboard bow, reducing speed for the harbor entrance. She recognized the boat. She watched it complete the turn and did not write it down.

The case was, officially, in Portland's hands. There was a form with her name on it, accurate in every particular, and what it would produce when filed was the record of a harbormaster who had come to check a bilge pump on the evening of October eleventh and had observed nothing unusual. That was the record.

She took out the log and found the next empty line after the morning entry.

She wrote:

*October 20, 1943. Interview conducted with Lt. R. Ennis, USCG, re: body found October 12. Formal statement submitted. No additional information at this time.*

She dated the entry and initialed it.

She looked at what she had written.

Then she stood and took the tide clock from the shelf. She set it back four minutes. The clock settled to where it should read for the time of day, or near enough — she hadn't calculated exactly how much it had gained, and near enough was what she had. She put it back on the shelf.

She sat back down and took out the fuel allocation forms that needed updating before the end of the week, and she worked until noon.
