# The Breakwater

The tide clock read four minutes fast. Eira wound it — two full turns, the key stiff in the cold — and set the key back on the shelf above the radio. She would reset the hands before she went out. She wrote the clock's state in the log's margin, as she did every Monday morning: *4 min fast, 0603*.

It was not yet six-thirty. The three-day blow had passed since Saturday evening but the harbor was still working through what it had left behind: the inner cove choppy and gray, kelp strung along the base of the pilings in low brown lines, a skiff at the far end of the dock riding with more movement than it should. She could hear the bell buoy a quarter mile out working in the leftover swell. The foghorn at the outer bar gave its two-minute note, which she no longer heard as sound in any ordinary sense — she heard it only when it stopped.

She lit the stove, set the kettle on, and opened the log to a fresh page. October 12, 1943. Wind NNW, 9 knots, easing. Barometer 29.8, steady. Tide high 0647. She dated the entry, wrote her four lines, and put the pencil down.

The log was a leather-bound ledger, the spine re-glued twice, the earliest entry dated June 1891 in a script so cramped the ink had spread into the grain of the paper. Some pages were water-stained from a roof leak in 1929. Some had been removed at some point before Eira's tenure, for reasons she couldn't now determine. The entry for November 12, 1935 was Owen's. It stopped mid-sentence, the ink trailing off toward the right margin as though he had been interrupted and meant to come back to the page.

He had not.

She had never removed the page. She had kept the same ledger and carried the handwriting forward from where his stopped, and what was now in the book was eight years of her own entries accumulated behind Owen's unfinished last line.

She was wearing his peacoat. She had taken it in at the shoulders in the spring of 1936, raised the hem an inch, re-sewn the lining where it had frayed at the cuffs. The brass buttons were her own addition. She did not think of it as Owen's on most mornings. She thought of it as adequate to the cold.

The BC-1206 radio set occupied most of the south wall of the shack, its dial face pale gray in the early light. She ran the morning check at 0645 — call sign, Rockland auxiliary's acknowledgment, exchange logged — and then pulled the Friday mail from the harbor board that she hadn't gotten to yet. The mail boat came Tuesdays and Fridays. She was never as far behind as the Friday pile suggested and she was accustomed to catching up on Monday mornings.

Outside, the sky had gone from black to a flat pewter that was not quite light. She could see the dock in it, and the shapes of the boats riding in the cove, and the white line of the breakwater running out two hundred yards to where the fog took it. Nothing unusual in the harbor. Nothing on the dock.

She was working on the berthing notes when the door opened without a knock.

---

The boy was Farber's youngest. Fifteen, she thought — she had seen him on the dock in summers, trailing his father — but she didn't know his name. He had come at a run and the run was still in him, in the set of his shoulders and in the way he was breathing.

There was a man at the breakwater end, he said. He'd thought the man was sleeping.

He was not sleeping.

Eira set the pencil down.

Which end? she asked.

The seaward end. By the light.

She took her lantern off the shelf, touched a match to the wick, and adjusted the draft until the flame settled. She put on her hat.

She told the boy to go home and tell his father where he was.

He started to say something else.

Go home, she said.

---

Two hundred yards of granite — the WPA's work from 1937, sound and well laid, the mortar joints still tight where she had inspected them in September — and the fog had moved back in overnight after the blow lifted. It was the thick kind, low and coastal, the kind that reduced the world to the stone underfoot and two or three feet ahead. She walked with the lantern held out to one side rather than in front, which gave her more to see. She kept the fixed red at the seaward end to her left. In the fog it was not visible, but she knew to keep it there.

She did not hurry. There was nothing to hurry toward.

The harbor sounds fell away as she walked. The bell buoy receded to the north. The foghorn on the outer bar came to her from a different angle than it did from the shack — flattened somehow, altered by the distance and the direction of the wind. On the ocean side the swell was higher than on the harbor side; she could feel the difference underfoot, the granite absorbing each wave's weight and transmitting it up through her boots in a faint steady pulse. The surface of the stone was wet and dark. She watched her footing.

Twice she paused and listened. The harbor was quiet behind her. Nothing on the water that she could hear. The fog muffled the town on the hill, the houses and the road and the Congregational church bell, which she could usually hear at this hour if there was anything to hear. There was not.

The red light resolved out of the fog at the last moment, closer than she expected — not blinking, fixed, but the fog gave it a pulse that was not in the lamp itself. The light standard was a cast-iron post on a granite pedestal, eight feet total, with a Fresnel lens housing she had inspected and certified in September. She looked at the housing as she always looked at it. It was intact.

---

He was at the base of the pedestal. His back was against the granite, his legs extended before him on the stone, and he had the look of a man who had sat down to rest and had not gotten back up.

She set the lantern on the stone beside him and crouched.

He was not young. Fifty or hard forties, she thought, though the three days of cold and blow had not been gentle and made precision difficult. He had a good face — her mother's phrase, which meant the way the bones were arranged under the skin, the architecture of it. Good structure. The stubble on his jaw was gray and had been coming in for several days.

He wore a dark wool overcoat, double-breasted, heavy in the collar, cut in a way that belonged to a city somewhere else. Not a Maine coat. Not a fisherman's coat. The fabric had been fine wool before the blow finished with it; the collar lay correctly against his neck, turned with care, the way a man dresses when he has thought about how he should look. Under the coat she could see the edge of a white shirt collar, wet through and gray now. His shoes were leather, thin-soled, built for pavement. The salt had done them. He wore no ring. He had no bag. There was nothing around him on the stone — no pack, no case, nothing the tide might have brought up with him, though the storm's high-water line had left a thin debris of kelp and white foam along the breakwater's cove-side edge and he was well above it.

His hands were in his lap, the palms turned upward, the fingers slightly curled in the way hands go when the tension leaves them.

She looked at his hands for a moment. Then at his face. Then she picked up the lantern and walked back to the shack without looking back.

---

She raised Ennis at 0712 on the auxiliary frequency. One male, adult, deceased, seaward end of the Coldwater Reach breakwater. No identification visible. Ennis said they would be there by nine.

She logged the call and wrote 0900 in the margin. She crossed it out and wrote 0900 again more clearly.

Before he arrived she went out to the second slip and checked the bilge pump on the Callie B. Farber's dragger — four years old, with a slow leak at the port quarter that had been worse since the chop. She had checked the pump on Friday night before the blow and again on Saturday morning; she checked it now and found it running clean. She noted the finding in the Callie B.'s maintenance folder and went back to the shack.

She finished the berthing notes. The fog did not lift. No one came down from town.

At 0810 she made tea with the last of the Tuesday ration and stood at the window of the shack and looked at the dock and the boats and the breakwater running out into the fog and the red light at its end that she could not see.

---

The Coast Guard truck came down the dock road at 0852. Ennis was a compact man, mid-forties, his regulation hat worn correctly, with the manner of someone trained to handle this kind of morning. He had two enlisted men with him and a Graflex camera in a canvas case.

She walked them out.

At the seaward end Ennis crouched where she had crouched and looked at what she had looked at. He had the enlisted men photograph from three angles, then a fourth. He went through the man's pockets himself — exterior coat pockets, the coat's interior breast pocket, both trouser pockets — and set the contents on the stone beside the body.

The pockets were empty.

Ennis said this aloud in the register of a man recording: nothing in the exterior pockets, nothing in the breast, nothing in the trousers. The enlisted man's clipboard caught it. Ennis looked at the man's overcoat — the cut of it, the collar — and then at his shoes. He looked at each of these things separately, without comment, and Eira watched him look.

Then he turned to her.

She had been on the dock the previous night, she said. Around eleven. She had come to check the bilge pump on the Callie B. — Farber's dragger, the second slip — after the three-day chop. The pump had been running high before the blow. She checked it, found it clean, and went home around eleven-thirty.

She had seen nothing unusual.

Ennis wrote this down in a small notebook he kept in his breast pocket and clicked the pen closed when he finished.

Had anyone else been on the dock? Not that she had seen. Did she know the man? No.

Ennis looked at the ruined shoes, then at the harbor. He said: not from here.

No, she said. Not from here.

---

They brought the body back on a canvas stretcher, one enlisted man at each end. Before they moved him, Ennis photographed the man's hands separately — working close, the Graflex nearly touching the fingers. Both hands. He stood up and looked at the photographs, not that he could see anything from the undeveloped plates.

He came back to the shack with her and asked for the log.

She gave it to him. He went through the pages covering the last seventy-two hours and the enlisted man removed them carefully, one by one, six pages in total, and squared them against his palm. Ennis signed for them in Eira's margin copy: *Pages 114–119, October 9 through October 12, removed per First District request, Lt. Ennis for Portland.*

There would be a formal statement later in the week, he said. They would be in touch about the record.

He thanked her for her time. He was not unkind. He shook her hand at the door of the shack and carried the log pages to the truck himself.

---

She stood at the head of the dock and watched the truck go up the hill road. It was past ten o'clock. The fog had not lifted and the truck's taillights held in it — two red points, moving, then smaller — and then the road curved and they were gone.

She went back inside.

The log lay open on the counter. The gap left by the pages Ennis had taken fell between the October 8 entry and the fresh page she had started this morning, and the gap had the quality of a room after furniture has been moved out of it: the same dimensions, a different weight.

She picked up the pencil and wrote the heading again at the top of the October 12 page. Then the weather: NNW 9 knots, easing; barometer 29.8, steady; tide high 0647. Then: *0654, one male adult, deceased, seaward end of breakwater, no identification.* Then: *Coast Guard arrival 0852, departure 1008.* Then: *Pages 114 through 119, October 9 through October 12, removed per First District request, receipt signed Lt. Ennis acting on behalf of First District Portland.*

She wrote the facts the record required.

She did not write what she had seen at eleven o'clock the previous night.

She closed the log.

The tide clock read four minutes fast. She had not reset it.
