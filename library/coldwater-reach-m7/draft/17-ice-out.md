# Ice Out

The ice had been pulling back from the northwest corner since the last week of March. Each morning she had checked it from the dock end before opening the shack — the strip of ice shelf along the cove's north wall narrowing by degrees, turning gray and porous, going soft at the edges. She logged the observations without commentary, the way she logged the barometer and the bilge on the Callie B. and the angle of the breakwater light at first dark: not expecting any particular morning's measurement to matter on its own, but keeping the increment because the day the increment did not come was a day that needed marking.

May 3, 1944. She opened the shack door at five-thirty-five and the inner cove was open water.

She stood in the doorway for a moment with her hand on the frame. The salt came in off the water the way it did in working season — not the thin cold salt of winter, which arrived only on high wind off the open bay, but the full harbor salt: diesel underneath it, the particular smell of the tide flat at low water and the channel bottom's specific mud and something above all of it that was specifically this harbor, this reach. It had been five months. She had not been keeping track of that, or had not thought she was, and standing in the door she noticed she had been keeping track.

She wrote in the log: *Ice out, inner cove, full extent. May 3, 1944. 0535 hours.*

She checked the tide clock. She had reset it Monday, as she always did on Monday mornings, and it was running true. She made the notation in the maintenance column.

She ran the radio: primary check, backup frequency, Coast Guard channel at Group Southwest Harbor, the weather broadcast from the Rockland station. Northeast, light and variable, high overcast, no precipitation through Sunday. She wrote it in the weather column.

Before the lobster boats went out she walked down to the second slip to check the Callie B.'s bilge pump. The pump had been running on slow-leak monitor since November — the port quarter taking water at its old rate, never more than expected, twice-weekly checks through the winter, one spike in January when the hard freeze put tension on the hull and the pump managed six inches over three days before things equalized. She had notified Farber in January; she had continued the checks. Now she ran the pump through its test cycle: float switch responding, intake clear, discharge running clean. She wrote it in the maintenance log. The pump was running clean.

The lobster boats went out before six. She counted them from the dock end: nine, which was the full complement for the harbor. The permits had been backed up in Portland — a classification question on the renewed forms that had to be resolved in writing — and had not cleared until the last day of April, but all nine boats had their papers and the nine boats went. Burgess first, as he had been first every season she could recall, leading out with the particular certainty of a man who was not waiting on anyone. Then the two brothers from the far end of the village, then the others in loose sequence, each one pushing through the channel mouth and picking up speed in the open water beyond the bar.

She watched them clear the breakwater.

At seven-sixteen the Callie B. went out, the Margaret Rose ten minutes behind it. She logged their departures. Reg Farber at the Callie B.'s wheel, standing slightly off-center in his usual way, raised a hand as he cleared the dock without looking toward it.

The radio was busy after eight. The weekly freight traffic from Rockland, a fishing advisory from Group Southwest Harbor, two exchanges on the lobster frequency that she monitored in case one of them was going to become a buoy-line dispute — one of them was, and she noted it. She ran the correction forms for the six seasonal mooring permits that had been in error since April: each one reviewed against the tonnage classification table, corrected, re-signed. Six minutes per form. She sealed the Portland envelope.

It happened every year — the harbor going from winter's compression back to its working configuration in the space of a single week.

---

Reg Farber came to the shack on the Friday after ice-out.

He was a large man and the shack was not a large room and he had developed, over years, the practice of stopping at the threshold rather than coming in. This kept things workable. She had never named it aloud and did not name it now. He stopped in the threshold with his cap in both hands and said he had a question about the June M.

The June M. was his lobster boat, a thirty-two-foot wooden trap hauler he had been running for eleven years. She waited.

He laid it out the way he laid things out: in order, without additions. The June M.'s seasonal dock fee came due at the end of the month. He was behind on it. The mooring line had snapped in the hard January freeze — the temperature dropping faster than the almanac had called for, the line under more tension than it was designed to carry before he got to the dock to ease it. When it let go it took dock cleat number seven with it, midpoint starboard of the outer float. The cleat had been in questionable condition before the failure. He had not wanted a dispute about whose responsibility the replacement was, so he had paid for it himself: forty-two dollars for the hardware and the installation labor. He had also had a winter where the trawl catch was down and the fuel cost was up, but he mentioned these in the way of context rather than argument and moved past them.

He was asking if she could defer the June M.'s seasonal fee to July.

The light in the shack was the flat morning light that came through the south-facing window over the radio. She could hear the radio cycling through its scan in the background, the low static between intervals.

The fee schedule was fixed by the District office. She did not have formal discretion to defer a seasonal boat fee. The hardship fund was administered through Portland with an application process and a result that came in August when it came at all, and August was not July and July was not May. Farber was not a man in financial distress — he had been running two boats from this harbor for fifteen years — but forty-two dollars onto a low season was forty-two dollars, and she was not going to name the hardship fund as an answer to his question.

She pulled the spring maintenance folder from the back of the desk.

She had kept the maintenance log in calendar quarters since she took the desk over in 1936. One page per quarter, each dock fitting and installation numbered and noted by location. She found the October 1943 page. She ran her finger down the entries until she found what she had noted on the evening of October 11: *Outer dock, east float hardware survey. Cleat #7, midpoint starboard: bracket wear evident, seating slightly loose. Recommend assessment spring 1944.*

She had written that entry in the fall and filed it and it had been on her spring list. She had not gotten to it. The spring list had four items ahead of it.

She looked at the entry.

She said: "I'll need to look at the cleat."

"It's been replaced," Farber said. "New hardware, good installation."

"The condition of the original fitting is a maintenance question. It was flagged in my fall survey for spring assessment." She set the folder on the desk. "A fee review pending that inspection is within the standard process."

He said nothing for a moment. He was working the cap slightly with both hands, not nervousness, just patience.

She wrote in the log: *June M. seasonal fee under review pending dock maintenance inspection — cleat #7, outer float, east of midpoint starboard. Per fall 1943 survey notation. Review to be completed prior to end of month.*

She dated it. She said: "Can you be at the dock Thursday morning?"

"Seven o'clock," he said. "I'll be there."

She said: "I'll write it up as I find it."

He held his cap for a moment. He said: "I appreciate that, Eira."

She didn't answer to that. He left.

She looked at the entry. The maintenance note from October was in the quarterly record, dated, in her handwriting. The spring inspection was overdue by her own log. Her authority to hold a seasonal fee pending a maintenance question was in the harbormaster's charter. The July timeline would follow from the inspection timeline without requiring the log to carry anything the log would not carry. The entry was accurate. It was complete.

She picked up the mooring permit form she had set aside when he knocked. The radio gave her the eleven o'clock weather update. She wrote it in the weather column and went back to the form.

---

Thursday morning at seven o'clock she was at the outer float.

Farber was already there. He was a man who arrived on time in the way that people who respected their own word arrived — no announcement, just there. She acknowledged him with a nod. She knelt at cleat number seven.

The replacement hardware was in good condition: galvanized steel, properly bedded in the float decking, the seating solid. She checked the surrounding plank for movement; none. She checked the adjacent cleats on either side for comparison; both showed similar surface weathering, both solid. She stood up and made the notation in the inspection log she had brought: *Cleat #7, replacement hardware, installation sound. Base deck condition within acceptable range. Pre-existing bracket wear at original fitting location consistent with fall 1943 survey notation. Failure mode: line-tension overload, not fitting failure.* She initialed it and dated it.

She said: "The cleat's in order. The original fitting was fair wear."

Farber said: "I know that."

"The inspection supports the fee schedule adjustment. The review is closed."

He nodded. That was all. He went back to the June M., which was in the slip behind them, lines checked, ready for the day's run. She watched him go aboard and wrote the final notation in the inspection log.

The morning was overcast, the temperature forty-two degrees, the harbor in its first full working week. She walked back to the shack.

---

The Heron came in on Tuesdays and Fridays from Rockland — mail, packaged freight, occasionally a passenger traveling one direction or the other. She used the outermost slip, the longest approach in the harbor. Eira had assigned it in 1942.

The second Friday in May. She was working through the monthly mooring-line inspection — outer dock, section by section, each line lifted and checked for chafe at the attachment fitting and surface rust on the hardware — when she heard the Heron's horn off the breakwater. She was at section eleven. She kept working. The Heron needed twelve minutes from the breakwater to the dock and she had seven sections remaining.

She was coming down the outer dock when she saw Marcus Klein standing at the end of it.

He was there for the mail, or for the same kind of parcel the Emergency Committee sent up through the Rockland freight — she had seen him once before on a mail-boat Friday, waiting for a box addressed in a Manhattan hand. He stood at the end of the dock the way he stood everywhere he stood: upright, coat buttoned regardless of the temperature, looking at the harbor with an attention that was not a harbor person's attention. He was watching the water. He was not reading it.

She kept moving.

He said: "Warmer."

She said: "Finally."

He moved in the direction of the Heron's slip, which was the direction she was moving. She kept her eye on the approach line.

He said: "The boats are back."

She said: "They are."

The Heron was throttling back at the right distance, coming in at the slight angle Ayers always corrected in the last fifty feet. She watched the approach.

He said: "Any word —"

She said: "Nothing from Portland."

He nodded. She was at the slip. The bow line came across and she held it while the Heron came alongside, then cleated it and checked the fender placement. Ayers said, "Morning, Eira." She said, "Morning." She went to check the stern line while the freight hatch opened.

She did not look back at Klein. She heard the hatch open and the shift of boxes. By the time she finished the stern line he had moved to the hatch, collecting whatever was addressed to him. She went back to section twelve.

The line at section twelve showed surface weathering on the starboard fitting — not structural, worth monitoring. She wrote it in the inspection log. She finished the remaining sections. The afternoon sea fog was beginning to come in off the bar, the familiar mid-May pattern, the visibility softening at the channel mouth without obscuring the breakwater light.

She walked back to the shack.

---

She wrote the day's entries at six in the evening.

Weather: overcast, high cirrus by early afternoon. Wind northeast seven to twelve knots, backing southwest by eighteen hundred hours. Sea fog, harbor approach, fifteen-thirty to seventeen hundred. No precipitation. Visibility unlimited otherwise.

Traffic: nine lobsters out, nine in. Callie B. out 0716, in 1638. Margaret Rose out 0724, in 1503. Heron arrived 1050, departed 1116. Two freight deliveries, afternoon, invoices attached.

*June M. seasonal fee review — closed. Cleat #7 dock inspection completed May 11. Replacement hardware satisfactory, installation sound. June M. seasonal fee rescheduled: due July 31, 1944.*

She blotted the entry and dated it.

She wrote the barometer reading — 30.09 inches, falling slowly — and the morning and evening tide observations. She wrote the mooring-line inspection summary: thirty-one sections inspected over two days, one notation for monitoring, no replacements required.

The shack had gone quiet around her. The radio was on the evening scan, low, cycling through the empty frequencies. Owen's log ledger was on the bottom shelf of the cabinet to her left, where she had moved it in February 1936 when she'd taken the desk over — his handwriting running from June 1891 through the last entry in November 1935, where it stopped. She had not moved it since. The current ledger, her third volume, was on the desk.

She had a quarter of the page left below the day's entries.

She wrote: *May. Ice out. Harbor open. All boats accounted for.*

She put the pen down. She blotted it. She looked at it for a moment.
