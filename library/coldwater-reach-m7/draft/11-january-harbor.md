# January Harbor

The inner cove had been icing since the second week of January — a thin skin at first, gray at the margins where the fresh water off the Reach mixed with the salt, then thickening toward the center until by the twenty-second it was opaque white ice that would not hold a man and would not let a boat through. It was not hard winter; the outer approach stayed open. The two draggers — the Callie B. and the Margaret Rose, Pelletier's — were tied at the outer dock and had been for nine days. The lobster boats were on their trailers at Farber's yard and at the Pelletier place up the road. The mail boat came Tuesdays and Fridays, worked the outer approach, tied at the deep-water slip, and was gone within the hour.

January harbor. Fewer reasons to be on the dock.

She did her rounds in the mornings. In summer the rounds took forty minutes: log, bilge checks, dock inspection, radio at 0800. In January they took twenty-five, with the fleet reduced and the radio check running once daily and the bilge pumps demanding less attention than they did in active season. She had learned, in her second winter at the post, not to stretch the rounds to fill the time. Owen had said something close to this before the fact, though she had arrived at it herself before she remembered that he had said it, and she was no longer certain whether she had arrived at it or remembered it.

She walked the outer dock each morning and noted the ice line in the log. January 22: inner cove ice approximately four inches, opaque. Outer approach clear. Callie B. and Margaret Rose, outer dock, checked and secure. Wind NNW at eleven knots. Barometer 30.14, steady. She checked the Callie B.'s bilge pump — running clean, had been since the boat was tied up — and noted it and went on.

The Callie B.'s bilge pump was what she had gone to check on the night of October 11. It had been running clean then too. She had made her note and left the dock at 2330. She had told Ennis this, and it was accurate.

Back in the shack she ran the 0800 check on the BC-1206 — weather, traffic conditions, the harbor operations she monitored on the coast — and logged the result. Then she turned to the ledger.

She had been opening it to October since November. Not every morning; some mornings she skipped it. But she had opened it enough that it was a part of the mornings, and she had stopped asking herself why, the same way she had stopped asking herself why she rechecked the Callie B.'s bilge pump when she knew perfectly well it was running clean.

The gap was on the right-hand page, midway through the ledger. Pages 114 through 119: Ennis had taken them at 1008 on October 12, folded them into his document envelope with the efficiency of a man who had done it before. What remained was a faint unevenness in the binding — not something you would notice if you weren't looking, but a different weight when you held the spread open. The stitching showed at the spine, pale thread where the section had gone.

The entry before the gap was October 8. Weather, tide, traffic, nothing unusual. Her handwriting, quick and competent. The entry after the gap was October 13: the body, the Coast Guard, the departure of the district lieutenant at 1008. Her handwriting again, abbreviated.

She held the spread open and looked at both sides of what wasn't there.

Then she closed the ledger and left it on the desk.

---

On Wednesdays she went to Merrill's for coffee. Owen had gone every morning; she had made it once a week, not from economy alone — once a week gave it something mornings would have worn smooth. In January, Merrill's had a particular quality: warm in a way the shack was never warm, and occupied in the way that the working middle generation occupied things in January, which was with a settled patience, people who had made their arrangements with winter and found a diner a reasonable place to sit with them.

She was on her second cup when Reg Farber came in.

She knew him as she knew most of the fishermen: by his boats first. Eight years of the Callie B. — the bilge pump, a mooring chain in '41, a registration discrepancy the fall before last — had given them a specific and sufficient acquaintance. Since October they had another kind: the kind produced by proximity to the same event. When they ran into each other at the dock or on the road, the body was there without being mentioned. This was common enough in a town of Coldwater's size. Most things that had happened were there without being mentioned.

Farber sat two stools down from her. The counterman brought him coffee without being asked. He looked into his cup.

He said: "I've been thinking about that fellow since October."

Eira said: "I expect most people have."

"My boy." He stopped. "He doesn't say much about it. Hard to tell what kids carry."

"No," she said.

He drank his coffee. She held her cup. Outside the front window the road was frozen and empty, the empty that Wednesday afternoon in January produced with particular thoroughness.

He said: "I was up late on the eleventh. Night before."

She set down her cup.

"Couldn't sleep." He said it without particular weight; insomnia in January was ordinary. "I was on the porch around eleven, eleven-thirty. I saw a boat come in." He paused. "Small wooden thing. No lights."

She waited.

"It tied up at the far end of the dock — past the dock extension, where you can't see from the road. I watched it for a few minutes. I've gone without navigation lights myself, close in, when I've been out later than I planned and I know the water. Didn't think much of it." He shook his head slowly. "Went back inside around midnight. It was gone by morning."

"When did it connect?" she said.

"Few weeks after. I was thinking about who the man could have been and I remembered seeing the boat and thought — I don't know." He looked at her. "I don't know that it's connected. I just had it sitting with me and didn't know where to put it."

"Did you report it to the Coast Guard?"

He turned his coffee cup on the counter. "No. Didn't think to. Should I?"

She said: "I'll mention it if it's relevant."

He said: "Probably isn't." He looked up at the counterman. "You have apple pie today?"

---

She walked home in the cold.

The road was frozen, the ruts from the last mud period locked into the surface now until spring. She kept to the margin where the ground was flat. January light at two-thirty had maybe ninety minutes in it before the sky gave up. She could feel it going.

What Farber didn't know: she had been on the dock. In the shadow of the Callie B.'s wheelhouse, twenty feet from the dock extension, close enough to hear the engine cut. She had seen the boat come in and she had watched it tie up. She had seen the man step off and stand at the foot of the dock for a moment, getting his bearings — the dark overcoat, the shoes that were wrong for the dock in the way they turned out to be wrong for everything. Twenty seconds, maybe less. Then he had turned and walked toward the breakwater. She had watched him go.

She had gone home and gone to bed. She had told Ennis she had checked the bilge pump, found it running clean, and left the dock at 2330. All of that was true. The truth as she had told it contained all of it except the boat and the man and those twenty seconds, which were not in the account she gave and not in the pages Ennis took and not anywhere except in her.

She had run the other version in October. Thoroughly, carefully, arriving at her conclusions the same way she arrived at conclusions about weather: by looking at the available information from each angle until the picture was clear. The conclusions held.

What was different now was Farber.

He had been on his porch. He had seen the boat come in from the same direction she had seen it. He had watched it for a few minutes — his words — which meant he had stood in the cold in the dark doing his own version of what she had done from the shadow of the Callie B., and then he had gone inside and not thought much of it until he had, and he had carried it for three months because he didn't know where to put it, and he had put it down at Merrill's on a Wednesday in January because the occasion was there and the Wednesday was long.

He didn't connect the boat to the body with certainty. He had said so.

The road turned uphill past the Congregational church. The church windows held the last of the afternoon light for a moment and then let it go. The sky above the tree line was pale gray, moving toward the blue that came before dark.

She had been the only person who knew about the boat for three months. She had not thought about it in those terms — had not told herself each morning, *I am the only person who knows the boat was there.* But she had known it the way she knew the tide tables: as a condition, a baseline, reliable because it had been reliable yesterday and the day before and the month before that.

Farber had put a second point of knowledge into the world. He had done it honestly, without urgency, because he had been meaning to go through that drawer and the occasion presented itself.

She was not the only witness now. She was the witness who knew the most. Farber had seen the boat. She had the boat and the man: the twenty seconds on the dock, the overcoat, the shoes, the direction he walked. Farber was missing those twenty seconds and he would not know to look for them.

The short road to her house. The maple in the front yard was bare, the branches black against a sky that was almost dark now.

She went inside.

She took off Owen's peacoat and hung it on the peg. The kitchen was cold; she had banked the stove before leaving and it needed tending. She put wood on and adjusted the draft and waited until the flame took before she closed the stove door.

She did not go to the log. She had thought, walking home, that there might be a form for Farber's account — some way of putting it into the record where it could exist separate from what she chose to do with it. She stood at the kitchen table. She did not go to the log.

She made supper. She ate. She cleaned up.

The barometer at four-thirty was 30.18 and rising. Clear weather coming, and cold with it.

Reg Farber's information was somewhere in her now, without a ledger to put it in.

She went to bed.
