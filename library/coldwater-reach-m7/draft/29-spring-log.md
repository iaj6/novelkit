# Spring Log

The tide gauge read four feet nine inches when she checked it at six forty-five. High water at nine fourteen; the published table had it at five feet two inches and the morning was confirming it. The inner cove was already full.

The lobster boats had been going out since before first light — she had heard the first engine on her walk down from home at quarter past six, one of the Beaumonts or Devereux, running early the way the early-season men did when the weather held. By the time she reached the dock end the channel was busy, the lobster boats moving through in the gray, their running lights off now that it was light enough to read the water. She counted them from the dock end as they cleared the harbor mouth. Seven, with Farber's June M. making the last one out at six fifty-two.

She stood at the dock end a moment before the rest of the morning started. The air off the water in late April had a quality that was not available in any other month — not the salt and cold of October alone, not the freezing fog of January, but something that was both of those with warmth beginning to work through them. Diesel from the morning departures. Herring bait from the bait house where the lobstermen had provisioned since before her time at this dock. Cold water at the beginning of warming.

Seven lobster boats were out.

She went to the shack and got the log and turned on the NNW channel.

The harbor radio was carrying the European news between the marine traffic. It had been carrying it all week, since the Soviet forces moved through the outskirts of Berlin, since the dispatches had taken on the particular quality she had learned to recognize over four and a half years: the careful evenness of language at the end of something. This morning the correspondent was reporting on the street fighting still going in the German capital, and then, in a slightly different register, on a second report: American and Soviet troops meeting at a river somewhere in Germany, the two armies shaking hands across a bridge in what had been the center of the German state. The river's name came through the static and she caught a syllable of it and lost the rest.

The Beaumont brothers checked in from the outer bar at seven-oh-four — position, conditions, normal traffic. She logged them and responded. Then Devereux's boat, then two more by hull number from the outer bar, then the coast-service check at seven-twenty.

The marine traffic she logged. The European news she did not log.

The two draggers — Lily B. and Mackerel Sky — were both running full season. She could see the Mackerel Sky's boom swung wide as it cleared the harbor mouth at seven-ten; she knew from the empty mooring that the Lily B. had gone out before her morning rounds. Both draggers active. She noted it in the log and set the log aside until the freight manifests came in.

---

The first manifest was the Bangor order: hardware and dry goods on the seven-thirty schooner out of Rockland. She went through the bill of lading crate by crate. Two items short — the same two items from February, the same backlog, the third consecutive month Bangor had not cleared the order. She noted the shortage and pulled a fresh form for the Goulding memo.

The second manifest was fuel oil, direct delivery to the south pier storage tank. She countersigned the driver's paper and stood through the transfer without incident.

The mooring dispute came between the second manifest and the third.

Reg Farber appeared at the shack door at eight forty-five with the two Pelletier brothers directly behind him. All three men came through the door together, which filled the fourteen-foot shack considerably. The dispute was the third float on the east side — the commercial float, held by the two draggers under seasonal assignment from May through October. In the winter layup, when both draggers were pulled, Farber had been mooring the June M. there on Tuesdays for the outside berth because the float was empty and the habit was convenient. The Pelletiers had done the same thing on their own Tuesdays. Two weeks ago they had come in on the same Tuesday, both reaching for the outside mooring, and the collision of dock lines had put a gash in the June M.'s bow rubbing strip, which was the sum of Farber's complaint.

She let both parties say what they needed to say.

Then she opened the dock register to the float assignments and laid it on the counter flat where they could both see it.

The third float listed the Lily B. and the Mackerel Sky under seasonal commercial assignment, May through October, registered at the start of each season. The winter field was blank. The Pelletier boat appeared nowhere in the third float's history. Neither did the June M. Both boats had been mooring there since the winter layup without a registration of any kind, and the only thing that had changed was that both had tried to do it on the same Tuesday.

Farber said he had been there since March.

She asked him to point out in the register where that gave him the assignment.

He stood with his finger on the page for a moment and then took it away.

She assigned the June M. the second float's inside mooring for the season and the Pelletier boat the second float's outside mooring for the season. Both in ink. Both signed and dated. She closed the register. The second float, inside and outside, both boats side by side — which was not what either man had wanted and was the only arrangement that treated both registrations the same way.

Farber started to say something about Tuesday departures.

She told him to work it out with the Pelletiers.

Neither man argued. All three filed out in the order they had come in, and she could hear the conversation continuing on the dock for another minute before it dispersed. Both parties equally inconvenienced.

She worked through the rest of the morning: the third manifest, the coast-service log update from the buoy tender visit last week, the carryover ledger entries. By eleven she was at the dock when the Heron came in.

Three bags: two mail, one package cargo for Goulding's store. She tallied the bags in the dock log and sent the cargo up the hill with the store runner.

No yellow envelopes in the Tuesday delivery.

She noted this in the margin of the dock log in the same compressed hand she used for the rest of it and went back to the shack.

---

The afternoon quieted early. The Heron was gone by twelve-thirty and the lobster boats would not start coming back until three at the earliest. The shack was warm — the south-facing window taking the April afternoon sun, the stove banked low, the room holding whatever heat the season offered. She worked through the remaining paperwork: the Bangor shortage memo, the second float entries transferred to the seasonal ledger, the Heron's cargo receipt.

At half past three she put down the pen.

Outside the window: the empty floats in the afternoon low, the lobster buoys marking the outer channel, the breakwater running east to where the red fixed light would come on when the evening reached it. It wasn't on yet. She knew where it was.

She had twenty minutes before the four o'clock radio check.

She opened the log ledger to the current date. Then she turned back.

The log ledger she was writing in now had been in use since January of last year — it was the fourth book in the harbor record since 1891, the first three on the shelf above the desk, the oldest book from June of that year in a different hand entirely, the harbor's record before any living harbormaster. She had re-covered the 1935 book two years after she started, when the spine cracked; she had done the 1923 book the year after that. She knew the pages by their weight and their smell. Her thumb found the pages for the fall of 1944, the winter, and then she was through that year and into the 1935 book and she was turning through the autumn and into November.

Owen's handwriting came in at November 1.

His hand had a slight backward lean, the letters large and clear, the ink faded to brown at the older dates but still entirely legible in the afternoon light. He had been a careful log-keeper. She had been his deputy for six years before he died and had read his entries through all of them, and she could still anticipate the shape of each letter before it arrived — the *r* with its small curl at the tip, the *s* that ran slightly compressed, the *barometer* where the final letters crowded a little as if his pen moved faster than his hand.

November 3: fog all day, two vessels anchored overnight in the cove. November 7: mail boat delayed, seas at the outer bar. November 10: barometer 29.8 and falling, wind northwest and freshening. November 11: wind NNW at twenty-two knots, sea state four to five, all vessels secured.

November 12.

*November 12, 1935. Weather deteriorating, wind NNW increasing, barometer 29.1 and falling. Putting out additional mooring lines on —*

The sentence ended mid-line. The pen had come up from the page and not returned. The rest of the November 12 page was blank, and after it the next page in her own hand with a different date — the date she had taken over.

She read it.

She had read it many times. She had read it in the first winter after, when she was still rebuilding the hours around the storm. She had read it in 1939, when the war started in Europe and she had found herself in the shack on a September evening with the log open for no reason she could fully name. She had read it in October 1943, the night before the body came in, sitting at this desk until midnight with a particular quality of attention she would not have called grief.

This time was not those times. She was reading it as someone who had finished what it was in the middle of.

He had stopped mid-entry to go put out the mooring lines. The weather had been moving faster than the barometer showed.

She closed the November 1935 page. She turned back to April 25, 1945.

---

She wrote the day.

*April 25, 1945. Weather: clear. Temperature 52°F. Wind SE, 6 knots. Barometer 30.2, rising. Visibility: unlimited.*

*Tide: high water 0914, 5 feet 2 inches. Low water expected 1527.*

*Vessels: seven lobster boats out; return expected by 1700. Both draggers active — Lily B., Mackerel Sky. Mail boat Heron, Tuesday arrival 1140, departed 1230. Three freight manifests received and signed: hardware/dry goods, Bangor order, partial, backlog, Goulding notified; fuel oil, south pier; Goulding parcel cargo, received and receipted.*

*Dock register action: seasonal berth resolution, Farber (June M.)–Pelletier, float 3 conflict. Farber assigned float 2 inside, Pelletier assigned float 2 outside. Entered and signed.*

*Mail boat Tuesday notification: none.*

She set the pen down and read it back. There was nothing to change.

She picked up the pen again.

Below the formal entry, in the same compressed exact hand she had been using in this log for nine years:

*Harbor in order. Nine years, five months. All boats accounted for.*

She put the pen down and looked at what she had written.

It was accurate. The harbor had been in order when she arrived at six forty-five and would be in order when she left tonight. Nine years and five months was what the calendar said, from November 1935 to April 1945.

She closed the log.

---

The tide clock: she checked it. Four minutes fast. She had set it Monday and it had gained its four minutes since, which was what it did. She reached up and moved the hand back.

At four o'clock she called the NNW channel. The Beaumont brothers checked in from the outer bar — position and ETA, conditions normal. She logged them. Two more boats checked in before four-fifteen; she logged them both and gave the harbor conditions and signed off.

She banked the stove draft further. She locked the desk drawer. She took Owen's key ring from the nail by the door — four keys, unchanged since Owen had carried them — and used the shack key to lock the door behind her.

The red fixed light on the breakwater was on. She had seen it come on from the window, the first light of the evening, the harbor mouth marked. The lobster boats were coming in; she could hear the engines cutting as they found their moorings in the early dusk. Seven out, seven coming back.

She walked up the hill toward home.
