# A Report from Portland

By the last week of March the ice was going from the inner cove — pulling back from the edges first, a dark rim of open water widening an inch a day, the granite shelf of the shore returning in the mornings. The center still held: opaque where it had been clear, four inches where it had been five, the surface pale and slightly sunken where the melt had come through and refrozen in February. She had been watching this progress for three weeks.

The Callie B. and the Margaret Rose had moved back to their slips on the twentieth. Farber first, because Farber was like that about weather and tide, and the Pelletiers a day behind. The lobster boats were still on trailers at Pelletier's yard — Kincaid's four, the two from the Dunbar brothers — waiting on the ice and on permits that came out of Portland at their own pace. She had six boats on the waiting list for the first week of April. The radio had been busy for two weeks: weather checks, supply questions, the kind of talk that accumulated when men had been on shore since January and the season was getting close.

She did her rounds at half past seven. Dock cleats, the drain at the float ramp, the fuel line, the BC-1206's daily check. The tide clock had gained a minute since she last reset it. She wound it, adjusted it, made a note. The spring smelled different from the other seasons — not better, she had stopped that mistake years ago — but specific: salt and diesel and the cold mud coming up where the dock skirting had held ice all winter, and underneath both, something opened that had been shut.

In the afternoon she wrote the permits. Six boats, six sets of documentation, the spring registration forms that had to be filed with Portland by April 15. She had been harbormaster for more than eight years and the forms had not changed. She worked through them from top to bottom, the same order every year.

---

The letter came in Friday's mail.

It arrived with the February billings from the fuel cooperative — they were always late — a parts catalog from Portland Chandlery, and a Coastal Patrol circular about spring procedure. She took the stack back to the desk and dealt with them in order: bills, catalog, circular. The envelope from Boston was last.

Standard government stock, cream-colored, slightly heavy. Return address: First Coast Guard District, Headquarters, Boston, Massachusetts. Redirected through the Portland auxiliary, which had added a date stamp in blue ink, March 28 — nine days in transit.

She opened it and read it through.

---

The letter was a page and a half. Dated March 19. An administrative lieutenant she didn't know. At the bottom, a notation in smaller type indicating the original inquiry had been received from the Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, port authority and was being distributed to relevant coastal stations for information and response.

The substance: a party in Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, had received no word from a man to whom they had either lent or rented a small wooden motorboat, approximately eighteen feet. The man had departed Yarmouth approximately September 28, 1943, reportedly heading south by sea, with the stated intention of making landfall in Massachusetts. He had not returned the boat. The owner had assumed, initially, that the occupant and the boat had reached their destination and that the boat had been sold or left somewhere. The missing-persons report had not been filed until February 1944, the letter noted, because the owner had been uncertain whether the report was his to make.

The description of the occupant: male, middle-aged, of continental European appearance, with limited English. No further identifying details available from the Yarmouth party.

The inquiry was being routed to coastal stations, the letter explained, because a body fitting some elements of the description — middle-aged male, civilian, no identification — had been recovered on the Maine coast in October 1943. The District was asking affected ports to provide any relevant information.

She set the letter on the desk. She read it again. Then she sat for a while with the radio on the south wall and the tide charts above it and the April light coming through the shack's east window.

---

What the letter had: a wooden motorboat, approximately eighteen feet. A single male occupant. Continental European appearance. Limited English. Departed Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, approximately September 28, 1943. Destination: Massachusetts, loosely.

What she had: a wooden boat, no lights, tied at the dock extension on the night of October 11, 1943. Gone before morning. A man stepping off the boat and standing at the foot of the dock extension before walking toward the breakwater. Dark overcoat, city cut. Leather shoes that had never been made for a dock. She had been in the shadow of the Callie B.'s wheelhouse, twenty feet away. She had watched him for twenty, thirty seconds. She had not seen his face.

She turned slightly and looked at the chart above the radio.

Yarmouth to Coldwater Reach. She had never made the crossing herself, but she knew the coast by chart and radio contact and the general arithmetic of small-boat passage. Yarmouth was in the southwest of Nova Scotia — a harbor town, working, the kind of place where you could rent a boat from someone who had one to rent. Once you cleared Cape Sable and came south along the outer coast you were in open water: the Atlantic running hard in autumn, the Bay of Fundy's tides working the approaches, no shelter until you reached the outer islands and could begin to work your way inshore. The crossing from Yarmouth to the midcoast of Maine was not impossible in a well-found boat with a good engine. In an eighteen-foot wooden motorboat, alone, in the last days of September — it was something you would undertake carefully, or not at all.

The end of September 1943 had closed badly on this coast. She had the record. Four days of northeast blow beginning the 27th, moderate on the 30th, another two days of wind and rain before it cleared around October 4. If he had departed on the 28th and run into the blow — and he would have run into it, there was no way not to, it had run from the Maritimes to Cape Ann — he would have had to find shelter somewhere and wait it out. An eighteen-foot wooden boat could have done this: put in behind an island, anchor in a cove, wait for the weather to settle. Then come out again and make what time he could.

September 28 to October 11 was thirteen days. Portland to Yarmouth by sea was roughly 200 miles along the coast. At that distance, with four days of bad weather in the middle, a small boat making eight or ten miles a day in the open and thirty or forty in a good stretch — thirteen days was not an impossible time. It was a long time. It was the arithmetic of someone who was being very careful, or very slow, or who had run into trouble more than once and recovered from it and continued south anyway.

She looked at the letter. Middle-aged. Continental European appearance. Limited English.

The man she had seen at the dock extension: a dark overcoat, city cut. Leather shoes. He had stood at the foot of the dock for thirty seconds before he moved. He had walked with the deliberate care of a man who was very tired or not well. He had not spoken. He had not known she was there.

No papers. The Coast Guard had searched the body on October 12 and found nothing: pockets empty, no identification, nothing to give him a name or an address or a record of where he had come from. She had been present when Ennis conducted the search. She had watched him go through the coat and the trouser pockets — exterior, breast, inside — and come up with nothing.

A man traveling from Yarmouth, Nova Scotia, in a borrowed boat, at the end of September, heading south toward Massachusetts, without papers. With limited English. With continental European appearance, which was the letter's language and not hers, but which was the right kind of language for what she had seen at the dock.

The letter did not contain a name.

---

The desk drawer stuck in the way it always did — the wood had swollen in February's wet and had not recovered fully — and she pressed it open and laid the letter at the back, behind the January freight manifests, which were thick and which she would not need again until the end-of-year accounting.

She should respond. The letter asked specifically for any relevant information; she had the authority to write a response and direct it through the Portland auxiliary to the District's administrative office. She would do this.

She closed the drawer. She opened the log and wrote the afternoon's conditions: wind NNW at ten knots, barometer 30.24 and rising, inner cove northwest corner open approximately one-third, outer dock in good order, registration forms filed for six vessels pending April permits. Tide times for the evening. She closed the log.

At three-thirty she did her afternoon rounds. Dock, drain, radio check. The cove had opened another half inch since morning.

---

She made supper and ate it at the table. She had done this every evening for more than eight years, since there was no one else to do it and no one in the house who would eat it with her. The kitchen was Owen's kitchen: his shelf heights, his arrangement of the cups on the left side of the cabinet, the window latch he had repaired in September of 1935 and which she had not replaced because it worked. She sat with her plate and then with her tea and with what the letter had put in the room.

He had been heading somewhere. He had borrowed a boat in Yarmouth and gone south, and Yarmouth was not where you ended up — it was where you passed through on your way to somewhere else, somewhere with a wharf and a street address and a person who was expecting you. Massachusetts, loosely. Boston, probably. He had had a destination and a reason for the destination and people at the destination who had expected his arrival.

They had received instead the kind of silence that comes when someone does not arrive and cannot be found. The owner in Yarmouth had waited five months to file his report, uncertain whether it was his to file. There might be other people who had waited longer, who were more certain that it was not theirs to file, who had no way to report anything because reporting would require them to explain why they had been waiting for him.

She sat with this for some time. The March dark had come down on the cove and the harbor and the breakwater's red light was running at the end of its two hundred yards of granite. Five miles visible offshore. The same as always.

She did not write anything in the log.

She went to bed.
