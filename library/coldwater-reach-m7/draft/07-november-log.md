# November Log

The barometer had been dropping since midday. By three-thirty it was past the threshold where she would have preferred to already be at the shack, and she went.

She came down from the harbor road with her kit bag over one shoulder and her flashlight in the other hand, though it wasn't yet dark. The harbor smell met her at the dock head: diesel from the draggers in the inner slips, cold salt, the brine of the tide running out past the seine sheds.

The two draggers in their slips first. She went over each in the failing light: mooring lines tensioned, dock cleats solid, the breast lines on the Callie B. doubled as she had planned. The bilge pump had been running clean since early October; she checked it anyway — the fitting, the discharge, the reservoir — and found nothing to object to. Then the spring lines on the *Agnes Mae* at the inner slip, then the dock cleats running east along the planking, then the open eastern end where the wood roughed out at the breakwater access steps. The full length of the dock. The breakwater light was on and fixed. She looked at it for a moment and went to the shack.

---

Inside, four o'clock. She lit the lamp and let the BC-1206 warm. When it came in she raised the *Agnes Mae* first: Gilmore, twelve miles south-southwest, still running. She gave him the barometer reading and the sea state and told him to come in. He said he had already turned. She said come in anyway.

The *Carrie Holt* was closer in, seven miles out, Brewer at the wheel. He had seen the sky and was already heading for the cove. She told him to stand off the breakwater until he had slack water, then come through on the flood tide; the channel would be workable. He said he understood.

She set the radio to receive. She moved the chair so she could see the harbor window and the door both from the desk. She opened the log to November 14, 1943, and wrote the time and the barometer: 29.4, dropping steadily. Wind southwest, twenty-two knots, gusting to twenty-eight at the anemometer mount. Sea state rough at the harbor mouth, three-to-four-foot chop with a building swell. Visibility two miles and closing. She listed the vessels: *Agnes Mae* inbound, twelve miles south-southwest. *Carrie Holt* inbound, seven miles. Positions and times. She set the pen in the binding.

The shack was cold. She had kept it unheated during the day so as not to waste the kerosene, and the November air had settled through the south wall completely. She went to the door hook and took down Owen's peacoat and put it on.

The wool smelled of salt and oil and the cedar block she kept in the breast pocket — a small dry piece she replaced each fall, since the cedar in the original lining had long since given out. The coat had been taken in at the shoulders in spring of 1936, Mrs. Thibodeau in town doing the alterations: shoulders taken in, hem raised one inch, lining re-sewn at the cuffs. Eight years of wearing had done the rest — the pockets worn soft at the lips, the cuffs showing use at the outer edge where her wrists rested on the dock railing. The shoulders were right. She buttoned it and sat down.

---

Outside the harbor window, the last light left the sky. The dock light at the slip head came on automatically at five. The inner cove surface was gray and agitated — not whitecapping yet but building from the southwest fetch — and the seine shed rooflines stood dark against the pale strip still left in the east. The breakwater ran out to her right, the seaward light on, doing its twenty-second sweep.

By seven the blow was proper. Brewer raised her from the outer cove at six-forty to say he was ready to come through. She told him the flood was making and the north side of the channel was manageable if he kept his speed down coming in. He came through and tied the west slip at seven-oh-two. Gilmore's *Agnes Mae* ground into the inner slip at seven-fifty with the motor coughing in the cold. She logged both in: *Agnes Mae* inbound 1950, *Carrie Holt* inbound 1902. Both vessels in and accounted for.

She lit the small stove in the corner. When the shack warmed she took the peacoat off and laid it over the chair back.

At eight she put it on again and went out to check the dock.

The blow drove the rain near-horizontal, the kind that found the collar gap no matter how you angled into it. She went with the flashlight and the rubber overshoes and was wet in the first minute. She kept the beam low on the dark planking and went cleat to cleat: the Callie B.'s breast lines, the *Agnes Mae*'s spring lines, the eastern cleats at the corner where the planking met the breakwater steps. All holding. The sound of the harbor was a low continuous roar — not the sharp crack of violent weather but the sound of a storm that had set and would hold.

The breakwater light showed through the rain and the spray coming up off the stone, fixed red, visible through the murk. She confirmed it and turned back.

She stripped off the overshoes at the shack door, hung the peacoat on the hook, wiped down the rubber. The stove had the shack fully warm. She put the peacoat back on and sat.

---

The storm did not break but settled — found its pitch and held it. The barometer at ten o'clock had barely moved from where it had been at six. She poured coffee from the flask and sat at the desk with the lamp burning and the radio quiet and the wind working steadily at the south wall.

She opened the log and wrote the dock check: 2008, all lines secure, no damage, both vessels in. Then the barometer: 29.2 at 2145, holding. Wind southwest, twenty to twenty-two knots sustained.

The log volume had been running since January. Her handwriting throughout: regular, compressed, the notation of someone who knew what a log entry needed and put nothing else in it. Eight years of this kind of writing. The entries did not vary much except in their details — the barometer and the wind, the vessel count, the incidents or the absence of them — and that constancy was what made them useful.

This binding held the older pages as well. She had re-covered the ledger the previous spring and assembled it from two sources: her current year's entries and, bound at the back, the pages from before. Owen's pages. She knew they were there each time she opened the log, the way she knew the peacoat's weight before she put it on — without needing to verify.

She put the pen in the binding to mark her place and turned to the back.

---

Owen wrote with a slight leftward lean and a fully closed *o*, an *r* with a small curl at the tip, the *s* compressed as if his pen moved faster than he did. He had a tendency to crowd the final letters of long words — *barometer* came out a little rushed at the end, *northwest* with the *t* tight against the *s*. She had read his handwriting enough times that she read it without slowing.

She found the page she was looking for.

*November 12, 1935. Weather deteriorating, wind NNW increasing, barometer 29.1 and falling. Putting out additional mooring lines on —*

The sentence ended there, mid-line. The pen had come up from the page and not come back. She had looked at this page perhaps two dozen times in eight years and the sentence still ended where it ended.

She held it for a moment. The sound of the wind. The stove settling.

Then she closed the ledger and turned it to November 1943.

---

She wrote the barometer — 29.2 at 2230, holding — and the wind and the time.

Then the pen stopped.

---

She had been at the dock around eleven o'clock. The bilge pump on the Callie B. — Farber had mentioned it needed attention and she had let the day get away from her, so she went down after the harbor lights came on. The pump was running clean when she got there. She was checking the discharge fitting when she heard the engine.

A small wooden motorboat. She heard it before she saw it — a single-cylinder engine, throttled back to idle — and then it rounded the dock's southern end without running lights and came in slow toward the eastern end. The open end. The end near the breakwater steps.

She stood up from the fitting and watched.

The man on the boat sorted his lines before the engine was fully off and made fast at the far cleat in two passes. He knew what he was doing. Not rushed, not fumbling: the efficiency of someone for whom dock lines were familiar. He had a lantern with him, shuttered down to almost nothing, and in its very low light she could see his hands working the knot — bare hands, working quickly, the knot made and tested with a tug.

She was at the head of the Callie B.'s slip. Forty feet away, or near enough. She did not move.

He stepped off the boat with the lantern and walked east along the dock planking toward the breakwater steps. He did not look in her direction.

She watched him go. The low orange glow of the lantern dropped below the level of the dock as he went down the breakwater steps, and then she could see it on the granite path that ran along the top of the breakwater — a small light moving steadily outward, not hurrying, not stopping. She watched until the dark took it.

She could not have described him. He was an adult man, not young. That was what the dark and the distance gave her, and she had had perhaps twenty seconds: from the moment he rounded the dock's southern end to the moment the lantern went out of sight down the steps. She had looked and she had not seen.

She did not call out. There was nothing irregular enough to call out about — coming in without lights was careless, tying at the open end was unusual, but neither was prohibited, and it was eleven o'clock at night and whatever the man was doing was his business. She finished with the pump and walked home.

In the morning he was at the end of the breakwater.

---

She did not write this in the log.

She wrote the barometer. The wind. The time. She wrote the page and set the pen in the binding and did not look at what she had not written.

---

The storm eased toward two, as she had expected. Barometer at 29.4 and climbing. The wind had come down from twenty knots sustained to fifteen, and the harbor's sound changed with it — lower, more regular, the surface settling from a slap to a chop. She knew the sounds of this harbor in every weather. She knew when the worst had cleared.

She went out at two-fifteen.

The dock was quiet. The rain had settled to mist — present but not driving, the kind that beaded on surfaces and didn't move. She went to the eastern end and found one of the spring lines on the corner cleat had slipped its keeper and needed reknotting. She worked the knot in her gloves, which was slower, but the rope told her hands what to do. She tied it and tested it and found it sound.

She looked up. The breakwater ran out ahead of her in the dark, two hundred yards of wet granite, the fixed red light at the seaward end clear and on. She could see the full length of it in the mist where she couldn't in the rain.

She went back to the shack.

---

At the desk she wrote the time: 0218. Barometer 29.4, rising. Wind southwest, fifteen knots and easing. Visibility improving. Two vessels in. No vessels out. No additional contacts. Eastern spring line at the corner cleat rerigged; otherwise all lines secure.

She set the pen in the binding.

Owen's coat was warm from the hours she had had it on, and she did not take it off. The lamp was turned down. Outside, the mist was the kind that thinned slowly — it would still be there in an hour, in two hours, lightening by degrees as the barometer climbed. The sound of the harbor was water on dock planking and the low creak of the *Agnes Mae*'s lines as the tide shifted her weight in the slip.

She sat in the shack with the lamp low and the log closed on the desk and the tide clock doing what it always did, and outside the window the mist and the dock light and the breakwater running out in the dark toward the fixed red light at its end.

She sat there until it was light enough to walk, and then she went home.
