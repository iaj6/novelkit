# The Mail Boat

The mooring assignments were posted the first weekday after ice-out and then adjusted through June as the fleet returned. By the first full week of July the harbor had settled into itself: seven lobster boats, the Callie B. and the June M. on drag rotation, three pleasure craft from Portland. Plus a day sailer from Rockland whose owner, a man named Burnham, had appeared at the shack on a Thursday morning in late June to ask about temporary mooring fees and had been visibly startled to find a woman behind the desk.

She'd charged him the full summer rate. He'd paid it. He had come back twice since for the weekly renewal and was no longer startled, which was the trajectory these things followed.

The summer work was inventory rather than crisis: dock fees, mooring assignments, freight manifests for the twice-weekly Heron runs, radio checks at six in the morning and noon, the September mooring schedule already half-drafted in her mind by late July. She knew the harbor's summer logic as well as she knew its winter logic, which she knew well. Which boats would run to the western ground and which worked the inner reach. Which lobstermen were in by two and which ran long toward dark and needed her to hold their dock space. The particular compression note of Arsenault's hauler clearing the harbor mouth. The June M.'s starboard engine knock, audible at the third slip on a calm morning a minute before the boat came around the point.

She had been running this harbor for nearly nine years. Owen had done it for six, before November 1935. She had learned a great deal from what he left behind in the log: his method of weather notation, his system for the dock-fee ledger, the way he organized the mooring priority by boat size and gear type rather than by seniority, which was the logical system and which she had kept. She had also, in nine years, learned things he had not had time to learn. The way the southwest wind behaved differently in August than in June, coming off the warmer water with a different load. The relationship between the inner cove's ice and the timing of the outer breakwater pressure in a hard winter, which she could now read in the barometer two days before the event. The harbor she ran in July 1944 was his arrangement in its bones and her arrangement in its living parts.

Six years, then nearly nine.

---

The Heron was sixty feet, white-sided, out of Rockland. It ran Tuesdays and Fridays, arrived at the outer slip at half past two and turned back at four-thirty. She was at the dock for both runs: freight manifests to sign, dock lines to check, the general accounting of what came in. The freight manifest was her authority over the dock; she took it seriously.

The Heron's crew sorted the mail in the hold before docking. When the run was done and the boat secured, the mailman came up the gangway with the canvas pouch and handed it to whoever met the boat — in summer, usually Gladys at the post office, or her assistant on Fridays. The sorted mail went into the pouch in the hold and came out the same way, which meant what was on top coming out was what the crew had put on top when they sorted.

The yellow envelopes were a particular yellow. You could see their color through the canvas of the pouch when the afternoon light was at the right angle, which it often was in July. She had learned by late June to read the mailman's stance in the gangway before the pouch came off — a slight hesitation, or no hesitation, which told you the same thing the canvas told you.

---

The second Tuesday in July the Heron brought two.

She was on the far side of the freight stack when the mail went — she had three crates of canned goods and a drum of machine oil to sign for, and the drum's stenciling was partially obscured, which required a manifest annotation before she could file it. By the time she came around the stack the mail was sorted and the pouch gone. She did not need to have seen it. She had seen the canvas on the gangway.

By suppertime the word was around town. Doris Hatch's older brother — E.J. Hatch, Navy, two years ahead of Doris at school, gone to the Pacific in the spring of '42. And Albert Eaton's youngest, who had come in from the Eaton farm up the valley road every summer to help with the August freight unloading when Eira needed extra hands at the dock, and who had enlisted the November after Pearl Harbor.

The harbor did not stop for this. The fly was running and three of the lobster boats came in heavy at half past three and the loading dock was busy until nearly dark. The weather was good: wind SW at twelve knots, swell steady, visibility clear to the outer buoy and past it. She logged it all. It was all true.

She went home that evening and ate supper and came back to lock the shack at nine. The harbor was quiet. The outer light was doing its work. She stood in the door of the shack a moment with the key in her hand before she locked it.

The following Tuesday the Heron brought no yellow envelopes.

She saw it before the boat tied up. The crew on the gangway. The mailman's hands on the pouch. She kept her eyes on the freight manifest and made the amendment notation and stood aside for the dock crew.

There were five people at the dock head waiting for the Heron. Two more than usual for a Tuesday in July. When the mail went to Gladys and Gladys sorted it at the dock-end table and there were no yellow envelopes, the five people at the dock head left in stages — not quickly, not dramatically, simply in the ordinary way people leave a dock when there's no particular reason to stay. She did not write any of this in the log.

She wrote the weather and the vessel statuses and the freight manifest and she went home.

---

Marcus Klein was at the dock for the second Tuesday in August.

She saw him at the dock head when she turned onto the dock with the freight manifest. He was standing with his hands in the coat pockets, weight back, watching the Heron come in. His wool coat was wrong for August, though August in Maine was the least wrong month for it she could imagine given the places he had come from, which she knew about only in general terms.

The Emergency Committee parcels came through Rockland freight every few months. She hadn't expected him today. She went past him to the outer end.

Four crates of hardware for Donnelly's, a pallet of seed stock for the Peabody farm up the valley. The Heron's first mate had the manifest ready; she checked it and signed it and stood aside for the off-loading. Klein had come down past the freight stack and was signing a shipping receipt with the crew. Brown-paper parcel, flat, twine-wrapped, books from the look of it. He tucked it under his arm and turned toward the dock head.

She had been checking the mailman's hands through all of this. Gladys was at the dock-end table with the sorted mail. No yellow envelopes. She could see that clearly. She had kept the freight manifest in front of her and she knew it clearly.

Klein was heading back up the dock, his parcel under his arm, and she said — at the distance she would use for calling to a lobsterman about a dock line:

"Dr. Klein."

He turned.

She said: "No yellow envelopes today."

He looked at her for a moment. He said: "Good."

She said: "So far."

She went back to the manifest.

He went up the dock. She filed the amendment and waited for the Heron to complete off-loading and walked the dock lines and checked the freight cage lock. It took the remainder of the afternoon. The Heron turned at four-thirty, its usual time, and she watched it clear the outer slip and heard its engine change pitch as it came around to the Rockland heading, and then it was around the point and she went back to the shack.

---

She wrote the harbor log at seven in the evening with the shack door wedged open against the day's remaining heat.

Hot, clear. Wind SW, eight knots. Barometric pressure 30.04, steady. Swell eight to ten inches. Callie B. in, routine. June M. out, due back Thursday 0800 estimated. All seven lobster boats accounted for by 1800 hours. Freight received: four crates hardware (Donnelly's Hardware), one pallet seed stock (Peabody, valley road). Emergency Committee book parcel, one unit, received per manifest by Dr. Klein, Coldwater School. Manifest filed and signed.

She wrote the day's operational entries in their order. Twelve minutes. She was not hurried. The log was the record and the record was worth the twelve minutes.

When the operational entries were done she held the pen over the page.

Outside the shack's open door, the harbor at seven in a hot August evening was doing what the harbor did. Boats at their moorings in the last of the direct light. The water picking up the west sky and lying flat and particular with it. The outer light at the breakwater end beginning to assert itself as the ambient light dropped. Arsenault's trap hauler at the third float, dock line coiled on the cleat the right way — it had taken her two reminders at the start of the season and now he coiled it the right way without being reminded. The June M.'s empty slip, her mooring line looped at the head. Nothing wrong. Nothing outstanding.

She wrote one more line.

*Hatch, E.J. — no telegram this Tuesday. The harbor was full.*

She had done this twice before. The first time in late November 1943, after the Tuesday boat brought no yellow envelopes the week following the week it had brought one — there had been a name in November too, a Coldwater boy she had known since he was twelve and crewing on his father's boat. The second time in June, in the middle of a run of good Tuesdays. Both times the same kind of entry: a name, a fact, the harbor's condition.

There was no column in the harbor log's official charter for this kind of entry. The charter — the document governing the post she held — required tide records, weather, vessel status, dock activity, freight summaries, notable maritime events. What she wrote was not a maritime event. It was not notable in the sense the charter intended. It was not any of the things the charter authorized. It sat in the log's column in her regular compressed hand, in the same black ink, the same weight as the freight summary above it. She did not put these entries in the margin. She did not flag them with a notation. They were in the log because the log was the record, and if the record was going to be the record at all it was going to be complete, and complete meant what actually happened, not only what the charter had space for.

She was not certain, yet, what she was making. The harbor log since June 1891 had recorded the harbor's official business: what came in, what went out, what the weather did, what the vessels required. She was now recording something else in the same space, without separating it. The October 1943 entry that the Coast Guard had removed was pages 114 through 119. Those pages had held the official account of the official event, and they were in Portland now, in a file she had been told she could not request. But the log was still here. Her hand was still in it. She had kept writing in it through November and December and every month since and the harbor she ran was in those pages completely, including the things the charter did not have a form for.

She set the pen in the desk tray.

Outside: the harbor in the last of the day. The boats at their moorings. The breakwater light ticking in the dusk. In the still water at the dock's near end, the reflection of the dock boards and the sky above them, the doubled image clear enough that the reflection looked like the thing rather than the reflection.

She closed the log.

She sat with her hand on the closed log cover.

Then she stood and went to lock up the shack.
