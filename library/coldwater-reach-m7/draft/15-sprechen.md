# Sprechen

April came in wet. Not the clean wet of rain — the slow wet of thaw, the ground releasing what winter had put into it, the low fields going to mud first and the mud staying because more came from above every second day and the higher ground was only drier by comparison, not dry. The farm roads had been impassable for two weeks in March. Even now the ruts in the south drive were four inches deep in the mornings, filling again before her father had the manure spreader across them.

The work changed with it. In winter you moved inside: repair, preparation, the woodwork and the harness work and the ledger kept beside the stove. In spring you moved back out into ground that didn't want you yet, into the mud and the half-frozen furrows and the particular cold of April that was colder than March because you had stopped expecting it. Her father had been trying to get at the low corner of the north field for three weeks. The drainage ditch below the elbow had been frozen since November, was open now but choked — sediment, dead stems, the runoff from the upper pasture backing up in the bend — and the low corner itself was standing in two inches of water that would not move until the ditch moved. He had put the prisoners on it Monday morning: two of them with drainage spades working the choke from the outlet upstream, the other two on the sod itself, turning forks, breaking the strips where the ground was solid enough to turn.

Lonzo Reed walked the south fence line. He had his Springfield on his shoulder and his hands in the pockets of his coat and he walked one direction and then the other at the end of the field, doing the job that was achievable: watching when he could watch, being visible the rest of the time. The field was long and the four men were spread across most of its length. There was no position from which one man could hold all four in view. Hannah had understood this about the farm's arrangement since at least October. She thought Lonzo had understood it since before she did, and had found his accommodation — the walking, the visibility, the attention at the moments when attention was possible — and left the gap between that and what couldn't be done at the back of his mind, which was where it belonged.

She was at the north fence. Three posts needed resetting: frost had lifted them through February and March, tilting two of them noticeably and leaving the wire slack between the second and the third. She had the post driver, the tamping bar, a bucket of gravel from the drive apron. The work was not complicated. Pull the post, clean the base, repack the depth, tamp the gravel, check the plumb, move on. She had done it three years running now. Next spring she would do it again.

The north fence work put her at the far end of the field from Lonzo, and roughly thirty yards from where Karl Reinmar was turning sod.

She had not arranged this. The north fence needed the work it needed. The prisoners had been assigned to the north field. The farm's geometry put her where it put her, and she was not going to spend the morning deciding what it meant or didn't mean.

The letter was in her school notebook, in her coat pocket. She had been carrying it since February, between the Whitman annotation pages where nobody would open. She had read it perhaps fifteen times — not to learn anything new from it, her German was enough for most of it and she had Dr. Klein's English for the phrase that mattered — but because she had it and Karl Reinmar did not know she had it. She had not returned it. She had considered the ledge beside the barn door, where things were left for other people's hands; she had considered slipping it into the pocket of his canvas jacket during the milk-can work. She had looked at each option and put it down. The letter stayed in the notebook.

She thought about what Dr. Klein had said. *Die die versucht haben zu gehen, sind weg.* He had given her the English: *Those who tried to go are gone.* He had said it quietly, without ceremony, looking at the page more than at her. And then: *The leaving is not the hard part.* She had not followed everything after that — the part about arriving, the brief word he used for Canada, the way he stopped himself partway through a sentence — but she had taken that line home and it had been there since, in the kitchen and in the barn and in the evenings when she heard the two voices through the glass of the icehouse window.

She thought about December. The fence post on the east side of the paddock, lifted by the frost, loose in its hole. Karl had found it before she did, had both hands on it to test it, and they had stood on either side of the fence with the cold between them for ninety seconds. He had said thirty-two words in uncertain English — *Fence. Post. Is loose — frost, I think. I can — with the tool — if you want.* She had answered. She had gone home afterward and decided to keep noticing what she noticed without deciding what it meant, and she had done this for four months, and now it was April and the ground was waterlogged and she was thirty yards from him with a post driver and a tamping bar.

She pulled the second fence post and cleaned the base.

She was going to speak to him in German.

She had known this for some time, not as a plan she had made and timed but as a thing that had already become true and was only waiting for the occasion. She could not have told anyone when she had decided it. Maybe when she read the letter at the kitchen table and put it in her notebook. Maybe when Dr. Klein said *the leaving is not the hard part.* Maybe earlier. She did not know how to locate when she had started moving toward something. She knew where she was.

She tamped the gravel and set the plumb. The post was straight. She moved to the third one.

Karl was twenty yards from her now, working a strip of turned sod. He had not looked up.

---

The occasion came on a Wednesday.

By four o'clock the light was going gray in the west and the temperature dropping, and Lonzo gathered the men in his customary way: a short two-note whistle, an arm wave, the walk from the field across the farmyard to the milking barn. The full cans needed moving before the evening escort back to the icehouse. Same procedure every afternoon.

Hannah was already in the barn. She had finished her portion of the afternoon milking — the late shift, her father's Guernseys, twelve animals in the south stanchion row — and was at the stanchions with the rinse pail when she heard the outer door.

Lonzo came through first, the way he always did, moving through the near end of the barn before the prisoners entered. He passed her without a word — that was usual too — and made his check of the space. Then the four men came through the door.

The three older ones went immediately to the far end. Fischer, Brandt, Walther: the motion of men who had done this a hundred times, moving to their positions at the carrying frames with the ease of a practiced routine. Karl came in last and took up his position near the door, where the two empty cans were waiting to be moved to the cold room.

The barn in April had its specific smell: winter still in the wood — straw, old manure, the cold settled into the rafters — and over it the new warmth the cows had been building since the temperature turned, something almost sweet, the particular heat of twelve animals at the end of a full day. She had known this smell her whole life. In winter it was comfort and in summer it was almost too much and in April it was both at once.

Lonzo said, to nobody in particular, in the mild and slightly apologetic tone he used when he was really talking to himself: I believe I could use a minute of cold air.

His boots on the door planks. The cold outside coming in briefly, then the door.

She had perhaps three minutes.

She moved the rinse pail to the corner. She turned.

Karl had the first empty can in his hands. He looked at her directly — no fence between them, no width of field — and he was nineteen years old and very still, the kind of stillness that is attention, not surprise.

She said, in Bavarian German, her father's Bavarian German, the kitchen German, the words she had grown up on before she had any other words at all:

Bist du von nah an Rosenheim?

He stopped moving.

He said — not the halting English he had been absorbing from Lonzo, not the vocabulary of weather and farm tasks and Lonzo Reed's fisherman's Maine, but his own German, the German of someone who thinks in German, the German of someone for whom German is the place the words come from:

Ja. Nah an Rosenheim. Du sprichst Bayrisch.

She said: Mein Vater ist Bayer.

He said: Ich weiß. Er hat mir gesagt. Wir reden manchmal.

She said: Ich weiß.

A pause. He looked at her steadily. He said: Verstehst du uns?

She said: Einiges.

He settled the can against his hip, looking at the middle distance for a moment — at the carrying frame, at whatever he was about to say — and said:

Es ist seltsam, das hier zu hören.

She said: Ich weiß.

Then Lonzo's boots on the door planks, the cold coming back in with him, and Karl moved toward the far end with the can. Fischer said something in German. Karl answered in two words. The carrying frames went up. The sound of boots and iron-wheeled carts on the stone floor.

The three minutes were over.

---

She finished the barn and walked up to the farmhouse. She went at her usual pace. She was carrying something and she did not want to drop it by moving too fast.

Her mother was at the stove. Her father's coat was on the hook — he was still in the south field, the manure spreader still to get through the ruts. The kitchen was warm after the barn's April cool, and it smelled of supper beginning: something on the back burner, the five-o'clock smell of the kitchen in every season she could remember.

She sat down at the table and held what she had.

She had spoken Bavarian German to Karl Reinmar in her father's milking barn. He had answered in it. They had been in the same language for approximately two minutes, for approximately forty words.

She went through them.

Her opening: Bist du von nah an Rosenheim. Then his: Ja. Nah an Rosenheim. Du sprichst Bayrisch. He had named it — the particular vowels, the particular verb placement, her father's dialect in her mouth.

Then hers: Mein Vater ist Bayer. Then his: Ich weiß. Er hat mir gesagt. Wir reden manchmal. She had already known this. She had been listening to the two voices through the icehouse window since at least October — her father's German, Karl's German, the back-and-forth of the exchange she could not fully follow but could read by its rhythm.

Then hers: Ich weiß. Then his: Verstehst du uns? Then hers: Einiges.

Then the last thing.

Es ist seltsam, das hier zu hören.

She turned the sentence over in the way she did when a German word was doing more than she could immediately account for. Seltsam was a word she had from her father. He used it for things that were wrong in a way that was also right: the first warm day in March when you weren't prepared for it, the quality of the light after a storm, a calf in its first hour looking at the barn walls with its whole body. Seltsam. Strange and also true. Strange in a way that was not a complaint.

He had said es ist seltsam, das hier zu hören while he was looking at the middle distance — at the carrying frame, at whatever he was about to say — and she had heard the strangeness and she had heard the other thing in it too. Something she was not imagining.

Her mother moved the pot to the front burner. Hannah looked at her own hands, still cold from the barn, a faint smell of the rinse water still on them. Two minutes in the milking barn in April. It was the first time in her life she had spoken German to someone who was not her father, not Dr. Klein, not her own reflection testing phrases in the window glass before school.

She thought about the letter. She had thought, for the few seconds before Lonzo's boots on the door planks, that this might be the moment — that she could reach into her coat and pull the notebook out and set the page in his hands. She had not done it. She was not sure why. The letter was still between the Whitman pages. She sat with it in her pocket and with what she had said and what he had said and let the kitchen be what the kitchen was.

---

After supper her father went to the icehouse window.

She could hear them from the kitchen, where she was doing the clearing. The sound came through the old single-pane glass above the sink, not words but the quality of words — the rhythm of the exchange, the structure of turns. She had been listening to this for months. She could read it now the way she read the farm by its sounds: what the cows told you before anything else did, what the specific direction of wind in the north ridge meant in November against what it meant in April.

In the barn it had been different.

She stood at the sink with the clearing water and tried to hold what was different about it. In the barn Karl had been more level. Not equal — she did not have his twenty years of German and he knew this; Einiges was the honest answer — but not the younger-man-receiving-instruction either. Something between. The German of speaking across to someone unexpected. He had not expected her to come to him in that language. The surprise was in his stillness. The surprise was in the word seltsam.

She was not from the home he meant. She had never been to Bavaria. She had never been south of Portland. The Bavarian she had was her father's Bavarian, brought across the Atlantic in 1922 and passed down through the kitchen and the barn floor and the songs she learned without knowing she was learning them, and it was as much a part of her as the farm. She was from Coldwater. She would always be from Coldwater.

Es ist seltsam, das hier zu hören.

Strange, to hear it here. She sat with the word seltsam the way she sat with things she wasn't finished with. Strange and also true. Strange and also, underneath the strangeness — she thought she had heard this in the word, in the way he looked at the carrying frame when he said it — not wrong at all. Not wrong in the way that strange things you haven't decided about yet are not wrong. The kind of strange that means: I did not know this was here. I did not know it could be here.

She thought he might also have meant: I am glad it is.

She did not know this. She had not imagined it.

---

She went to bed at half past nine.

She had done the thing she had been moving toward since she did not know when. The Wednesday had come and Lonzo had gone out for his three minutes of cold air and she had turned and said her father's words into the barn's April smell and Karl had answered in his own German, full and careful and from the same ground.

She did not know what it meant for the farm, for her parents, for the arrangement. She did not know whether Karl would carry it forward or whether the icehouse window would have a different quality now. She did not know whether the letter still in her notebook meant the same thing it had meant in February, or whether she should return it, or whether returning it was something entirely separate from what had happened in the barn.

She did not know how to locate when it had started: the fence post in December, the letter in February, Dr. Klein's classroom, the north fence in April and Karl thirty yards from her at the turning fork. Some combination of these and something underneath the combination she didn't have a name for.

Two minutes in the milking barn in April. Forty words in the language her father had brought from Bavaria to Coldwater, Maine. Es ist seltsam, das hier zu hören. She had said Ich weiß, and she had meant it.

She went to sleep.
