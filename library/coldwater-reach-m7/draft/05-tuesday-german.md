# Tuesday German

The letter was from 1847, a botanist writing to his brother from somewhere in the foothills north of Munich. The coal furnace in the corner of the classroom had been running since first period and was making the sound it made when it was working at full effort without much result — a low rattle behind the draft plate, intermittent, like a man clearing his throat in another room. The east windows held whatever October afternoon light was available, which at three forty-five was pale and going. Three coats on the hook by the door. The room smelled of chalk and cold wool and the faint chemical undertone of the wax Henry Crow had put on the floor in September, which cold air kept releasing in small quantities.

Dr. Klein stood at the board with the mimeographed passage in his hand. Doris Hatch had her pencil laid flat on the desk in front of her. Louise Pelletier was watching the window, not the glass of it but the sky beyond — the strip of gray sky between the school roof and the maple, which still had most of its leaves.

He read the first line aloud and asked Doris to construe it.

Doris construed it carefully, the way she did everything in this class: accurately, with a quality of not wanting to give the language more of herself than the task required. The verb position was right. The case was right. The sentence came out in the correct English order, stripped of whatever the original sentence had been in its own mind, and Dr. Klein said good and moved to the next line.

Louise took the third line. Louise had a facility with French that she had been applying sideways to German since September, which produced results that were more elegant than Doris's and occasionally less accurate. This time they were accurate. Dr. Klein told her so. He wrote the construction on the board: the dependent clause, the verb at the end, the reason for it. He asked what they noticed. Louise said it reminded her of something in French. He said that was not accidental and they could return to it in a later session.

He found the next paragraph and asked Hannah.

She took the fourth line: the botanist describing the larch in the valley, the way the trees held their yellow through October until a hard frost came through and dropped it all at once, and the road between the two towns she had never been to but knew by another road's name, the way her father named them — a slightly different vowel, a verb placed one beat earlier in the sentence than the textbook would place it, which was how it sat in her head because it was how her father said it. The sentence arrived whole, the way German arrived for her, and she said it the way she had heard it since she was small enough that hearing it had not seemed like a particular thing.

She came to the end of the sentence. She looked up.

Dr. Klein was not writing on the board. There was a pause that had a shape to it — not long, but present, like a page that had turned differently than expected. He was looking at the mimeograph. Then he said, in German: *Ihr Akzent kommt aus dem Süden. Östlich von München.*

Your accent places you south, east of Munich.

She said, in English: My father is from Bavaria.

He said: Yes.

That was all. He looked down at the paper and found the next paragraph and asked Doris to take the construction at the top of it. Doris bent over her page and began working out the verb.

Hannah looked at her own paper. The larch trees in October. She had gotten them right.

---

After class Doris and Louise went ahead. Hannah stood at her desk until they were through the door, then took her grammar and came up to where Dr. Klein was erasing the board.

She said she had a question about a construction — the conditional clause in the second paragraph, whether the finite verb always moved to clause-end or whether there were exceptions in older texts.

It was a real question. She had noticed it in the letter. She was also standing at the front of the room because she wanted to know whether he would say anything further.

He set the eraser on the chalk tray and considered the question properly, which he always did — she had been in this class since September and had noticed that he treated even the simple questions as worth the full consideration. He said yes, the finite verb moved to the end in dependent clauses, conditional and relative and otherwise, with specific exceptions in some eighteenth-century texts, particularly in certain genres of correspondence. He said the exceptions were catalogued in the grammar, page forty-eight, margin note.

He did not say anything further about the vowels.

She said thank you and went and took her coat from the hook by the door and put it on.

---

She walked the long way, past the harbor.

The direct road turned at the bottom of the school hill and angled inland; the harbor route added ten minutes and she had the south field before school tomorrow and the evening milking still ahead. She took the harbor route. She had been taking it since the middle of October, since the morning Doris had come into first period saying she'd heard someone had found a body on the breakwater, which turned out to be true.

The afternoon was going gray. The road curved down around the shoulder of the hill and the harbor opened below — the dock's gray planking, the seine sheds, the Callie B. and two lobster boats in their slips. Past the dock's end the breakwater ran out two hundred yards across the inner cove, granite, dark at the waterline where the tide was coming in. At the far end the fixed red light had just come on. The first light of the evening, a clean red against the gray.

She was looking at the point at the seaward end. Nothing was different. The stone was stone, the water was the harbor, the light was the light. The body had come in on a Tuesday morning three weeks ago and had gone out again the same day, taken by the Coast Guard and the tide of things the Coast Guard managed, and the breakwater was now just the breakwater.

She looked at it for a few seconds. Then she kept walking.

The road bent inland and the harbor went behind the seine sheds and then behind the hill, and after that it was the inland road, the smell of mud and leaves, the farms in the distance.

---

Her father was already at the table. He had changed from his work clothes; the almanac was open in front of him. Magda was at the stove.

Her mother did not turn when Hannah came in. She had a dishcloth over her shoulder and the wooden spoon in her right hand, watching the pot with the particular quality of attention she brought to anything she was doing, which was all of it, all at once, and was not the same as the absence of attention it sometimes resembled from the doorway. Hannah hung her school coat and came to the table.

Albrecht looked up. He said: *Das Südfeld braucht morgen früh Aufmerksamkeit.*

She said she would do it before school.

He nodded and looked back at the almanac. He had been reading it in sections all month and it did not appear to change anything about how he farmed.

Her mother brought the pot to the trivet and went to the counter for the bread and the butter and the pickled beets they had put up in September, Albrecht's beets, which always went farther than the canning jars could hold, and set everything on the table and sat down and they ate.

Hannah's private theory about her mother's silence had been with her long enough now that it had stopped being a theory and become a working assumption: Magda had decided somewhere around April that language was not a safe material for the duration and had set it down the way you set down a tool you had no use for this season. The doctor in Rockland had listened and examined and found nothing physically wrong. It moved through the kitchen with purpose. It knew where everything went.

They ate. The stove ticked. The draft at the kitchen window came and went, the window she had been meaning to stuff with rags since September.

Her mother ate and cleared and refilled the water glasses without setting anything down harder than it needed to be set, and then she cleared the rest and Albrecht went for the almanac again and Hannah washed the dishes while Magda dried them, which they did in the silence that was ordinary now, the kitchen sounds filling in what the voices didn't. The dishes went to their shelves in the same order they always went. Outside the window the yard was dark already, the maple at its edge a mass against the sky, barely any leaves left.

---

Albrecht went out while Hannah was wiping down the table.

She heard the back door close, and then through the wall from the direction of the icehouse came the low sound of his voice. She couldn't make out the words. She knew there would be an answer through the window from Karl — the icehouse window that had been cracked two or three inches since August — but Karl's voice didn't reach this far and she couldn't hear it. She could hear her father's voice going, a sentence, then a pause, then another sentence, going past where a simple answer would have stopped.

She turned on the kitchen radio.

The Philco on the counter was old and received unevenly: some nights clear, some nights mostly static, with the sense of something being said underneath it — a voice in another room. Through the wall, her father's voice continued.

She turned it off at eight. Her mother had already gone upstairs. The kitchen was quiet. She banked the stove for the morning and took the lamp to the stairs.

---

The hallway upstairs smelled of the cedar her mother kept in the linen chest. Hannah's room was the second door, the one with the view of the maple, and she set the lamp on the dresser and wound the alarm clock to four-fifteen and undressed in the lamp's reach and got into bed with the extra blanket.

The maple outside the window was almost bare. Two weeks ago the color had still been in it — a yellow that her father would call Goldgelb if he were naming it, which he wouldn't, because color was not what he named — and now the branches were branches, the sky behind them going from dark gray to black as the clouds moved east.

What Dr. Klein had said sat in her the way a stone sits in a boot: not sharp, but there. Not the grammar, which had an answer. The other thing.

He had heard her father in her German and he had named where he heard it — south, east of Munich — and she had said where and he had said yes. And that had been the whole of it.

She didn't know what he'd heard, exactly. She knew what she'd heard, which was that he'd recognized something she hadn't known was recognizable. Her father's German: the verb placed where her father placed it, the vowels her father used, which were not the vowels in the textbook and had never been. She had been speaking textbook German in that class since September, or she had thought she was, and somewhere in the line about the larch trees in October she had apparently stopped.

She didn't try to do anything with this. She turned onto her side.

From below — from the direction of the icehouse, the geometry of the farmhouse making it hard to fix precisely — she could hear her father's voice again, low and going, the German in the dark coming through the floor and through the wall at once, talking to Karl through the cracked window on the other side of the wall. She couldn't hear what he said. She hadn't been able to hear it since the first night she had noticed it, in August, and she had not gotten any closer to the words since then.

The maples had been yellow. Now they were branches. November was coming in the next few days, and after November came the months that took everything you had and didn't offer much back, and the south field needed its attention before school tomorrow, and the alarm was set for four-fifteen and would do what it did, and she was already closing before it.

Her father's voice went on below her in the dark.

She was asleep before it stopped.
