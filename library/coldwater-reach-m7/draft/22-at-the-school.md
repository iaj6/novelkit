# At the School

He was through the back door at five-twenty, the skeleton key and the lift-and-turn that still worked after twelve years. The light chain at the bottom of the basement stairs.

The Vulcan No. 6 had been banked overnight and wanted four scoops now: two from the left bunker, two from the right. October ran different from September. The right-side draft plate needed a half-turn clockwise from where he'd left it Friday, and the main flue open a full fifteen minutes before he throttled back. He had done this in October twelve times. He did it now in the same order.

The school's sounds arrived in the quiet while the furnace caught. The pipe knock in the second-floor east corridor, familiar enough by now that it had become a form of punctuation rather than a sound. The floor joists in the east wing. The building's long creak in the second-floor hallway, where the floor had been riding a slight eastward lean since before he arrived. He listened to these the way the harbormaster, he supposed, listened to the harbor: not for information he lacked but to confirm that nothing had changed since Friday. When something changed you heard it. This morning nothing had changed.

He checked the pressure gauge at six-forty and again at seven-fifteen. He cleared the chalk ledges in Marcus's end of the second floor — Marcus wrote everything twice on the board, the original text and then the English below it, all of it in a small compressed hand that produced more fine dust than you would expect from that much precision. He swept the first-floor hallway, the second-floor east corridor, and the front steps, which had the last of the maple leaves from the trees over the road. He unlocked the building at seven-forty-five.

The students came at eight. Fourteen of them, down from eighteen in September.

---

Mrs. Ingrid Pease had been school secretary since 1931. She did not call him to the office for nothing.

He came in at nine-thirty to pick up the supply order and she told him: there had been a formal written complaint submitted to the school board on Monday — yesterday — by Mrs. Almira Vose. About Dr. Klein. About whether a man of German birth, however long ago he had left Germany, however little he had to do with any of it, should be teaching in a school whose boys were dying in France.

Mrs. Pease said she did not think the board would act. Dr. Klein was the only qualified German and Latin instructor available in this part of the county and the school had no one to put in his place. The complaint was feelings, not cause for dismissal; anyone the board consulted would say so. She expected the outcome to be a formal acknowledgment and a letter to Mrs. Vose expressing the board's appreciation for her concern. Still. The complaint was in writing and going to the November meeting and she thought Henry ought to know. She had the look of a person who had been holding something since the previous afternoon and was glad to set it down.

He said: does the supply order include the new chalk for the second floor?

She checked. She said it did.

He said thank you. He took the supply box from the collection shelf and went.

---

He distributed the supplies — chalk to the second floor, mimeograph paper to Mrs. Durant's room, the replacement bulbs for the hall fixtures, the new blotter for the principal's desk — and he was not thinking about the complaint with the part of his mind he used for the supply order.

He was thinking about it with the other part.

The two parts had an arrangement that had developed over the years in this building. The first part ran the maintenance. The second part ran the assessment of things that were not maintenance. They did not often interrupt each other.

The second part ran the assessment as follows. The board would likely not act. The school had no replacement for Marcus; the board knew it; a complaint grounded in feelings rather than cause was not grounds for termination and any counsel would say so. He put the probability of inaction at eighty percent. Eighty percent was where things generally worked out.

Eighty percent was not a hundred.

The informal complaint from the previous year — a different parent, not put in writing, not submitted to the board — he had heard from Mrs. Pease and assessed it and it had gone nowhere. He had not said anything to Marcus, because the weight of knowing it would have been Marcus's to carry for no purpose. The weight had not been necessary. This was different. This was filed, in writing, going to the board, and the November meeting was six weeks away. Six weeks was enough time for a person to prepare. What Marcus would do with six weeks was Marcus's question, not his. What was his: Marcus did not yet know, and the six weeks were available to give.

---

He saw Marcus at midmorning. Second floor, east corridor, Marcus between his nine o'clock and his eleven o'clock, coming out of the German classroom with a roll of examination papers under his arm. Henry was at the east-corridor window, the one that had been swelling in its frame since September.

Marcus said: east window sticking again?

Henry said: no, fixed. He kept moving. Without pausing he said: Mrs. Vose sent a letter to the board.

He did not explain who Mrs. Vose was. Marcus had taught her son Latin the year before; the name would locate.

He kept moving. He said: about the German-teacher question.

He was past Marcus now, at the stairwell door.

Marcus said, after a beat: I see.

Henry said: November meeting. He went down.

He did not look back. Not a meeting, not a closed door, not anything that put Marcus in the position of having to manage his face while another person watched. He was at the landing by the time Marcus would have turned to go.

---

He finished the flue bracket installation he had been deferring since September, ran the two-thirty furnace check, swept the front stair landing, and reconciled the supply delivery against the order. At three-forty-five he locked the storage closet. At four-fifteen he locked the school.

The maples over the road were nearly bare. October air, offshore, cold at the edges.

He walked home.

---

Lillian had supper. Salt fish and potatoes, which was right for October. He sat down and he told her about the complaint: Mrs. Vose, the formal letter to the board, the November meeting.

Lillian said: does he know?

Henry said: I told him.

She said: good.

They ate. After a few minutes she said: it won't go anywhere.

He said: probably not.

She put more food on his plate.

---

At the table after supper he did not think further about the complaint. He had put it in the column where it belonged: filed, Marcus notified, the November meeting six weeks out, the eighty percent.

He went to bed.
