# Drafter guidance — Coldwater Reach

## Voice register

The target register is restrained literary adult fiction grounded in historical specificity. Primary reference points: Anthony Doerr's *All the Light We Cannot See* for POV discipline and period precision; Elizabeth Strout's *Olive Kitteridge* for the Maine register and the oblique interior; Marilynne Robinson's *Gilead* for prose that does emotional work through precision rather than statement. The book is quiet, patient, and trusts the reader. No scene should announce its meaning. No chapter should tell the reader how to feel.

Three POVs, three distinguishable registers. A reader who has finished the book should be able to identify whose chapter it is from a passage alone. If the three registers are converging toward a single literary default voice, that is the primary drafting failure.

## Sentence-length expectations

Targets vary by POV character:

- **Eira**: average 14–18 words per sentence. Short declaratives (3–4 in sequence) are her characteristic rhythm. Sentences that extend are doing observation or calculation, not feeling.
- **Hannah**: average 16–22 words per sentence. More associative syntax; longer sentences accumulate sensory detail. Short sentences mark discovery or shock.
- **Marcus**: average 18–24 words per sentence. Subordinate clauses and qualifying phrases are characteristic. Emotion shortens him — when genuinely moved, his sentences compress below his norm.
- **Maximum acceptable in any POV**: 45 words. Rarely needed; if a sentence approaches this length, verify it is earning it.

## Chapter-opening conventions

Open close to the physical and procedural reality of the POV character.

- **Eira's openings**: dock, log, weather, radio. She is at work before the chapter is a sentence old.
- **Hannah's openings**: the body in the cold dark barn; what she hears before she sees; what four-thirty a.m. requires of her.
- **Marcus's openings**: a specific object he is handling, or a specific sentence he is working out — a formulation he has been revising since the previous evening.

**Do not open with**:
- An abstract statement of theme
- A weather paragraph that the POV character doesn't immediately instrumentalize
- Interior reflection floating above any physical scene
- "She/he had always known that..." — the literary attractor's most reliable first sentence

The first paragraph should place the reader in the character's body and physical location within two or three sentences.

## Chapter-ending conventions

Literary fades and resolved beats are both correct for this register. Variety is required. Target distribution across 30 chapters:

- **Resolved beats**: 8–10 (the chapter's central question settles, even quietly)
- **Literary fades**: 6–8 (sensory or atmospheric close; the scene shapes itself and stops)
- **Elliptical closes**: 4–6 (open question, unfinished gesture, deliberate ambiguity)
- **Turns**: 4–6 (new information or recontextualization — typically in the mystery thread)
- **Cliffhangers or mid-action**: 2–3 (used sparingly; never in consecutive chapters)
- **Coda**: 1–2 (aftermath beat; the final chapter is a candidate)

Never end two consecutive chapters with the same ending mode.

## POV strategy

One anchor character per chapter. Approximately ten chapters each for Eira, Hannah, and Marcus. The fourth perspective (Henry Crow, Lonzo Reed, or Karl Reinmar) may appear in 2–3 short chapters if the structure specifically requires something none of the three leads can see — not as a regular rotation.

POV character voices must not bleed into each other:
- Eira does not produce extended interior monologue in the conventional sense; her interiority arrives through procedural precision.
- Hannah lets things accumulate without analysis; she does not resolve ambiguity within the chapter, on the page.
- Marcus qualifies; he does not efface. His precision is a character trait, not an authorial stance of omniscience.

## Dialogue density

Moderate to low. Dialogue is load-bearing for character texture, not for plot delivery. The story's plot information (what Eira knows, what is happening in the barn, what Marcus is working out) is not delivered through dialogue exchanges between knowing characters. Scenes between characters who barely know each other (Eira and Marcus, early in the book) should feel like two people performing careful neutrality. Scenes between characters who share language history (Hannah and Karl in German; Hannah and Albrecht; Marcus and Henry in the evenings) carry a different charge even in brief exchanges.

Default dialogue tags: *said* and *asked*. Other verb-of-manner tags only when the manner is not otherwise apparent. No interpretive glosses on dialogue — do not write "which meant," "as if to say," or "in a way that suggested." The reader hears the line. The line is enough.

## Comic timing and tonal balance

The register is serious and restrained. There is no punchline comedy. Occasional dry wit is present: Lonzo Reed is a figure of gentle comic inadequacy; Marcus has a habit of formal understatement that is occasionally funny to the reader without being intended as humor by Marcus. The humor lives in the specific — in what Lonzo actually says, in the exact phrase Marcus writes in his notebook — and never in the narrator's frame or in ironic authorial distance.

Do not soften difficult historical material (the prisoners, the silence of Albrecht and Magda, Marcus's internment, what Eira knows) through irony or comic deflation.

## Anti-defaults — what the drafter must actively avoid in this register

- **Do not open chapters on extended interior reflection.** The literary attractor's default is a paragraph of memory or psychological meditation. This register opens on the physical. Move from the body outward.
- **Do not let Eira name her feelings.** She feels them in the language of harbor management. If she is grieving, she audits the log or checks the barometer. A direct interior sentence about longing or grief in Eira's chapters is a register failure.
- **Do not make Karl Reinmar symbolic.** He is a nineteen-year-old farm boy from Bavaria who is cold, tired, and wants a cigarette. The symbolic charge (German, enemy, neighbor's-neighbor) belongs to the reader, not to Hannah's narration, not to the novel's authorial frame. Render him specifically.
- **Do not resolve the mystery sequentially by POV.** Each character holds a piece. No chapter should land with a moment of comprehension — "now she understood" — that advances the reader toward a solution. Understanding, if it comes, is partial, provisional, and late.
- **Do not let Marcus's formality read as coldness or distance.** His precision is a survival mechanism with a frightened, displaced man underneath it. The reader should feel both layers. If the formality is all the reader gets, something is wrong.
- **Do not phonetically stage dialect or second-language English.** Albrecht's English is formal with Germanic syntax — not phonetically transcribed. Karl's English is simple, improving, and never comic. Marcus's English is careful and slightly overcorrect, the kind that learned from the page. None of these should feel like stage accents.
- **Do not place all three POV characters in the same scene.** The brief specifies they never meet as a group of three. Track this.
