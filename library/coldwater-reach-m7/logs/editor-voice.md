## 04-statement.md

**Fixed in text:** Farber exit scene — "He waited a moment, and then it became apparent there was no more to it, and he nodded and went down to his boat." The interpretive clause "it became apparent there was no more to it" reads in Marcus's analytical-qualifying register, not Eira's. Eira observes behavior; she doesn't name the apparent social logic. Removed the clause; kept the observable action: "He waited a moment, and then nodded and went down to his boat."

---

**Flagged for writer judgment — two items:**

**1. Ennis description parenthetical (mid-chapter):**
> "He was, in the particular manner of a man from a larger office with a form to complete, correct."

The syntactic form — a heavily parenthetical mid-sentence qualifier — is Marcus's signature construction, not Eira's. Eira's complex observations extend "clause by clause, as if she is running a calculation," but this sits the qualifier *inside* the predicate rather than adding to it, which is Marcus's habit. The content (reading Ennis's institutional correctness with precision) is entirely Eira; the *form* is the issue. A writer-level call: if this sentence were restructured to Eira's more additive syntax — e.g., "He was not hostile. He was correct, in the way of men from larger offices with forms to complete." — it would land as Eira's voice without losing the precision. But the current version is borderline enough that it may be intentional.

**2. "the morning had become something else" (tide-clock paragraph near chapter end):**
> "…since the Monday when Farber's boy had come at a run and the morning had become something else."

The phrase "had become something else" is slightly literary for Eira's register. Her emotional life is meant to arrive through the precision of the object (the gaining tide clock), not through a softly evocative descriptor on the morning itself. "Something else" deliberately refuses to name the feeling — which is Eira-consistent — but "had become something else" is the kind of atmospheric clause that belongs more in Hannah's register (accumulative, atmospheric, unresolved). In Eira's voice, the sentence could end at "at a run" and the tide clock does all the work. However, "something else" *is* holding back rather than naming, so this may be within bounds. Writer's call.

## 05-tuesday-german.md

**Fix applied:** Removed italics from `Goldgelb` (Hannah's narration, final bedroom section). Style guide is explicit: German vocabulary items in Hannah's narration are "not italicized, treated as part of the narrative's texture." *Goldgelb* is a single embedded word in Hannah's free indirect voice and should sit without typographic marking. (Full German dialogue sentences — *Ihr Akzent kommt aus dem Süden...* and *Das Südfeld braucht morgen früh Aufmerksamkeit.* — are a distinct case; those italics signal "spoken in German" and are consistent with standard convention for full-sentence foreign-language dialogue.)

---

**Flag 1 — Translation line after Klein's German (writer judgment required):**

> *Ihr Akzent kommt aus dem Süden. Östlich von München.*
>
> Your accent places you south, east of Munich.

The style guide says German in this manuscript is "untranslated." The bare line "Your accent places you south, east of Munich" reads structurally as an author-gloss for the English-speaking reader. However, it could equally be Hannah's immediate comprehension rendered in free indirect discourse — she grew up in her father's German and would parse this instantly. If the latter is the intent, consider a small FID anchor ("She heard it, understood it") or let the German stand alone and trust the reader; if the intent is to keep the German accessible to readers who don't know it, this is a deliberate departure from the style-guide rule that needs to be a conscious choice. Compression has already passed this chapter, so this line cannot be cut here — but the writer should confirm the intent.

---

**Flag 2 — Pronoun ambiguity: "It moved through the kitchen" (writer judgment required):**

> The doctor in Rockland had listened and examined and found nothing physically wrong. It moved through the kitchen with purpose. It knew where everything went.

"It" in the third sentence is intended to refer to Magda's silence (the subject of Hannah's theory two sentences back). But the sentence immediately preceding — about the doctor — creates a false referent: "it" reads momentarily as the doctor's conclusion (nothing physically wrong) rather than the silence. The ambiguity clears on re-reading but stalls on first pass. The fix is at the level of sentence order or a pronoun clarification ("The silence moved through the kitchen with purpose") — either of which is a compression-adjacent choice, so flagged for writer decision rather than applied here.

---

**Flag 3 — Possible Marcus-register bleed in Doris observation (low severity, writer awareness):**

> accurately, with a quality of not wanting to give the language more of herself than the task required

The phrasing "a quality of not wanting to give the language more of herself than the task required" carries a subordinate-clause formality that sits closer to Marcus's register than Hannah's. Hannah observes sharply, but her observations tend to stay in the concrete or the sensory; this clause is more analytical and abstract in its construction. The sentence is not wrong — a perceptive adolescent can think this — but if the chapter accumulates several such formal observations, they could collectively shade Hannah toward Marcus. At a single instance it's within tolerance; flagged in case it recurs.

## 11-january-harbor.md

**"go through that drawer"** — paragraph beginning *"Farber had put a second point of knowledge into the world."*

Full sentence: *"He had done it honestly, without urgency, because he had been meaning to go through that drawer and the occasion presented itself."*

Two problems in one clause:

1. **Ungrounded physical detail.** Farber's account contains no mention of a drawer. He says only "I just had it sitting with me and didn't know where to put it." Eira's voice is literal and precise; she builds from what is observed. A drawer she invented is out of character.

2. **Colloquial idiom.** "Go through that drawer" reads as casual register — the kind of phrase a character uses to mean *sort through accumulated things*. Eira does not reach for idiomatic expressions; her language is procedural and exact.

A clause-level repair would need to replace the idiom with something grounded in what Farber actually said — e.g., *"because he had carried it long enough and the occasion had presented itself"* — but that changes the meaning (removes the implication that Farber had a specific intention before reaching Merrill's). Author should decide whether to cut the intention entirely, ground it in what Farber said, or add a line to Farber's dialogue that plants the occasion.

**One borderline register note** — requires writer's judgment, not a local fix:

Near the end of the Sherbrooke meditation, Marcus is thinking about what he came to understand in Coldwater:

> "By the time he arrived in Coldwater he had begun to understand that the difference was other people, and a desk under a window, and a kitchen where someone put a cup at **your** place before **you** sat down."

Marcus's documented register is "somewhat overcorrect" English learned on the page. The "your / you" here reads as either (a) a casual generalizing second-person — slightly below his register — or (b) a deliberate reaching toward the reader at an emotional peak, where the qualifying architecture has dropped and "you" carries the warmth he won't name directly.

The case for (b) is not implausible: the style guide permits compression and register simplification at emotional peaks, and this is the chapter's most exposed emotional moment. But "your / you" sits right at the edge of what his established voice permits. If the intent is (a), change both to "one's / one." If the intent is (b), consider whether the effect is cleaner with the compression elsewhere in the sentence doing the work, and "one's" restored for voice consistency.

## 21-the-second-october.md

**Overall verdict**: The chapter is voice-clean at the word and clause level. Hannah's register holds throughout — the agricultural similes, the German vocabulary items, the associative accumulation, the short discovery sentences, the reluctance to resolve. No word-level fixes applied.

**One unresolved formatting question for writer's judgment:**

The extended German dialogue exchange at the wall scene uses a convention — German line, then italicized English translation on the next line — that sits in tension with two style-guide directives:

1. *"Do not gloss dialogue."* The italicized translations (*Will the mortar hold if the ground freezes deep.* / *If it sets right. You have to let it cure.* / *Ten days. Maybe two weeks. Depending on the temperature.*) function as subtitles. The style guide's "do not gloss" caution targets "as if to say" and "which meant" constructions, but subtitle-style translations operate on the same principle: they instruct the reader on what was said rather than trusting them with the line.

2. *"German phrases in Hannah's narration surface as vocabulary items, not performance."* The translation lines mark the German as requiring explanation, which moves it from embedded texture toward performed foreignness.

The earlier German in the chapter — *Herzlichen Glückwunsch*, *Danke, Vater*, *Wie viele Kühe?*, *Dieselben wie letztes Jahr?*, *Wie lange* — all work without translation because their meaning is either recoverable from context or inferrable from the word's register in English. The full mortar-exchange sentences are longer and less inferable.

The author needs to decide: leave the German exchange fully untranslated (trusting context and the surrounding description of what Karl is doing with the trowel), or keep the translations but strip the italics and fold them into the narration as reported speech. The current subtitle-on-its-own-line convention is the one option that most clearly conflicts with style.

**Note on Karl's italicized English phrases**: *That post wants setting.* / *Come on, old girl, come on.* / *Cold moving in, Lonzo says, from the northwest.* — These are italicized to mark them as Karl's absorbed Lonzo-idiom, a distinct register within the narration. This is defensible (the italics signal "borrowed voice, not narrator's voice") and does not conflict with the style guide's German-phrase rule, which governs Hannah's narration specifically.

## 29-spring-log.md

Two register slips in the Owen log-reading passage that require writer's judgment. Both are interior emotional labeling — the failure mode `canon/style.md` defines for Eira: "She does not produce interior monologue in the conventional sense. Her emotional life reaches the prose only through the precision of her professional notation."

**Issue 1 — naming grief by negation:**
> "She had read it in October 1943, the night before the body came in, sitting at this desk until midnight with a particular quality of attention she would not have called grief."

"she would not have called grief" is interior emotional labeling even in the negative. Style guide: Eira's emotional life surfaces through professional notation (objects, numbers, gaps), not through naming or refusing to name an affect. Suggested direction: replace the clause with a physical/procedural detail from that sitting (stove cold, lamp on, tide clock audible — something observable). The grief will register without the word.

**Issue 2 — interior analysis of the act of reading:**
> "This time was not those times. She was reading it as someone who had finished what it was in the middle of."

Both sentences name the emotional meaning of re-reading the log entry — what the reading *is*, what it signifies about where Eira now stands. This is exactly the post-scene subtext naming the style guide forbids for Eira. The preceding sentence "She read it" and the subsequent physical action (closing the page, turning back to the current date) carry the beat; these two sentences caption it. Suggested direction: cut both and let "He had stopped mid-entry to go put out the mooring lines" open the paragraph directly after "She read it." The reader will feel the difference in this reading without being told what it means.
