
## 01-the-breakwater
**Summary:** Monday morning, October 12, 1943. Eira Bowman is at the harbormaster's shack before dawn — winding the tide clock (4 minutes fast), writing the morning log entry (NNW 9 knots, barometer 29.8, tide high 0647), running the radio check. Owen's last log entry from November 12, 1935 stops mid-sentence on a page she has never removed. Farber's youngest son arrives at a run: a man at the seaward end of the breakwater, not sleeping. Eira walks the 200-yard granite breakwater in fog, finds a middle-aged man in a city wool overcoat and leather city shoes, palms up, back against the light-standard pedestal. She returns to the shack and radios Lt. Ennis (Coast Guard, Portland); checks the Callie B. bilge pump while waiting. Ennis arrives at 0852 with two enlisted men and a Graflex. He questions Eira: she says she was at the dock around 11 p.m. checking the Callie B.'s bilge pump, saw nothing unusual, left at 11:30. The Coast Guard removes the body and takes log pages 114–119. Eira writes the new October 12 entry — discovery time, arrival/departure, pages removed — and closes the log. She does not write what she saw the previous night. The tide clock still reads four minutes fast; she never reset it.
**New facts:**
- Body found at seaward end of Coldwater Reach breakwater, October 12, 1943. Male adult, middle-aged or older, dark double-breasted city wool overcoat, white shirt, leather city shoes (ruined), no ring, no bag, no identification, empty pockets, palms upward.
- Eira's stated reason for being at dock Oct 11 at ~11 pm: checking bilge pump on Callie B. (Farber's dragger, second slip). Pump found running clean. True but incomplete.
- Lt. Ennis (Coast Guard, Portland) arrived 0852, departed 1008. Two enlisted men. Graflex camera. Photographed body and hands separately.
- Log pages 114–119 (October 9–12) removed by Lt. Ennis per First District Portland request. Formal statement from Eira scheduled for later in the week.
- Harbor log: earliest entry June 1891. Owen Bowman's entries stop mid-sentence on the November 12, 1935 page. Eira has kept the same ledger since.
- Tide clock in harbormaster's shack: runs fast by 4 minutes per week; Eira resets it every Monday morning. On this Monday she never reset it — the morning consumed the routine.
- Callie B.: Reg Farber's dragger, 4 years old, second slip. Slow leak at port quarter, bilge pump running clean as of Oct 12 1943.
- Morning conditions Oct 12, 1943: wind NNW 9 knots easing, barometer 29.8 steady, tide high 0647, fog returned overnight.
- Eira wears Owen's peacoat, taken in at shoulders and re-hemmed in spring 1936, brass buttons her own addition.
- BC-1206 radio set is on the south wall of the harbormaster's shack.

## 03-the-crow-house
**Summary:** October 14–16, 1943. Marcus's German class: Hannah Pfeiffer absent, Doris Hatch and Louise Pelletier construe Lessing's Minna von Barnhelm with competence and no feeling. Marcus writes "Wohin? / Where to?" below the passage without explaining why. Afterward, in the hallway supply room, he hears (but is not addressed by) two teachers trading what they know: no papers, no name, Coast Guard took the log. He files the fragment — no papers, no name, not from here — with philological precision. He walks the nine minutes to 14 Elm Street; the harbor briefly visible from the ridge. The Crow house: white clapboard, Henry with the Rockland paper, Lillian with real coffee. Marcus reports the body; Henry says "Found him Tuesday?" then returns to the paper — a receipt, not a dismissal. Marcus reads this correctly after months of learning. In his room (second floor, east-facing, slanted ceiling north wall), he works the Zenith portable shortwave: finds the BBC on 9.41 megacycles, loses it, finds it again; news from Italy and the Pacific and the convoys; then full static. Sitting with the static he approaches but does not name the parallel: a dead man with no papers, dressed for something and arrived at the wrong end of it — and his own August 1941 arrival in Boston with a suit that didn't fit and a document classifying him as resident alien enemy. He notices (and does not pursue) the thought. He notices the English handwriting on the spine of his German notebook. He turns off the radio. Literary fade: room dark and cold, harbor somewhere in the dark, where he lives for the moment.

## 04-statement
**Summary:** October 18–20, 1943. Eira finds the auxiliary station note Monday morning (signed by Osgood) and sets it on the corner of the desk. She spends two days doing ordinary harbor work — fuel logs, the anemometer mount, the Mabel Lena registration, the inner-cove ice — without reconsidering what she will say. On Tuesday Farber appears, mentions he'd heard the Coast Guard was coming; she gives him nothing. Wednesday at nine, Lt. Ennis arrives with a yeoman and a Remington portable. The shack is too small; the typewriter takes the desk, the harbor log goes to the windowsill. Ennis takes her through October 11 and the morning of the 12th. Her answers are exact, limited, and technically true: 22:45 at the dock, bilge pump on the Callie B., no individual observed, no vessel not previously seen, no unusual activity. When Ennis asks whether the dock lighting near the breakwater steps would have made it difficult to observe activity there, she says yes, the lights are kept low. He writes something, makes a check, and moves on. She notes the tide clock at 9:17 and does not need to note it. Ennis tells her: civilian clothing, city-quality overcoat, shirt collar intact, man in his forties or older, medium build, considerable time in water or weather before the breakwater — consistent with the three-day blow. The investigation is not formally closed but there is no information sufficient to pursue. The record will be held in Portland. She reads the typed statement — accurate in every particular — and signs. Ennis shakes her hand and leaves. She writes the log entry for October 20 and sets the tide clock back four minutes. She hadn't reset it on October 12 or on October 18. She works the fuel forms until noon.

## 06-at-the-harbormasters
**Summary:** Friday, November 7, 1943. Marcus walks down from the school to the dock to collect a school book shipment that arrived on the mail boat Heron from Rockland. The crate requires the harbormaster's counter-signature on the freight manifest before the school can take it. Marcus has been preparing, in his philologist's way, an oblique approach to what he actually wants to know: what Eira Bowman knows about the body on the breakwater. In the shack, the freight business takes four minutes and is precise and efficient. He thanks her and does not leave. Standing at the harbor window, he asks — without inflection — whether there has been further word about the body from October. She gives the official line: Coast Guard, First District Portland, no identifying information. Without emphasis. He asks whether any vessels that put in during the blow had been connected to the man. She says no. He asks, obliquely, whether it would have been unusual for an individual not connected to any vessel to come into the dock area at night in that weather. She answers a question about vessels seeking shelter in a three-day blow. He hears that she answered a different question. He takes in the shack interior — the tide clock above the window, the BC-1206 on the south wall, the log ledger on the desk, and the visible change of hand in the ledger's earlier pages (the older, looser hand and the current compressed regular hand) — and says nothing about it. She hands him the manifest (school's copy) and he looks at it: above the freight description, in her compressed hand — 'Collected 7 November 1943, 14:10.' He cannot account for this annotation in any documentation chain. He tucks it in his pocket and goes. Outside, walking up the hill with the crate, he arrives at the clearest thing he has understood about Coldwater in twenty months: she answered every question she chose and left the rest intact without indicating that this was the activity. He knows the skill. He has used it in other rooms and other years. This is not comfortable to recognize about a woman he has just spoken to for seven minutes. Whether there is anything to do with this understanding is a separate question.
