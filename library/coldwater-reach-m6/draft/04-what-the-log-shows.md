# What the Log Shows

The winter mooring schedule ran to three pages. She had been at it since seven.

Each vessel in the harbor required a notation for the season: status, date of last mooring inspection, condition of the ground tackle, whether the lay-up slip had been filed. The form went to the state harbor office by end of October and it was already two weeks behind. The early-October blow had taken her inspection window. The ten days after had taken more than that.

She was on the south float list — three entries, two of them vacant since September, the third the *Penelope D.* still showing active though Galey had hauled it in August — when Mercer's car came down the harbor road at nine-twelve.

She had been expecting him without quite expecting him. Ten days since the body; a full week since the last word from Sprucehead. She set the pencil flat on the schedule and watched the gray sedan pull to the foot of the ramp. He came out with a manila folder under his arm and his hat on correctly. She had not looked at him carefully on the morning of the twelfth — the body still on the breakwater, the blow still running — but now in the flat midmorning she could see he was perhaps thirty-five, efficient, unhurried. He had the walk of a man who had learned to move through working harbors without being in anyone's way.

She went to the door.

"Lieutenant," she said.

"Mrs. Bowman." He stopped at the top of the ramp, hat in hand. "I wanted to come by before we filed anything. Have you got a few minutes?"

"Come in. I'll put the kettle on."

He came in and sat in the chair across from the desk without waiting. The folder went flat on his knee. Through the south window she had the full run of the south dock — planks gray with the morning damp, the cleat lines empty along both rails, the float lines running down to the high-tide rings. Still incoming. Not yet at the top.

She put the kettle on and turned.

"I'm closing the case," Mercer said. "Filing by end of week. I wanted to tell you in person."

"What did you find?"

"Very little." Without apology.

"Description went to Portland, to Rockland, to Castine, to Searsport. Nothing came back. No vessel in the district short a man. No family inquiry matching the description. The Portland and Rockland harbormasters have nothing." He looked at the folder on his knee without opening it. "The coat was examined. Maine made, most likely — no mill marking, nothing traceable. Ten years old at least. The trousers were newer."

She thought of the coat. Heavy navy wool, kept up well, lapels still clean. Town clothes. The coat of a man who had somewhere to be.

"The medical examiner," she said.

"Probable cardiac event." He said the qualifier precisely. "There was no water in the lungs. He was dead before he went in."

"Not a drowning," she said.

"Not a drowning." He turned the folder in his hands. "Heart stops — coronary event, stroke — and then the storm carries him. Or someone put him in after, which we can't rule out. But there's no evidence for it. No blunt trauma, no ligature, nothing to call it otherwise. The ME says cardiac and we have nothing to contradict." He set the folder on the corner of her desk. He left it there, closed. "No evidence of crime. No identification. There's nowhere further to take it."

"The body," she said.

"County burial if there's no identification by end of November. The file stays open, technically." He looked at her directly. "I know that's not a satisfying answer."

"It's the right one given what you have," she said.

The kettle came up. She made two cups — cup, tea, water, nothing added — and set one in front of him and sat down with her own. He wrapped both hands around it. The barometer on the wall had held at thirty and two since Thursday.

They sat in the kind of silence a formal visit has when most of its business is done. On the south dock, a gull landed on the near cleat and found nothing and left.

"Was there anything in the days before the storm," Mercer said, "that you'd want on the record? Unusual vessels, anything in the harbor not registered." He had the pencil out, not quite on the folder. Checking rather than writing. He had done this before — given the question its full form without pressing it.

"Nothing in the harbor log I didn't account for," she said. "No unregistered vessels in the days before the storm."

Both sentences were true. The dock was not the harbor.

"The night of the tenth," he said. "Were you out?"

"In bed at ten. Up at four. Standard before a blow."

"Nothing in between?"

She had walked out at eleven-fifteen for the barometer. Southeast seventeen, steady, the fleet on their moorings. She had come back in. An hour later she had been at the south window because the wind had come up and she wanted to see the outer moorings. She had not left the shed. She had stood at the glass.

"Barometer at eleven-fifteen," she said. "Seventeen southeast. I came in and went to bed."

Everything in that sentence was accurate.

He made a short mark on his notepad. She watched his hand stop.

"Nothing after that," he said.

"No."

He capped the pencil. He looked at the harbor for a moment. Then he said, without changing his tone: "Off the record. Not the file. Did anything strike you — before or after? Anything you noticed but didn't know where to put?"

She had known a question like this was coming.

Two figures on the south dock, past midnight, the night of October tenth going into eleventh. One past middle age, solid, dark coat, looking toward the outer harbor. One wider, heavy across the back, feet planted the way a man stood on solid ground. Neither a fisherman. She had no name for either of them, no vessel associated with either of them, nothing that attached to any record she kept or any record she knew.

She was not a detective. It was not her case. All of this was true.

"Nothing I could put a name to," she said.

Mercer looked at her. He was not an incurious man; he was a patient one. He held the look one beat past what the visit required. Then he nodded, picked up the folder, and stood.

"You run a clean log," he said. "I appreciate it."

"It's the record," she said.

---

She walked him down to the car. She extended her hand before he could decide about it — she had learned, in the first year of the post, to do that first — and he shook it and got in. He turned around in the gravel and took the harbor road north, careful driver. She watched until he was past the chandlery and into the long curve toward Sprucehead.

The harbor was ordinary. The sardine carrier *Ellsworth* was in her usual berth, back from the Eastport run ten days ago, the crew gone home. The Gale brothers' trap hauler, the *Clara M.*, was at the inner float, traps covered and cordage coiled. The moored fleet rode head-to on the northwest, lines just taut. The tide still coming.

She went back up the ramp and shut the shed door.

---

The current logbook was on the shelf above the telephone. She lifted it down and opened it to October.

The entry for October 12 ran three lines, the times in the left-margin column.

*Body discovered at 0615, breakwater elbow, S. face. Male, apparent age 50+. Navy wool coat, wool trousers, no footwear. No identification of any kind. CG Sprucehead notified 0622. Lt. A. Mercer and two enlisted arrived 0711. Body removed to CG custody 0723. Storm damage survey in progress. Harbor status nominal at 0600.*

Every fact correct. Every time exact to the minute.

She had written it at seven-thirty that morning, less than two hours after the body was removed, sitting at this desk with the shed door still cold from the blow. She verified each time against her scratch notes before she committed the ink. That was her practice for any entry another person might read.

She laid the book flat on the desk.

The addendum she could write: *October 11, approx. 0015 — two figures observed, south dock. Male, two individuals, unidentified. No vessel registration.* Margin note, bracketed, cross-referenced by date. Proper form for a supplemental entry. She could write it in two lines.

What she had: a shape through the south window at past midnight, the dock lit only by the overcast sky and the weather light on the near piling. Two men. She was confident now, in the way she was confident about a hull seen twice in different conditions, that the nearer figure — solid, dark coat, looking toward the outer harbor — was the man she had found at the breakwater elbow the following morning. Not proof. The kind of confidence that did not belong in the column until it was proof. The second figure: wide through the back, feet planted differently, inland posture. No name. No vessel. Nothing a reader of the log could act on.

An entry that could not be completed was not an entry.

She turned the page.

October 13. October 14. The harbor continuing.

---

Through the south window the dock sat in flat midday light. The tide had come in another eight inches since she'd gone to the shelf; the float rode high on its lines, the slack taken up. The *Penelope D.*'s old mooring ring was just beneath the surface on the near side — she could see it at this tide, a dark circle under the chop.

The column was wrong. Not false — she had written nothing false, and she had said nothing false to Mercer — but wrong the way a dead reckoning position went wrong when you had not accounted for the set of the current. Calculated position was one thing. True position was another. The gap between them grew with distance.

*Insufficient to file* was both a legitimate reason and a convenient one, and the difference between a legitimate reason and a convenient one was whether you could stand behind it when the distance grew. She could stand behind it. She did not have enough. What she had was two shapes in the dark on a dock in October, attached to nothing she could name in the harbor record. In eight years of the log she had not put unnameable shapes into it.

That was the line she was standing on. The entry was wrong and she accepted it, the way she accepted any open condition she was waiting on data to close: not as exoneration, not as excuse, but as an account with a balance outstanding.

She went back to the desk.

The mooring schedule was where she'd left it, the pencil across the open page. South float, two pages remaining. She pulled it in front of her.

The *Penelope D.* — hauled since August — was still listed under active south float moorings. She drew a single line through the entry and wrote *hauled August 1943, mooring struck* in the margin column, neatly, in the place for it.

The entry in the log was wrong somewhere else. The mooring schedule wanted two more pages. She moved to the next line.
