# Henry Crow

The boiler came on at five forty-five — not because Henry had left it that late, but because he had set the stoker timer the night before and then arrived at five-thirty to run his own check before it fired. There was a sequence to managing a firebox this size in November, and the sequence began before the fire did. He went through the pressure gauges on the main line and the secondary, tapped the return valve, opened the bypass briefly to clear the overnight sediment, and closed it again before the stoker kicked. He had been doing this for sixteen years. The building's warming from a cold night to a workable morning was entirely a function of what happened in the hour before anyone else came into it, and he would not have known by anyone's behavior whether he had done it correctly or not.

He had done it correctly for sixteen years.

The school ran the heat through radiators in the twenty-six rooms and the gymnasium and the three-floor central corridor. Each radiator had its own cycle — the ones on the north side ran slow in November and would need the secondary bleed valves opened by early December; the ones on the south side ran hot and were the first to develop the ticking in cold weather that someone reported to the principal's office at the first staff meeting of every autumn as though the tick were new. It was never new. Every October somebody sent a note down. Every October Henry adjusted the expansion valve and the ticking stopped. He had been adjusting the expansion valve for sixteen years. The boiler itself had been in the building since 1914. He had the system in his head the way he had the bus route in his head: not as a sequence he thought through, but as what was next.

His title was Chief Custodian. The children in Coldwater Reach spent their school days in warmth or in cold depending on what he did in the hour before they arrived. These facts did not appear in each other.

The town had ways of knowing who did what and ways of accounting for it, and the two were not the same. Henry had understood this since 1928, which was his first year at the school, and since well before 1928, which was the rest of his life.

---

Marcus Klein had come to the Crow house in March of 1942.

Henry had known before Marcus arrived that the school board had hired a Continental educator for the German class, and that the Continental educator required lodging, and that Lillian had agreed to take him in. He had also known — because he had been in the building every day for fourteen years and had learned what a man needed when he arrived somewhere new — that the Continental educator would want to know what kind of place Coldwater was, and that the understanding he developed would take the shape of whoever was first kind to him.

What Henry had watched for two and a half years was a man performing the temporary resident with very great expertise. The expertise was recognizable because Henry had seen variations of it before, though in different registers. The temporary resident kept his things organized. He held his opinions in reserve. He did not say *when I was in Vienna* except occasionally, and when he did say it he said it as evidence, not longing. He walked everywhere and said good morning to everyone on the street. He graded papers after supper and in his free hours translated things from one language into another, apparently for the exercise, filling small notebooks that he stacked on his windowsill beside whatever stone or piece of metal had caught his interest that season.

What Henry had also observed — and had not said, because it had not been asked — was that somewhere in the past year the performance had begun to wear through. Not through exhaustion. Through practice. A man who had practiced being a temporary resident for long enough had, without deciding to, become simply a man who was here.

Marcus came downstairs every morning before Lillian had the coffee finished. He sat at the table in the chair that had become his chair. He said something precise and not performed about the overnight weather, the state of the furnace in the basement (which ran low on Tuesdays), the condition of the world as reported by the radio. He had been doing this for two and a half years. It was the quality of a man who had stopped expecting himself to need the exit.

Henry had not said any of this to Marcus. Marcus had not asked.

---

The letter from James had come last Tuesday.

Henry had brought it in from the box before Lillian was up, had set it on the shelf below James's photograph, and had stood with his coat still on looking at the handwriting. James's handwriting had not changed — the same even slant, the same narrow letters, the capital J that curved a little further than it strictly needed to. The letter was shorter than the ones that had come before.

James's letters in '43 had run to two and three pages. A young man writing when he had things he was allowed to say and things he was not and was still finding the shape that held both. This one was a page and a half. Tight in the way of a letter from a man who had decided, with care, what to leave out.

*The work is hard.* That was the sentence, halfway down the first page, in the middle of a paragraph about the weather and the food and a chaplain named Delmas who had been good company on the transport ship. In James's earlier letters the equivalent sentence had been *this is a strange place* or *I am keeping busy* — the general-purpose sentences a man writes when the specific ones are not available. *The work is hard* was different. It was the precision of a man who had stopped pretending the imprecision was sufficient.

Henry had not said this to Lillian, because Lillian had read the letter and had not said anything about *the work is hard* either.

That evening Marcus had been at the table and Henry and Lillian had talked with him about the school board meeting and a minor repair to the front steps. The letter was on the shelf, under James's photograph, in the place where the letters were kept. The photograph showed James in uniform — the studio background, the cap on straight, the same face Henry had been watching for twenty years and would know at any distance.

---

He had been thinking, lately, about October a year past.

On the twelfth — he knew it was the twelfth because it was the day before the body came in, and the body had been the talk of the building for the rest of that week and the next, and the sequence had fixed itself — he had taken the five forty-five bus as he always did, because the boilers wanted feeding before the building filled. The bus went north on the main road toward the school. He had been in it three minutes when he saw the man on the harbor road.

The man was on foot, going the other direction, toward the pier. The bus came around the curve past the Baptist church and Henry had had a clear view of ten seconds, maybe less: the man's back, then his side as the curve came around. Heavyset, not young. Square in the shoulders, a laborer's build. Wool coat with the lapels turned up, which was reasonable at five-forty in the morning in October. He was not from Coldwater. Henry had been watching the people who moved through this town for long enough to know a Coldwater walk from a coming-from-somewhere walk, and this was the second kind — deliberate, not hurried, a man who had somewhere to be and was going there without announcing it.

Henry had noted him and looked away, because the bus went on.

He had thought about it once, in the first week, when the talk in the school hallways had settled on the body and what could be known about it. He had thought: a man I didn't recognize, on foot on the harbor road, before six in the morning, going toward the pier. He had not thought it useful information, because the man had been alive at five forty-five, and the body had come in that afternoon after a three-day blow pushed the outer anchorage in. He had not gone to the Coast Guard with it, because what he had was a ten-second view from a bus window, and that was not what the Coast Guard called evidence.

He had let it sit.

And then Marcus had asked him, in the kitchen, sometime in the winter, whether he had ever heard of a man named Werner Grau. Marcus had asked it in the careful way he asked things he wasn't sure he had the right to ask. Henry had said no, because the name meant nothing to him, and this was true.

The name had stayed with him, the way names did when they arrived from a man who was usually careful about what he said and was being careful about that too.

He had put the name alongside the figure on the harbor road — the build, the coat, the direction of the walk — and he had put both of them alongside the talk of the body, which had been described in the schoolroom hallway by a seventh-grader who had gotten it from his father: a man nobody recognized, heavyset, no papers on him, probably off a vessel. Henry had not said anything in the hallway. He had listened and continued to what was next.

The three things — the figure, the body, the name Marcus had asked about — had a specific gravity when placed together.

He had not told Marcus. He had said no when Marcus asked, because the name Werner Grau had meant nothing to him then, and this remained true. What Henry had was not a name. What Henry had was a heavyset man in a turned-up coat, walking toward the harbor in the dark before the storm, who fit the description of someone Marcus was asking about a year later with the careful precision of a man who was not asking casually.

Whether that was useful to say, he had not yet decided.

---

He banked the stoker for the mid-morning cycle and checked the pressure gauge one more time. The secondary line held at its value. The north-side radiators would want their bleed valves by the first week of December, as they always did. He turned off the work lamp, took his coat from the hook, and went out into the corridor.

Upstairs the building was filling: chair legs, a door, the percussion of books dropped on the wooden stairs. The school was warm. Nobody had said anything about the warmth since October first. He did not expect them to.

James's letter was in his coat pocket. He put his hand to the pocket without taking the letter out — just to feel the paper's weight against his palm, the page and a half of James's careful leaving-out. Marcus would be at the table tonight. Lillian would have the supper on.

He had his piece of it.

He went up the stairs toward the light.
