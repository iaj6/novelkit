# Chapter Thirteen — Displaced Scholars

The translation papers were in alphabetical order, as Marcus had trained himself to keep them, and the name at the top was Pfeiffer.

He had six students in the advanced German section. Two were seniors who had enrolled to satisfy a modern-language requirement and who wrote translations that were technically correct in the manner of things produced under mild compulsion. Three were juniors of varying ability; a boy named Graves had a natural ear for German syntax but lacked the patience for the long clause, which was a correctable problem Marcus had been correcting since September. The sixth was Hannah Pfeiffer, who was also a junior and whose work Marcus graded first not because she required more attention — she required less than any other student in the section — but because her translations tended to establish the ceiling for the evening's task, and the ceiling, once identified, made everything below it easier to assess.

Tonight's passage was from Schiller. Five lines from *Die Bürgschaft*, short and subordinate-heavy, with two dependent clauses that required the student to decide where the sentence's weight fell. The predictable failure with such constructions — the one he had spent half the autumn correcting in the intermediate section — was to flatten them: to take the weight of a dependent structure and distribute it evenly across the sentence, producing translations that were accurate and empty. In the advanced section Marcus was marking for something a Vienna professor he had once admired called *die Entscheidung der Unterordnung*, the decision of subordination — not whether the student could identify the clause but whether they understood what its dependence was doing to everything around it.

He read Hannah Pfeiffer's rendering:

*Though he who asks is master of the hour, the thing asked for lives in a different grammar.*

The original was more compressed; she had given herself room. The word *grammar* was her own interpolation, absent from Schiller's German, which had *Zeit*, time. As a rendering it was less accurate than Schiller's word. As a reading of the construction — of what the subordination was for, of why the clause held its position in the sentence rather than dissolving into the main verb — it was more precise than anything Marcus could have specified in the assignment.

He took up his pencil and wrote in the margin: *The grammar of the subordinate clause is not decoration — it is the shape of what is being withheld.* He considered the sentence for a moment, then left it. She would understand what the note was about, or she would ask him on Friday, and either would serve the instruction.

He moved through the remaining five papers in forty minutes. The classroom was empty; Henry had come through with the push broom at half past three and not returned. The afternoon light through the south-facing windows had thinned — March, longer now than February and not warm, the late light catching the dust on the glass at a low angle. The heating pipes ticked in the wall behind the blackboard. Forty minutes, and he set the graded stack to his left and uncapped his pen.

He had been composing the letter to Sondheimer in his head for nine days. The content required no composition — a name, a description, a question. What had taken nine days was the framing. In twenty years of letters written under circumstances where the wrong word could establish the wrong thing, Marcus had learned that the architecture of a request carried as much weight as its substance.

Dr. Heinrich Sondheimer's position at Columbia was nominally in the German department, but his actual function — as Marcus had understood since his own time on the Emergency Committee rolls, in the autumn of 1941 — was closer to that of an archivist of displacement. He had been on the intake end of the rescue network that the Committee had assembled as the war made rescue necessary: the person who matched names to placements, who held the files, who knew which scholars and artists had passed through the system and which had simply vanished somewhere in the gap between one country's border and another's paperwork. It was not an official position. It was a position constituted entirely by memory, by the particular willingness to keep track of people because someone ought to. In Marcus's experience, such positions were more reliable than official ones.

He wrote:

*Dear Dr. Sondheimer,*

*I hope you are well. I write on a matter of administrative record that falls, I believe, within the range of your particular knowledge. I am trying to establish the history of a Werner Grau — German national, painter or laborer by occupation, last known address Portland, Maine — specifically whether he appears on any Emergency Committee roll or affiliated rescue register, and whether his immigration status in the United States was ever formalized. I ask as a matter of private inquiry, not on behalf of any institution or authority. I have reason to believe Mr. Grau may have been in the Boston area around 1936, and that he has been in the United States since approximately that time.*

*Any record you hold, or any channel you might pursue on my behalf, would be of genuine assistance.*

He read the letter back once. The phrase *a matter of private inquiry* was accurate and included what it did not state. The phrase *any channel you might pursue* communicated that this was a question for informal handling — through the network of people who held such things in filing cabinets and memory rather than in any official record.

This was approximately true.

He was addressing the envelope when the thought arrived: what he had described was Marcus Klein in 1940.

A man without the correct documentation in a country not constructed for him. A man whose standing depended on a committee's willingness and a series of telephone calls between people who had been, at least, willing to make them. A man who could be required, at any moment, to demonstrate what he was doing here and whether anyone official had approved it.

He had been, in the autumn of that year and the winter that followed it, a Werner Grau.

He did not write this in the letter to Sondheimer. He did not write it anywhere. He pressed the envelope flap closed and set it on the right side of the desk where he kept things requiring the post. Then he sat for a moment in the quiet of the classroom — the graded papers on his left, the thin March light leaving the window glass, the heating pipes gone still — and held the thought in the way he had learned, at Sherbrooke, to hold thoughts that had nowhere to go.

---

The road from the school to Henry and Lillian's house was three blocks toward the harbor and then one block west. He had walked it for two years in every condition this section of the Maine coast offered, and he had come to know it with the intimacy that daily repetition produces: not attachment, exactly, but familiarity so complete it had become spatial memory, each crack in the pavement belonging to its season. The spring thaw was at its worst this week — the road heaved, the edges soft, the ground giving back what it had been holding since December. He knew the smell of it. He had not expected, when he first walked this road, to come to know its smell by season as a fact of his own life, the way he knew the smell of the Ringstraße in December or the particular damp of the Paris sixth in autumn. He knew it. He walked through it.

He came in at the side door, as Lillian had told him to in his first week.

She was at the table with her cup and a length of white fabric, a piece of church sewing Marcus had seen in various states of progress for the past three weeks. Henry was across from her, and between them lay a V-Mail letter — the small photographed form, reduced by the postal process, that was what arrived when something arrived from the Pacific. The letter had the position of a thing already read and placed back with care: not put away, not left carelessly, but replaced as if putting it elsewhere had not yet been decided.

"James," Lillian said. She had not looked up.

He set his coat on the hook. "Good news?"

"He's well." She smoothed the edge of the envelope. "Somewhere he can't say."

Henry did not add anything. He drank his coffee.

Lillian read a portion of it aloud. The part she chose: James had received a meal that had given him a new and firmly held position on the subject of Army provisioning, which he had committed to paper — he noted it was the most cogent military document of the current conflict — and sent up the chain. Two weeks later, on a separate ship, a crate of canned peaches had arrived. He took this as the official reply. He asked whether the school boiler was behaving. He asked whether Mr. Marcus had been informed about the spring mud, and if not, whether someone could see to it before the term was too far gone.

Marcus said he had been informed.

Henry smiled briefly — reserving, as he always did, the full extent of it.

Henry stood and rinsed his cup at the sink. He crossed to the small shelf above the kitchen radiator — the shelf that held the clock, the accumulated mail, and the one or two objects a household gathers over twenty years without deliberate selection — and placed James's letter under the clock. He had done this with every letter from James since the first one, in the spring of 1942. The clock held the paper flat. The letter did not go anywhere else.

He stood for a moment and looked at the photograph on the wall.

James in his uniform, taken at Camp Upton before he shipped — standing before some building Marcus did not recognize, shoulders squared, the expression of a man being photographed: present, composed, held slightly in reserve. The photograph had been on that wall for eighteen months.

Henry looked at it for perhaps half a minute. He did not say anything. Then he went through to the back of the house, where a pipe had been making a small complaint since the previous week.

Lillian said nothing. Marcus drank his coffee.

He had written letters like James's letters, from Paris, from London, from Sherbrooke in the months when letters were permitted, and the letters had been full of meals and small complaints and inquiries about things at home that were not the things he was thinking about. He knew what James's letter contained that was not in the portion Lillian had read. He did not say this. He sat with his cup and listened to the pipe being attended to in the back of the house, and when Lillian refilled his cup he said thank you and meant it precisely.

---

Sondheimer's reply came on a Friday, nine days after Marcus had posted the letter.

He found it in his school mailbox before the first class — addressed in Sondheimer's direct hand, the handwriting of someone for whom a letter is a professional instrument. Marcus read it standing at the mailboxes, then took it to his classroom and read it again at his desk, with the deliberate attention the contents required.

Sondheimer had made a telephone call to a contact in the Boston office. That contact had made another. The answer: Werner Grau, German national. Entered the United States at Boston in March 1936, on a one-year visa sponsored by the Boston Alliance for the Arts, which had brought over a small group of painters and printmakers under a cultural exchange program that ended when its funding was redirected in 1937. The visa had been renewed once, in March of 1937. It had expired in March of 1940. After that: no renewal application on record, no deportation proceeding initiated, no departure recorded.

He had been in the country without legal standing for nearly four years. From the spring of 1940 to October 1943, Werner Grau had been in the United States in the way that many men in those years had been in countries not their own: by the extension of circumstances originally intended as temporary, prolonged by a war that had made the simplest crossings impossible, sustained by the patience of whoever around him had chosen not to ask.

Marcus placed the letter beside the folder in the bottom drawer of his classroom desk: his transcription of the letter Werner had written to Albrecht Pfeiffer, set down before returning the original in January; the two pages of careful handwriting Marcus had made for himself in December, his private account of what he knew and the chain by which he had come to know it. Three documents. They occupied the space where a Latin dictionary usually rested. He looked at them briefly, then closed the drawer.

Werner Grau had been, by the classification the relevant governments applied, an enemy alien without formal standing. As had Marcus Klein, at Sherbrooke, in 1940: held for ten months under a warrant whose precise language Marcus had never been shown, released to the Committee rolls in a country that had not yet settled what to do with him. The form of Werner's situation was different. The underlying condition was the same: a man whose right to be where he was could not be demonstrated to any authority that chose to examine it.

If the body on the breakwater had been identified — if the file at Sprucehead bore a name rather than a blank — Werner Grau's four years without papers would have become the first fact of the official record. A German national, undocumented, found drowned on the Maine coast in October 1943. The sentences organized themselves cleanly. They were not false. They were simply not an account of anything that mattered about Werner Grau.

He sat with the drawer closed and the translation papers on his left, marked, ready for return in the morning. On top: Hannah Pfeiffer's Schiller, the word *grammar* in the third line, the note in his own hand in the margin. The March light had gone off the roofline of the building opposite. The heating pipes had been quiet for an hour.

He was not going to report Werner Grau's status to any authority that could act on it.

Whether this constituted his doing something, or his failing to do something, or simply the act of a man who understood that what mattered about Werner Grau was not the state of his papers — that question remained open, in the drawer, alongside everything else he was holding.
