# Misdelivered

The mailbox slot for K held, on most days, nothing, or a circular from the state education department issued at what struck him as deliberately irregular intervals, or a late assignment slipped through the school office rather than handed to him directly. He had been collecting his mail from this slot since March of 1942 and had developed a reliable expectation. Today there was the circular and a student paper, the latter with Cornelia Foss's name written in the right corner, the handwriting precise and — he may have been reading too much into handwriting — slightly apologetic, as if the paper were aware it had arrived by a less direct route than she typically chose. He held it for a moment before taking it.

Beneath the paper was a white envelope.

It was addressed to someone else. *A. Pfeiffer, Coldwater Reach, Maine.* The handwriting on the envelope was forward-leaning and careful — a hand that had been taught to write in a particular tradition and maintained the teaching without either elaborating or neglecting it. German Kurrent in the baseline construction, the letterforms and capital treatments identifiable once he looked for them, altered somewhat by years of American paper and American habit but not dissolved. Not the hand of an academic. Not the hand of someone who had learned to write imprecisely. The hand of a specific generation with a specific schooling, and the country of origin of that schooling was not ambiguous.

Mrs. Ledbetter had paper-clipped a note in her own script:

*Dr. Klein — This came to the school by mistake. As it's in German, I thought you'd be best placed to handle it. —M.L.*

He read this twice, standing at the office counter. Then he put the letter on top of the circular and went upstairs.

---

He closed the classroom door. He did not usually do this; the door stood open as a matter of preference, for reasons that were not complicated to explain but that he preferred not to explain. He closed it now because the letter seemed to require the absence of the hall sounds.

He sat at his desk and examined the envelope.

Return address: a street in Portland, Maine. He did not know the street. Postmark: September 28th, 1943. The envelope had the slight softness that paper acquires when it has traveled an indirect route or been held somewhere before final delivery — though whether this was the postal history or simply the autumn damp he could not determine from the paper alone.

He turned it over. The adhesive on one corner of the flap had let go slightly.

The letter did not belong to him. He had not been authorized to open it; he had been asked, by a pragmatic school secretary who had identified his utility correctly, to "handle it," which admitted a range of interpretations, the most reasonable of which was: deliver it to the Pfeiffer farm, since that was the nearest interpretation requiring no further decision on his part. The Pfeiffer farm was eleven miles from Coldwater. He did not own a car. The afternoon in November was already beginning its early failure. Whatever Albrecht Pfeiffer needed to know about his mail could survive the delay of a more practical conveyance.

But he set the practical argument aside and turned to the other one. If he returned the letter unopened — through the postal service, through the office, through any route that kept him at a distance from the contents — it would arrive at the Pfeiffer farm as a sealed envelope in German, which Albrecht Pfeiffer could read. It could also arrive as a sealed envelope in German at a farm that housed four German prisoners of war under military escort, in the hands of a local postal system, and its existence as correspondence in German at such an address might attract the sort of official interest that correspondence in German at such an address attracted in November of 1943. This inference may have been unwarranted. He could not know if it was warranted without reading the letter.

And — the simpler argument, which was also the more suspect — he was the only person in Coldwater Reach who could read what was written on the other side of the envelope. Mrs. Ledbetter had recognized this and said so plainly. He recognized it himself. It was not nothing.

He was not unaware that neither of these arguments would have satisfied a stricter examination.

He slid his thumb under the loosened corner and opened the envelope.

---

The letter was two pages — a single sheet of the same paper as the envelope, written on both sides and folded once. The handwriting inside was the same as the address, the Kurrent influence more visible in its natural medium, the letterforms settling into the page with the confidence of a hand that wrote this way habitually rather than carefully. He read it through without stopping.

*Lieber Albrecht.* Dear Albrecht. The *lieber* was the word of an old friendship — not the formal address, not the perfunctory salutation, but the word that meant: I knew you before whatever separated us. The German of the first page was domestic, careful, the German of a man who had spent considerable time away from the language and had come back to it here as to a tool not used in years — deliberate in the joints, but accurate. He had settled in Portland some time ago. He had been thinking of Albrecht for longer than he had found it possible to write. He had hoped to arrange a visit and believed now that he had managed it. He planned to come up the coast in October. *Nach so vielen Jahren.* After so many years. He wanted to see how Albrecht was managing.

The second page was more specific.

He had heard that they had sent Reinmar's boy to the Pfeiffer farm. *Reinmars Junge.* He understood the restrictions — the regulations — he was not asking Albrecht to do anything that was not permitted. But if it was possible, only if it was possible, to tell the boy that someone was thinking of the family, that would be something. Because he had news of the Reinmar family in Ansbach. He had been carrying it for some months now. *Ich trage es seit Monaten.* He did not say what the news was. He said only that he had it, and that it seemed to him that a son might want to hear news of his family, whatever the nature of that news, and that he would say more when he arrived.

*Ich komme, wenn ich den Transport einrichten kann.* He would come when he could arrange the transport.

He hoped for Albrecht everything good that could still be hoped for, and he remained — *Ihr alter Freund, Werner Grau.*

Marcus set the letter on the desk.

Werner Grau. Portland, Maine. An old friendship with Albrecht Pfeiffer that had preceded Pfeiffer's emigration, which put the origin of the friendship before 1922, which meant Bavaria or somewhere proximate to it. A man who had also come to America, at some point, by some route, and had made his way to Portland. A man carrying news from Ansbach that he had been holding for months.

In September of 1943, a man in Portland, Maine, was carrying news of a specific family in a specific Bavarian town — news he had held for months, news he would not commit to paper but would bring in person when he came. The possibilities for how such news traveled in 1943 were not numerous. He was not going to resolve them from this chair.

He turned the letter over and read it again.

---

What expanded on the second reading was not the content but the weight of it — the specific word *trage*, to carry, something physical rather than something merely known. Werner Grau had not heard this news recently; he had been carrying it. That was a different sentence.

Marcus set the letter down and looked at the window. The playing field was empty, the November grass pale and reduced to its essential function.

He knew what he knew about the man on the breakwater from Stillman's staffroom account: no papers, town clothes — not a fisherman, not a sailor, not a man who had been on the water before he died — came in after the October storm, the Coast Guard had taken it, the case was closed, no family had come forward, no inquiry had matched the description. Portland medical examiner, Stillman had said, with the qualified confidence of someone transmitting information he had received at two removes.

September 28th: Werner Grau wrote from Portland, planning to travel up the coast in October.

Postmark to arrival: three weeks, which was sufficient. A man who was coming when he could arrange the transport, which he believed he had now arranged. A man who knew Albrecht Pfeiffer, who knew the farm, who had reasons — he would not say reasons but it seemed likely — for arriving at an address housing German prisoners without announcing himself through official channels.

Town clothes. No papers.

The connection was plausible. He turned this over with some care, because plausible was a large and undiscriminating category that contained many things that did not survive contact with evidence. There were other men in Portland in September of 1943 who had planned to travel north in October. Some of them had done so and arrived safely and were currently making no demands on anyone's attention. What he had was a postmark, a window of time, and an absence — Werner Grau had planned to come and there was, so far as he knew, no record that Werner Grau had come. Absence was not evidence. Plausible was not probable.

He also could not unthink it, which was a different problem.

The question of what to do with plausible was not one he could answer this afternoon.

He opened the center drawer of his desk, set the envelope at the back of it, and placed the Latin dictionary over it. He closed the drawer.

He sat with his hands flat on the desk. The return of the letter to Albrecht Pfeiffer was still available to him; it was simply a different return than it would have been an hour ago, because the letter had been read and that could not be undone. Reporting the connection to someone with standing to act on it was possible in principle. In practice he would be reporting a letter he had opened without clear authority, and an inference he could not confirm, to an authority he could not identify, about a case that Lieutenant Mercer had already closed. He weighed this and did not arrive at a decision, because the decision was not yet his to make; the decision would present itself when he had more information, if more information came, and until then what he had was a letter in the desk drawer.

He pulled the Foss paper toward him and read the Cicero translation. It was, in fact, quite good — she had handled the genitive absolute with more confidence than most students managed, and the translation of the participial phrase was precise without being labored. He made three marks in the margin and put it in the folder for Thursday's class.

---

He locked the classroom at the end of the day. The school was emptying in its familiar way: voices thinning down the corridor, the light already going at four o'clock, the smell of chalk and floor wax mixing with whatever the boiler produced as it came up to the evening's demand. He went down the central stairs and out the side door.

He walked south on Main Street with his coat buttoned and his hands in his pockets. At the harbor intersection he went left along the harbor road rather than up Elm Street. He did this when the day had not finished with him and the walk had something still to do — the harbor in November held his attention in a way he had not examined and did not intend to, the cold stripping it back to something that was not harbor use but simply the fact of the water's edge, which was in November more legible than in any other season.

He walked past the boatyard and the hull on its railway, past the inner float where the trap hauler rested. The light was nearly gone from the south, and the water was gray and flat and quiet in the way it went quiet when the wind had settled. He walked along the harbor's southern edge and came to the south dock, which stood on its pilings with the tide running out and the planks dark from the day's cold. He looked at it as he always did when he passed it, without particular purpose, in the way a man looks at what stands at the edge of his regular view.

He turned and walked up Elm Street. The harbor went behind him and then the boatyard and then the lower road's cold, and above him the Crow house window would have the kitchen lamp burning by the time he arrived. His footsteps went uphill in the dark, and Werner Grau was in his desk drawer under the Latin dictionary, and the plausible connection between a postmark and a body on the breakwater moved with him up the hill and did not ask anything of him yet.
