# Advanced German

The bus from the Pfeiffer road dropped her at Main and Birch at seven fifty-two, and Hannah was inside the school building before the cold had finished with her collar. The building took her in: chalk and floor wax and, already, the cafeteria working at something that had been on the stove since six, a smell that had no precise name and did not require one. She hung her coat on the third hook from the left and checked the weather board at the office end of the hall — northwest, twenty, gusting thirty — before going down to the ground floor.

Third period was advanced German. Room 114, southwest corner, smallest classroom in the school. At the start of term, Marcus Klein had asked Mr. Crow to move the extra desks out, because there were three students and the gap between three students and twenty empty desks was not a ratio that served anyone. Roland Bates had told Hannah this story in the first week with some incredulity, as if the rearrangement reflected on someone. She had not said anything.

The class was Roland, who was there because his mother had arranged it and made this legible in every sentence he attempted, and Patrice Audette, who was the genuine article and knew it without making a show of the knowing, and Hannah. She had been doing German homework at the kitchen table since she was twelve, sitting across the room from a man who used the language as a country he could live inside. Whatever shape she had of the language, she'd learned it alongside that.

They were three days into the second stanza of the Rilke. *Herbsttag* — Marcus had put it on the board at the start of the week with the note written below the title: *We are working on syntax. The poem is the occasion.* Below that, the two stanzas. Below those, brackets, arrows, clause-structure notation, with the poem's actual subject — autumn, a man with no house, solitude moving in like a weather system — visible through the scaffolding but not addressed.

This morning he started from the first line of the stanza: *Wer jetzt kein Haus hat, baut sich keines mehr.*

"Roland. The subject of the second clause."

Roland looked at the page in front of him. He looked at his page with a consistency that implied something would eventually be on it if he waited long enough. "Wer."

"The same *wer* as the first clause?"

A pause. "Yes?"

"Tell me why."

Longer pause. Patrice was looking at the ceiling in the way she looked when she was waiting for something to become apparent to someone else.

"Because it carries across," Hannah said. "One subject, two predicates. The *wer* takes both."

Marcus drew an arc on the board connecting the two verb phrases. "Yes. The indefinite relative pronoun distributes without repeating. That's its purpose — it names a category once and the structure holds it." He turned. "The *keines*. Case."

Roland looked at his page again.

"Accusative," Hannah said. "Object of *baut*."

"And the *sich*?"

She thought about how to put it. "Dative. The building is for himself. Whatever he's not building, it's his own not-having."

She hadn't planned to say it that way, and she saw Marcus turn the chalk in his right hand — the small physical notation she'd come to read as satisfaction, not performed.

"Exactly. The dative makes the absence personal. He has no house, and not having a house is something he does to himself, or that does itself to him — the German leaves the direction unspecified, marks only that the self is involved." He wrote the sentence clean on the board, without brackets: *baut sich keines mehr.* "Read it beside your translation. Tell me what the English costs."

Roland produced a translation slowly, as if constructing it on the way. "'Whoever has no house now builds himself none more.'"

"The 'none more' is awkward. What is *mehr* doing?"

"Saying he won't build any."

"It's stronger than 'any.' It's 'further,' 'any longer' — the building has been decided against. The finality is already in it." He wrote on the board: *builds himself nothing further.* "That's still not right. The precision has no equivalent. You choose what to lose."

Patrice said: "'Nothing further' loses the sense that it was always going to be nothing. The *mehr* makes it retroactive."

Marcus looked at the board for a moment. "Yes. The *mehr* folds in the past. The English says it's over. The German says it was always going to be over." He set the chalk in the tray. "That's what makes it difficult. Note it."

She wrote: *retroactive finality. the German folds in the past.* She wrote it because it was worth having, even if it was too large for any exam she would ever take.

She watched him move to the third line, chalk in hand. He wrote one word and stopped, waiting for Roland to catch up. The pause was unhurried, calculated to the minute — not impatience held back but patience given precisely. Hannah had been watching it since September and had not said anything.

At the bell they were two lines from the end.

---

At lunch, the two girls at the table by the windows were still on the body. More than a month now and the story had grown its shape: a merchant sailor, someone had heard, come down from Eastport or up from Bath depending on who was telling it and which direction made the drowning feel more final. He'd been drinking somewhere up the coast and gone over a dock rail. Or he'd been running from something — a debt, a wife, a thing he couldn't put behind him — and the October storm had been waiting for a man in exactly that condition.

Hannah ate her sandwich. She had a piece of data that didn't fit the shape of any version on this table: her father's hands on his knife and fork at the dinner table, stopped for three seconds at the words *no papers*, the knife set down without deciding to, and then resumed. That data did not attach to the merchant sailor. It did not attach to any story being assembled here. It was not a thing she could put on this table, at this price, in this form. She ate and listened and the merchant sailor acquired one more detail she had no reason to believe.

---

After class Roland left immediately and Patrice was already gone. Hannah stayed because she had a question that was not a pretext for anything else — just a grammatical question she'd been carrying since Monday that her notes hadn't answered.

"The *es*," she said. She read the line: *Wer jetzt allein ist, wird es lange bleiben.* "I can't find what it's standing in for."

Marcus turned from the board where he'd been erasing the bracket notation. He looked at her page without appearing to need it.

"It's not standing in for anything earlier," he said. "It anticipates. It holds a place in the syntax for the predicative adjective — the aloneness — which the grammar already knows is there." He went to the board and wrote: *wird es lange bleiben.* Below: *wird … allein bleiben.* "In German—" He wrote the word: *Korrelat.* "The correlate. A placeholder the structure makes for something it already holds. The sentence knows its meaning before it has finished stating it."

She wrote *Korrelat* in the margin and put a bracket around the *es.*

"My translation has 'will long remain so,'" she said. "I wasn't sure the 'so' was right."

"It's better than 'it,'" he said. "The 'so' implies continuation — the condition already established, extending forward. That's accurate to what the German is doing." He considered it for a moment, the chalk still in his hand. "Your question was a real one."

She put the notebook in her bag. He had said *Korrelat* before he reached for the English for it — the way he always reached for the German word first and the English second, as if the German were the thing you could rely on and the English were the approximation you settled for. Her father's German was nothing like this. Her father's German was a retreat — a door he kept shut since the war started, something he moved into when his English wasn't safe anymore. Marcus moved toward German the way he moved toward precision; it was the direction of accuracy, the closer register. She did not have a word for the difference and she was not going to look for one in room 114.

---

Dinner was salt pork and potatoes from the root cellar. She set the seed catalog at her father's place before she sat down and he reached for it as he sat, without comment, in the way he reached for anything that was properly his to reach for. He turned to the back half first — root vegetable prices, winter grain, the columns of numbers — before moving forward through the variety pages. He did this while he ate with the attentiveness he gave to anything with a practical season coming. Her mother was at her place with a straight back and ate enough that you couldn't say she wasn't. The radio was off, the way it had been for most of November.

They ate.

---

After the dishes she brought the German translation to the kitchen table. Her father moved to the sitting room with the farm accounts, the small desk by the window, and she could hear the sound of his pen through the wall — long strokes for the main columns, short ones for corrections.

She spread the Rilke page.

The *Korrelat* note in the margin, the bracket. She moved on to the last two lines: *und wird in den Alleen hin und her / unruhig wandern, wenn die Blätter treiben.* The *treiben* was where she kept stopping. To drive, to be driven, to drift, to blow — she'd tried four versions since Monday. She tried "when the leaves are driven," but "driven" in English wanted a driver, a thing pushing, and the German had no driver; it had just the fact of the motion. She tried "when the leaves run," which was wrong. "Drift" was closer but it lost the driven quality, the sense that the leaves were not choosing. She put a question mark beside "drift" and wrote both versions under the line.

She had been working with the language for five years and still hit the words that wouldn't cross. Her father never hit these walls because the language was not a wall for him; it was the room he lived in. She was working on its ceiling.

She was finishing the fair copy when she heard the icehouse door.

---

She put the pencil down and went to the window to draw the curtain.

Karl was outside without a coat over the POW jacket, his arms at his sides, his head tilted back. The sky was the November clear that arrived after the first hard frost — stars close and particular, the cold making the air exact. He was standing still.

He said the word.

*Seidenschwanz.*

She said it at the glass, barely a breath. *Seidenschwanz.* October's word, coming back. The same quality to it — not a complaint, not addressed to anyone or asking anything of anyone, just the naming of something he knew. Saying it to the sky to keep the knowing current.

He stood a moment longer. Then he went inside. The lamp in the icehouse window was low; she watched it dim through the oil paper until it was gone.

She pulled the curtain and went back to the table.

Her father's pen moving in the accounts. The stove ticking as it cooled.

She opened the German translation to the Rilke page. Below the *Korrelat* note, below the bracket, below the question mark beside *treiben*, she wrote *Seidenschwanz* in the margin.

It had nothing to do with the poem. She drew a small circle around it to keep it from the rest.

She closed the notebook and left the lamp on, because her father was still at the accounts across the room.
