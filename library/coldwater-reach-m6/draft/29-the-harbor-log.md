# The Harbor Log

The county harbor authority convened at half past nine in the Rockland county building, third floor. The room had two tall windows on the east wall and a rectangular table long enough for the panel with an additional chair for the party under review. Eira arrived at nine fifteen, signed the attendance ledger in the corridor, and took her seat.

The panel filed in at nine thirty. Carver, the retired vessel inspector — white-haired, in his seventies, with the particular economy of movement that came from decades on working boats — took the rightmost chair and had read down the first two pages of her summary document before Pratt settled into the center. Pratt was the county board member who had initiated the proceeding; he set a folder on the table with the care of a man who had been waiting some months for the occasion. Haverford, from the county maritime licensing office in Camden, took the leftmost chair. She was neutral, local, harbor-familiar — there to make the proceeding formally complete.

Pratt called the proceeding to order. He read the resolution: the county board had convened a formal review of the continuity of appointment in the harbormaster's office at Coldwater Reach, to determine whether the office had been administered in compliance with the county maritime charter. Four minutes of reading. Eira followed along on her copy.

"I brought additional copies," she said when he finished, and distributed one to each seat. Her copies were tabbed with numbered flags at eight years of records: the harbor logs, the mooring registry, the fuel allocation ledger, the safety inspection reports, the annual reports filed each December.

Carver started with the logs. He asked how entries were made — contemporaneous or summarized.

"Contemporaneous," she said. "Each day's entry before midnight, dated and signed. During severe weather the entry might be written at first light the following morning covering the night's events, but it's dated to the day of occurrence."

He asked about the mooring registry — whether vessels were logged in and out or only in, whether seasonal moorings were billed differently from transient. She walked him through the September 1944 and September 1943 registries. He read them. He asked about the fuel allocation ledger. She had ledgers for all eight years; he read the September 1944 reconciliation and the quarter-end figures for three additional quarters. He asked no questions she could not answer with the open document in front of him.

Pratt asked about the October 1943 safety inspection. Who had conducted it. Whether a second party had countersigned.

She had conducted it. There was no countersignature. The harbormaster's assistant had been drafted in January 1942; no replacement had been authorized. She had conducted the inspection herself, in her capacity as acting harbormaster, and had noted one deficiency — a mooring cleat on the north face of the fuel dock with a cracked base plate — and arranged for its repair. She had the work order. Repair completed November 17, 1943.

"Your own signature on your own inspection," Pratt said.

"The charter requires the harbormaster to conduct or supervise annual safety inspections," she said. "The harbormaster conducted it. Whether the harbormaster conducts or supervises is at the harbormaster's discretion under the relevant charter section." She turned to it and put her copy on the table in front of him.

He read it. He put it aside.

Haverford had not yet asked a question. She had made two notes on her pad.

At eleven o'clock Pratt came to the matter of appointment continuity.

He read from the November 1935 county board resolution: *Eira Bowman is hereby appointed acting harbormaster of the Port of Coldwater Reach, effective December 1, 1935, pending formal review and confirmation by the county harbor authority.*

"The review," he said, "was never conducted."

"It was not," she said.

"The charter requires review and confirmation within sixty days of appointment. That review was not completed in sixty days. From the record before me, it has not been completed in eight years. The board's position is that an acting appointment without formal confirmation constitutes an unresolved appointment."

She had the 1935 letter in front of her. "The appointment letter is in the record," she said. "Signed by three board members. Issued by the authority that had the power to issue it. The authority subsequently failed to conduct the review the charter required. That failure belongs to the authority, not to the administration of the office. The harbor has been operated without administrative irregularity for eight years. Twenty-seven safety inspections. One deficiency noted, corrected within thirty days. No incident entered in the harbormaster's casualty column." She looked at him. "If the authority would like to identify a failure in the administration of the office, I'll address it. The record is here."

Pratt looked at his folder.

Carver turned a page.

Haverford wrote a note, read it, and wrote another beneath it.

The panel recessed at eleven-thirty. Eira went to the corridor.

She stood at the window at the end of the hall. Below the county building, Rockland harbor ran out toward the breakwater — a dragger working past the head, its course straight and deliberate for the open water; a smaller boat coming in from the outer islands, sitting low and slow with traps stacked aft. The dragger was heading offshore; the lobster boat had been out since before light and wanted its dock.

Coombs came down the corridor at a quarter past twelve. She had worked with him for a dozen years; he ran traps off the Caldwell Bank and landed his catch at the cold-storage house at the pier. He had his hat in one hand and a folder under his arm and was going somewhere on some other county errand. He was nearly past the window when he caught sight of her.

He stopped. He nodded and she nodded back.

He went on.

She stood at the window until the clerk came to the corridor door.

---

The vote was two to one. Carver read the finding: the authority was satisfied that the harbormaster's office at Coldwater Reach had been administered in compliance with charter requirements for the duration of the appointment. Eira Bowman's appointment as harbormaster was confirmed, effective from the date of original appointment, with tenure now formally registered in the authority's records.

Pratt had voted against. He said nothing after the finding was read and left while Carver was still signing. Haverford held the certification across the table and gave her a look.

Eira signed her copy. She put it in her case with the summary documents.

She walked out of the building and across to the truck.

---

The coast road went south with the sea appearing between the headlands in sections — grey water, the March light flat and white on it, patches of dirty snow on the north-facing slopes where it hadn't seen sun since December. She drove without the radio. She stopped once for gas, which took a coupon she had been saving since February. She ate the sandwich she'd packed from home outside Thomaston and watched a crow work the road verge for half a minute before the cold made staying pointless.

The harbor came into view around the south headland at half past four, the late March light going pale. The boats on the moorings had swung to face the same direction. She could have named the state of the tide before she had an angle on the water.

She parked at the top of the pier and walked down.

---

The shed was cold; she hadn't had the stove going since morning. She laid a fire and got it started and stood at the window while it caught. The south dock lay in the March dusk below — the gray water against the pilings, the boats tied and riding, the breakwater a dark horizontal line across the harbor mouth. The navigation light at the elbow would come on at five-thirty.

She sat at her desk.

She opened the middle drawer.

The Portland letter was on top, folded into thirds: the harbor authority inquiry returned, *W. Grau, 114 Franklin Street, Portland, Maine.* She had read it enough times that the address was a piece of furniture in her head. She took it out. Beneath it the CG form — blank where her signature should go, the form for reporting additional information relevant to a pending investigation, which she had picked up at the Rockland station in the spring of 1944 and brought home and put in this drawer.

She read both of them.

The case had been officially closed since late 1943. The CG's finding: unidentified male, probable drowning, storm-related, no identification found on person or clothing, no next of kin established. No additional reporting had come. More than a year. No letter from Portland. No inquiry at the pier, no one at the counter asking about a man from Portland who had gone missing in an October blow.

She had a name and a Portland address. She had a dinghy — stove in and beached in the south cove — registered to that name. She had her dock observation from the night of October 11, 1943, and she was about to put it where it belonged.

She did not have anything that would serve Werner Grau. The form, completed, would go to an addendum in a closed case — that was all it could accomplish. She had served the machinery of record for eight years. She knew when it served a purpose and when it did not.

She folded the CG form along its creases and put it back in the drawer.

---

The harbor logs were on the bottom shelf on the north wall: eight of hers, the first of them beginning in November 1935, and behind hers Owen's volumes going back before that — his handwriting that got faster and more abbreviated over the years as he settled into the work, the last of his stopping in November where November had taken him.

She took down the 1943 log. She carried it to the desk.

October. She turned to October.

The October 12 entry was in the middle of the page, below the October 11 night entry and above October 13. She had written it at seven in the morning, after no sleep, in the plain language that a seven-in-the-morning required: *Body discovered outer breakwater face, 0610 hours. Adult male, no identification found on person or clothing. Coast Guard notified 0620 hours. CG arrived 0740 hours, Lieutenant and two enlisted. Body transported 0820 hours. Weather at time of discovery: NW 30, gusting 38, seas 8–10 outer harbor. Vessel traffic nil.*

Below it, three lines of space before the October 13 entry.

She got her pen from the top drawer.

She wrote in the space below the October 12 entry, in her regular hand:

*0055: two men observed south dock. One unknown. One familiar by gait, farm-type. Neither vessel registered in port. Weather deteriorating.*

Nineteen words. Her handwriting matched the rest of the entry — the same pen, the same hand she'd used since the first of November 1935. The ink was the same black.

She closed it.

She sat with it on the desk for a moment. Then she stood and carried it back to the north wall and slid it into its place between the volumes on either side.

---

She banked the stove, turned off the lamp, and locked the shed door behind her. She walked to the end of the pier.

The navigation light at the breakwater elbow was on. The harbor was dark except for that light and the mooring light at the pier head, and the harbor's own quiet sounds: water against the pilings, a halyard tapping somewhere on a moored boat, the low creak of the cold-storage building settling in the ebb.

The tide was going out. She could tell by the sound — the slap against the nearest piling was lower than it had been at four-thirty, the sound coming up from a different angle. She stood there long enough to be certain, and she was certain.

The certification was in her truck. The entry was in the log.

She turned and walked back up the pier.

She went home.
