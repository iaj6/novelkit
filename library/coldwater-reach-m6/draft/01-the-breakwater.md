# The Breakwater

The outer face had taken green water.

She could see it from the headland stairs — the wrack line sitting high on the granite, a length of tarred cable draped across the top course, the iron mooring rings at the elbow gone dark with wet. She went down.

First light. Northwest wind out of Canada, fifteen knots and dropping. The blow had peaked sometime around two in the morning and then, the way storms sometimes did on this coast, had simply stopped. By four she'd been lying awake listening to the silences between gusts. By five she'd been dressed. The air coming off the water now was cleaner and colder, carrying nothing with it but temperature.

She had her clipboard.

The top course was intact along the north arm's full length — no slippage, no voids. She moved toward the elbow, checking each granite face, feeling the joint conditions where she had to. Some of the stones she knew well enough to read by looking; others needed a hand pressed to them, checking for any give. The rubble fill had held. The crown height was unchanged along the north arm.

She noted the cable. Tarred, fairly new, and it didn't belong to any vessel currently in the harbor. She'd check the boatyard, see if something in storage had lost it. She noted it for inquiry and for removal.

At the elbow, the course had shifted. An inch and a half toward the inner harbor, maybe two. She measured by eye and then by hand and noted it. The elbow always took the worst of a southeast blow, and what she was looking at was not as bad as 1939. The mortar joint at the south corner had widened. She put her finger in it: a quarter inch, three-eighths. Before the freeze, someone would need to point that joint; otherwise the ice would open it further.

The repair list for the breakwater had been three items before this morning. It was now five.

She wrote them all down.

The kelp had piled waist-high against the inner face of the elbow, black and dripping, salt-stinking. Woven into it were a coil of pot warp, a length of buoy line, the slats of a wooden crate that was stamped with something she didn't recognize — from a vessel, or perhaps from the boatyard if the storm had lifted something from the rails. A glass-ball float. A plank with a bronze fitting through it, the bolt stripped, which meant it had been working loose before the storm claimed it.

She went around the elbow, and saw the man.

---

He was face-down in the wrack, in the inner corner of the elbow where the kelp had piled deepest. The storm geometry was right for it: anything coming in from the southeast over the outer edge would have been carried into exactly this corner. He was lying with his arms at an angle — the left arm back along his body, the right arm out and slightly under him — that said he had not arranged himself there.

She stood for a moment.

The light was improving. She could see well enough.

Town clothes. A heavy wool coat, navy, with lapels that had no business on a working waterfront. Trousers, not work trousers. No boots anywhere on the breakwater that she could see, no oilskins, no gear of any kind. No vessel anywhere in the inner harbor she didn't know.

She set the clipboard on a flat section of granite and crouched down.

She checked his pockets.

Inside the coat, left breast: nothing. Right breast: nothing. Outside pockets — in the left, nothing; in the right, a folded handkerchief, and below it, grit. His trouser pockets, each in turn: nothing. Nothing. No wallet. No papers. No draft card. No ration book. No keys. Nothing with a name on it.

She stood up.

Male. The hands said fifty or older — the thickness across the knuckles, the looseness of the skin on the back of them. The hair was grey, close-cut, a working man's cut. The wool coat was good quality, that much was clear even soaked through. The trousers were wool as well. Somebody's travelling clothes, or a man's good clothes, or both.

Nothing about him said this harbor.

She picked up the clipboard. She walked back to the shed at the pier head.

---

Inside, she called Sprucehead.

The duty officer answered on the third ring. She gave him everything in order: her name, her title, the station address, the date, the time — six-twenty-two by the clock on the wall — and then the description. Male, she said. Face-down in the wrack at the elbow. Middle-aged or older. Town clothes. No papers, no identification of any kind, nothing to indicate a vessel or how he had come in. She had not touched him beyond checking his pockets. She had not moved him.

The duty officer said he would send someone. She thanked him and hung up.

She put the kettle on the single-burner plate. She checked the barometer — risen three-tenths since midnight, consistent with the storm's track, consistent with what she could see through the north window: the sky clearing fast from the northwest, the cloud deck breaking up at altitude, showing the grey beneath. She recorded the barometer reading in the tide log and noted the wind direction.

On the shelf above the telephone, seven logbooks stood in order. She'd started the current one in January, and its spine was still dark and square. The six before it went back to 1936. The one before those — thinner, its spine faded to the color of old water, Owen's handwriting on the label in the particular heavy-slanted hand he'd used for anything official — was set a little apart from the others, at the end of the shelf where the bracket met the wall. She had not moved it. It wasn't where she would have put it if she'd been setting up the shelf from scratch.

She poured a cup of tea and went back to the breakwater to wait.

---

The wind had dropped to ten knots by the time the Coast Guard truck came down off the main road onto the pier. She was standing at the breakwater's head when Lieutenant Mercer got out with his two enlisted men.

He was younger than she'd expected — mid-twenties, with the winter uniform pressed correctly and a manner that was efficient without being brisk. He introduced himself and looked at her when she gave her name.

"You're the harbormaster?" he said.

"Eight years," she said.

She walked him out to the body.

He crouched the way she had crouched, looking before he touched. He checked the pockets — the same ones she'd checked, and two more. He stood up. He asked her: when had she found it, had she moved anything, had she contacted anyone else.

No, she said. No. No.

"Any record of a vessel in difficulty? Radio calls? Report from boats that came in before the blow?"

"Nothing in the log from the past three days. No distress calls. Two draggers out to the grounds and back in before it peaked. The sardine carrier *Ellsworth* running to Eastport — she went out before the storm, due back the eighteenth."

"And no vessels you can't account for."

"Nothing in the harbor I can't account for."

He wrote in a small notebook. He was thorough rather than curious. He asked whether anyone else had access to the breakwater during the storm, and she said no one would have come out on it while the blow was on, and anyone could have come since, but she'd found no footprints in the wrack. He wrote that down as well.

She gave him the harbor log for October 9 through 12. He looked at it carefully and handed it back.

The two enlisted men brought the stretcher down from the truck. The operation was careful and correct. She watched from a few feet away and he did not ask her to step back. By the time the stretcher was going back up the breakwater, she had answered two more questions and had learned almost nothing except that the lieutenant's first name was Amos, from the nametag she'd seen when he turned his coat.

"We'll be in touch if we have questions," he said.

"I know where to reach you," she said.

The truck pulled out of the pier at seven-twenty-three.

---

She finished the breakwater survey. She walked the full length again from the shore end, checking each section now that nothing was in her way. The damage added one more item — a section of the north face's drainage where the fill had settled — and she added it to the list. She wrote the estimate for the elbow repair while she was still on the breakwater, because the light was good and she could see what she was writing about.

Back at the shed, she opened the harbor log to the day's page.

She wrote for ten minutes. The entry ran to half a page.

*October 12, 1943. Sunrise 0611. Wind NW 15kts at 0600, dropping to 10kts by 0800. Barometer 29.74 at 0600, rising. Post-storm survey, breakwater, 0615–0730: granite course displaced at elbow (est. 1.5 in. inward), mortar joint widened at south corner of elbow, iron mooring rings displaced (2), kelp and debris accumulation at elbow, drainage fill settled N. face — all noted for fall repair.*

*Vessels: all moored vessels accounted for. Sardine carrier* Ellsworth *Eastport run, ETA Oct. 18.*

*0615: Unidentified male, deceased, recovered at breakwater elbow. No papers, no identification of any kind, no associated vessel. CG Sprucehead notified 0622. CG responded, Lt. A. Mercer and two enlisted, 0711. Body removed to CG custody 0723. CG assuming jurisdiction.*

She put down the pen.

She read it once. It was accurate. Every time was correct.

She set the logbook to one side.

---

It was not yet eight-thirty. The first boats wouldn't be in from the grounds for another hour, maybe two. She stood at the shed's south window.

The south dock was empty. She could see its full length from here — the grey planking, the pilings standing in the flat water, the dock light still burning at the head of the dock, unnecessary now in the morning. The Gale brothers had moved their trap hauler to the inner float in September. The dock had not been in regular use since then.

The night before it had not been empty.

October the eleventh, going into the twelfth. Past midnight. She had been at this window watching the barometer fall. The blow was building. She had already gone out and checked the mooring chains on two of the draggers — around nine, ten o'clock — and had come back to the shed to wait. The chains were what they were. There was nothing more to do in the dark in a rising wind.

Two figures on the south dock. The dock light behind them both.

One she had not recognized. He was looking toward the outer harbor. Dark coat. The build of someone past middle age — not heavy, but solid, the kind of solid that sets into men who have worked outdoors for a long time. Standing still in the way of a man who had come somewhere specific and arrived.

The other one she had not seen clearly. Half-turned from her, the dock light cutting across from the wrong angle. The shoulders. The way of standing. Not a man whose body had been built around boats — she knew that much. Wide across the back. Feet planted like a man used to standing on flat ground, not a deck. Somebody from inland.

She had thought: two men on the dock before a storm. She had thought: their business, not mine.

Now she looked at the harbor log, open on the desk.

The entry was complete. Each time was correct.

These were separate facts. It was possible they had nothing to do with each other, and the log entry she had written was true in every particular, and there was nothing more to do.

She picked up the pen.

She held it.

She set it down.

The south dock stood empty in the flat morning light. The harbor beyond it had gone from grey to silver as the sky cleared. The tide was going out.
