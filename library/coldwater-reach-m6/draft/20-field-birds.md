# Field Birds

By noon she had made seven trips from the pump to the field.

The south field was down: her father had started the mowing before the light was up, and by the time she came out to milk at six the windrows were already lying in their long parallel lines across the field, the green going pale in the August morning. The baler was running before breakfast. She knew the sound of it from every August of her life — the engine's steady knock, the thump and clank of the bale chamber, the sharp clicking of the knotters that meant the machine was working right — and she had long since stopped hearing it as sound. It was what August was. The field going from windrow to bale to stacked wagon in passes and passes until the south field was bare and the hay was in.

The heat was complete by ten.

She walked the fence line with the water bucket. The cut-grass smell was everywhere — the sweet fermenting smell of hay in the August sun, the dusty smell of it when the baler shook it down. She had her sleeves rolled and her hair under the old straw hat she kept on the back porch for fieldwork. The back of her neck was damp. Ahead of her the tractor crawled through its next pass and her father's silhouette was small against the far end of the field, the exhaust coming up straight into the dead August air.

She gave him water first. He drank without looking at her, used his handkerchief, put the machine in gear. She watched him go and then went down the fence line toward the near wagon.

Dieter took the dipper and said something she couldn't hear over the baler. She nodded. He handed it back. Rolf and Hans in sequence, coming off the wagon for thirty seconds and returning to the work. Then the baler rounded the north end of the field and the noise changed quality and she could hear what was under it — the insects in the uncut grass along the fence, the dry clicking sound the heat made in the cut windrows, a crow somewhere on the far side of the treeline calling once and not again.

Karl came last.

He took the dipper and drank and stood looking at the fence for a moment before handing it back. She reached into her apron pocket.

---

She had picked up the notebook from the windowsill that morning when she went past it, without deciding to. Now she held it out.

It was a composition notebook in its second summer of use — the covers gone soft from handling, the corners worn through to the cardboard, a water stain low on the back from a September she had been getting ahead of herself. Twenty-three entries now, two columns across each page: German on the left, English on the right, the scientific names above in her best handwriting. In the margins were her drawings — pencil, done from life where she could and from memory where she couldn't, accurate in species and imprecise in anatomy. The kingfisher looked like a hammer. The hermit thrush had too much neck. The cedar waxwing, drawn twice, was the best.

He took the notebook from her carefully — more carefully than the notebook required, or exactly as carefully as it required; she wasn't sure which. He turned each page from the bottom corner. He was reading both columns.

She watched his face.

*Rotkehlchen / Robin* — he stopped on that one. *Krickente / Green-winged Teal* — he looked at the sketch. *Blaumeise / Black-capped Chickadee* — she could see him placing the English, his lips moving the faintest fraction.

The baler went past on a return pass and the noise was everything for thirty seconds.

When it moved away Karl was on the first page.

*Seidenschwanz / Cedar Waxwing.*

The drawing was the best one: a fat-bodied bird with a crest and the yellow tip of the tail, the red on the secondary feathers done in the only colored pencil she owned — a stub of red from the kitchen drawer. She had looked at the drawing a dozen times since October and each time she had decided it was close enough. The bird was recognizable. The color was slightly off.

He stood with the notebook open to that entry for long enough that she counted the seconds without meaning to.

He said: "October."

He said it looking at the page, quietly, not quite to her.

"October," she said.

He looked up. She didn't have the German or the English for what she saw in his face — she had the shape of it, the shape without the name.

He looked at the entry once more. He closed the notebook and handed it back.

---

She put it in her pocket and thought of something to give him.

"Hawk," she said. She held her arms out in the flat spread and slight angle she had worked out over the summer — the motion for soaring birds, good enough to be understood if not quite accurate.

He watched her hands. He said: *Habicht.*

She repeated it. He shook his head — not wrong, but not the right bird. He made the gesture back at her with a different angle at the wrist: the motion of a bird that came in direct and low rather than wheeling overhead. That hawk. Then he made another gesture, smaller, quicker — the rapid flutter above the fence line, the hovering she knew.

*Sperber,* he said.

She knew this one. She had been watching it all summer over the south fence — the sparrow hawk, the small one that held its position above the grass before it dropped. She had had the English and not the German until now.

She wrote both in the notebook, standing there: *Habicht / Goshawk. Sperber / Sparrow Hawk.* She would draw them both later. She would probably get one wrong.

The dipper dripped onto the dry grass.

*Kommen,* he said. Then in English: "Come."

She said it back: *kommen.* He said "come." They had been adding verbs to the nouns all summer, one piece at a time — *kommen* and "come," *bleiben* and "stay," the words needed for what the day required. By her count they had perhaps eighty words between them now. A grammar of necessity, from what the farm asked. It was not a language. It had the bones of one: birds and tools and the weather words, the farm-labor nouns, and now some of the basic verbs in two forms, the direction and the tense.

She said: *Morgen.* Tomorrow.

He said: "Tomorrow."

She said: "Morning."

He considered. Then he held up one finger: *Morgen,* pointing forward. And then *Morgen* again, spreading his hands to indicate the pale light, the hour before the baler started. The word going two directions at once. She had known this but had not thought about it as something he would be carrying — the German word that meant both at the same time, tomorrow and morning.

She nodded. He looked at the field.

The baler came around again and the noise filled everything.

---

The last load came off the field at half past four.

The light had gone from white to long gold and the heat had settled into something different — still full but changed, lying quiet and dense on the cut stubble. The wagon came in with the final bales piled high, swaying slightly on the ruts of the farm lane, and her father steered the tractor through the gate and into the far bay of the barn. The four prisoners off-loaded in twenty minutes: the steady rhythm of lifted bales and stacked rows, bodies that had been at this all afternoon and knew the count without being told.

Her father unhooked the baler and drove the tractor around to the equipment shed.

Lonzo came to the barn door. He spoke to Dieter. Dieter and Rolf and Hans filed out the south gate and headed toward the icehouse, Lonzo following. At the fence corner he stopped and took his cap off and fanned his face with it, put it back on, stood with his arms folded, looking out at the cut stubble field.

Twenty yards. A little more.

Hannah had not left the far bay.

Karl was still there, stacking the last bales from the second wagon against the back wall. She worked from the other side — four bales wide, two high, the satisfying thunk of each bale placed. The barn smelled the way it always smelled after a hayload: the sweet dry smell of this summer's cut grass over the older smell of every summer the barn had been receiving hay, the accumulated smell of all those Augusts stacked against the back wall and the one before that and the one before that. She had been breathing this smell her whole life. She could not have said what it was called.

She thought of nothing in particular. She worked.

---

He set the last bale in the rack.

He stood with both hands resting on the top bale, not moving. She had stopped too, and they were at opposite ends of the finished work.

He was looking at the fence. Not at Lonzo — past him, at the post where the top rail ran into the corner brace. She had watched Karl Reinmar through ten months of fieldwork and water rounds and the pidgin they had built between them, and she had learned his different kinds of looking. The looking that was rest. The looking that was waiting for the next instruction. The looking that was the field and nothing in it.

This one she did not have a name for.

She waited.

"Werner," he said.

Quietly, looking at the post. Not to her. The name released in the way he released things sometimes — not offered, not explained, put into the air and left there.

A pause long enough that she thought he had finished.

"Von Ansbach."

---

*W. Grau. A. Pfeiffer. Bamberg, 1921.*

She had looked at that photograph perhaps twenty times since April. The young man standing in outdoor light — twenties probably, city clothes, a street behind him she didn't know — standing beside her father who was not yet her father, who was twenty or twenty-five in some other country, whose face she could find in her father's face only if she looked for the bones of it. *W. Grau.* She had been holding the initial for four months. A door with no handle.

Werner.

From Ansbach. Near Bamberg. The same low Bavarian hills, the same fifty kilometers that was not far in that country — not far if you grew up in it, not far if you had grown up knowing the roads between those towns. She had measured it on the classroom map in April, after she found the photograph. She had used her thumb and converted.

Werner Grau. From near where her father was born.

---

*Er wollte kommen,* Karl said.

He wanted to come.

Not: he wants. Not: he is coming. He wanted: past. The tense of a thing that was already finished before it arrived at where it was going.

She understood the past tense. She had understood it in October at the dinner table — her father's fork stopped between plate and mouth, the brief stillness, the resumed eating that was not the same eating as before. She had understood it again in April, the photograph face-up on the oilcloth, his hands going still again for the same reason.

Karl had not turned from the fence.

She said: *Du weißt.*

Both hands on the top bale.

Karl had not turned from the fence.

He turned. He looked at her across the stacked hay, the afternoon light crossing the barn floor in angled stripes. Then he nodded, once, and turned back to the fence.

---

A meadowlark landed on the post where Karl had been looking.

It stood for a moment — yellow-chested, upright, indifferent to the barn and to them and to the whole August evening. Neither of them said its name.

It lifted and was gone into the cut field and the long light.

---

She picked up the bucket from beside the wagon wheel.

She walked out of the barn and across the yard. The sun was at the west hillside now, the long shadows running flat across the stubble field. The south field was pale and open in the low light — the hay off, the cut stubble clean and even, the way a field looks in the first hours after: not yet itself.

The notebook was in her apron pocket. She felt the weight of it — the soft-covered composition notebook, its twenty-three entries, first October to now. Light, and specific.

She was thinking of her father's hands stopped above the plate. She was thinking of the name coming from two directions — an initial from a tin box, a given name from a fence post in an August barn — and landing in the same place.

She went up the back step and into the kitchen. She did not look back at the barn.
