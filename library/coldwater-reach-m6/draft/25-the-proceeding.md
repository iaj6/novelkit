# The Proceeding

She spread the harbor logs across the desk in order: 1935 at the left, then 1936 through the current year running right in a line, the current year's volume at the far end. Eight volumes plus the one she was still adding to. She had brought them down from the shelf above the stovepipe in two loads, the stack heavier than she usually moved them because she did not usually move more than one or two at a time. The stove was unlit; cold kept her sharp, and she had a long evening's work ahead.

The formal notice had come on Monday by the county carrier, addressed to *Acting Harbormaster, Coldwater Reach Harbor* and forwarded from the district port authority office. The language was the careful language of procedure: *notice of formal proceeding to review appointment status*, not *challenge to tenure*; *right to present the record of service before the board*, not *obligation to appear*. At the close, in the sentence that mattered most: *at the board's discretion to determine the appropriate status of the position going forward.* She had read it three times on Monday and once more on Tuesday. She had the letter in her coat pocket now, folded on its original fold, the county seal at the top left in blue ink and the county clerk's signature below it and the date: November twenty-second. Two weeks.

The record was about safe harbor management and vessel registration and fuel allocation; the proceeding was about a post, and who was understood to occupy it, and whether the occupation would be made formal. Someone at the county level had decided the occupation should change. The procedural vehicle available to them was the appointment status — she had been acting harbormaster, technically, since 1935, because the formal confirmation hearing that should have followed within eighteen months of her taking the post had never been scheduled. She had not pursued the scheduling. The harbor had not cared about her status; the work had required doing. The county board had not cared, evidently, until someone gave them a reason to care.

None of this would be said at the proceeding. The proceeding would concern itself with the record.

She opened the 1935 volume.

---

Owen's handwriting ran to November seventh.

The November sixth entry was the last full one — weather at the head in the abbreviated form, fleet positions in order, any maintenance items requiring follow-up at the bottom. He had written: partly cloudy, wind northwest twelve and building through the afternoon, barometer falling slowly. The fleet was in except for the *Selma Hatch* at the outer mooring, which he had noted was exposed with the weather developing and which he had recommended be brought to the inner before nightfall. There was a note about the bait pier fuel line, which had been difficult all October: *Will call Trowbridge in the morning. Can't have another week of this.* Three inches of handwriting. She knew its particulars — the *d* formed with the loop left open, the figure sevens cut flat, the word *barometer* abbreviated to *bar* and the word *northwest* to *NW* with the capital letter slightly larger than convention — in the way she knew any handwriting she had read beside its author for twenty-three years.

The November seventh entry was a half-line: *Storm developing. Fleet in order. Outer mooring advised clear.* Below it the date; below that, nothing.

The eighth had nothing.

Her first entry was November ninth, 1935.

She read it now for the documentation. The handwriting was not his: her loop closed, her sevens rounded, her abbreviations formed differently, her hand on the word *northwest* a shade lighter than his. The same column headings. The same abbreviated form. She had written the weather at the head — northwest fifteen moderating, sea state three at the outer head — and then the fleet positions, and then the bait pier fuel line: *Trowbridge sent a man Friday. Fitting replaced, line in operation.* Three paragraphs of ordinary harbor work, written on the first morning she had held the post. She did not remember writing them. The content was correct and the handwriting was hers.

She would bring this volume to the proceeding.

The point was not the handwriting. The point was the date. November seventh in his hand, then the gap of the eighth, then November ninth in hers — the same form, the same columns, the same attention given to the same harbor. The work had not stopped. The log showed it without requiring anyone to say so. She was not asking the board to read anything into the gap between his last entry and her first. She was asking them to see that the entries resumed, and in what condition the harbor was in when they did, and what the record showed for the eight years that followed.

She set the 1935 volume to the side and moved to 1936.

---

She went through the volumes in order.

Mooring registry first, in the systematic form she had introduced in 1937: vessel by vessel, seasonal assignment, transfer record. Forty-two registered vessels in 1936; sixty-one at present, with the additional traffic from the Navy work and the sardine carriers' increased runs to Eastport. The *Maud Eleanor*, which had been Owen's benchmark vessel for weather judgment, now running sardines twice a week in season. Every transfer in the record, every assignment accounted for, every reassignment documented. The registry and the revenue ledger matched in every quarter because she checked them against each other quarterly and had done so since 1937. This was not required. She had done it because the record was either correct or it was not.

Harbor income: the comparison figures against Owen's last two full years came out eleven percent above. She had run the calculation in 1940 as part of the annual accounting and had noted the result in the supplemental ledger. She had not done it for the proceeding. She clipped the supplemental to the registry stack and set them on the left side of the desk.

The fuel ledgers she went through page by page: five columns each, every barrel from 1936 through the current volume. The supplemental allocation from the county and the USCG restrictions were in the margin for 1942 and 1943, when the allocations had dropped twice in four months. She had managed the harbor's fuel through both drops without interrupting the week's ordinary work. One error in the fuel ledgers across eight years: a posting error in October 1942, the sardine carrier's allocation entered in the wrong column in a month when she had been doing the accounting alone. Found in the same month's reconciliation. Corrected before the quarter closed. She had the correction note and the letter from the fuel authority acknowledging the correction. One error in eight years, found and corrected within the reporting period.

She had been doing the accounting alone because the assistant had been drafted in January 1942, and the county had not authorized a replacement. She had not put this in the annual reports as a complaint. She had put the assistant's line marked *vacant* and continued to do the work.

Safety inspection files: twenty-seven inspections since 1942 under the new USCG standards. One failure. The *Cecelia R.* in October of that year — port running light out, cracked exhaust elbow on the main — brought into compliance within eleven days of the written notice. She had reinspected on the twelfth day and issued the certification. The notice, the repair documentation the owner submitted, the reinspection form, and the certification were all in the file in the order the file required.

No casualty attributable to her decisions in eight years.

She put this in the summary document as the absence of an entry — the casualty log for that column was empty, and she would bring the casualty log. She had run the harbor through the worst season of 1942 with half her normal resources and no assistant and the Coast Guard treating the waterfront as their own facility whenever they wanted it, and the fleet had come in whole every November. She did not expect the board to weigh this heavily. It was in the record regardless.

---

The summary document was twelve pages of foolscap, running through the registry, the revenue comparison, the fuel record, the safety inspection record, and the casualty log. She needed three more pages to complete it: the comparative figures from Rockport and Searsport, which the county assessor had and which she had requested on Tuesday, and a final summary page. She would have the comparison numbers by Friday and the document complete before the weekend.

What the document could do was establish the record. What it could not do was address the question the proceeding was actually asking — whether the county board was prepared to confirm the post to the person who held it or to reassign it to someone they had in mind. The record was her answer to the procedural challenge. Whether the procedural answer was sufficient to meet the political one was not a calculation she could run tonight.

---

She opened the middle drawer.

The Portland letter was on top.

She had placed it there in September: the Harbor Authority's reply to her registration inquiry on the stove-in dinghy, four sentences and a name. Below it the CG form, the blank space for identifying information on the body received at Coldwater Reach on October 13, 1943, still empty as it had been since she drew the form from the cabinet in October of last year.

She took both papers out.

*W. Grau, 114 Franklin Street, Portland, Maine.* The letter gave the rental record from the Commercial Street chandlery: dinghy last rented September twenty-eighth, 1943, renter's name and address as noted. She had read this in September, twice, and had not sent it to the Coast Guard because she could not use it. A rental record placed a named individual in possession of a specific dinghy on the twenty-eighth of September. By the middle of October that dinghy had been in the south cove long enough to show ten months of growth at the waterline. There was the body. There was the rental record. There was the name. She could draw the inference; she could not certify it, and the CG form's blank required what could be certified. The blank was therefore still blank.

She set the two papers on the desk corner, away from the harbor log stacks.

Not related to the proceeding. The proceeding was about the appointment status; the letter and the form were about the body on the breakwater, which was a different authority and a different column. A thing waiting for its proper entry. A thing she could not close because she did not yet have what the closing required. The CG form was blank because she had not been willing to write an entry she could not verify. The proceeding existed because someone at the county level had determined that a tenure of eight years required formal confirmation before it could be made formal.

She put the Portland letter and the CG form back in the drawer. The county-header forms were behind them. She took two and closed the drawer.

---

She copied the county reference number from the formal notice onto the first header sheet and set it beside the summary document. She would transcribe the summary onto the headed sheets when the draft was finished. The draft was not finished.

The oil lamp had burned low while she worked — the reservoir nearly to nothing, the flame going to nothing with it. She set the pen in the groove of the inkstand and went to the cabinet by the door for the oil tin. She had a little less than half a tin. She lifted the glass chimney carefully, filled the reservoir to the proper level, replaced the chimney, and adjusted the wick until the flame came back to its correct height: even, steady, clear.

She stood at the window before returning to the desk.

The harbor in November. The water between the pier lights was black; the inner mooring field held the laid-up shapes of most of the season's fleet, the hulls low and still in the flat water. The south headland was a dark line against a sky slightly less dark. She could hear the bell buoy at the channel entrance — faint from here, at the shed's distance from the outer pier, the slow quiet sound of the ordinary tide. No weather behind it. The outer anchorage was empty. Everything in its place.

She had been coming to this shed for nine years and had known this harbor for longer than that, and she knew what it required of whoever ran it, and she had given it that, and the record of it was on her desk.

In two weeks she would stand before the county board and present the record.

She went back to the desk. She spread the summary document in front of her and picked up her pen.

She was not done.
