# Chapter Fifteen — What Grandy Soule Saw

The registration table went up at the pier head the last week of April the way it had gone up every spring Eira could remember: the clipboard, the fee box, the carbon forms weighted at the left with the big shackle she kept for this purpose, because the wind off the harbor had no patience for paperwork in April.

She had the table set before seven. The harbor lay in front of her the way it lay every spring — the light longer now, working into the east faces of the pilings and under the boathouse eaves where the winter sun hadn't reached, the water still cold enough to raise a thin steam at the harbor entrance in the early morning air. The mud at the basin head had softened. Dark fronds of weed were coming off the south rocks. A pair of eiders worked the current beside the fish house float and did not look up.

The first boat in was the *Bertha L.* at half past, Pete Alley at the wheel with his boy in the stern. She had the hull in the card file and wrote it up while he tied off: tonnage, engine, mooring assignment. He'd put fresh paint on her over the winter and the hull had that new brightness to it that wouldn't last but was good while it lasted. She collected the fee and the signature and filed the carbon.

By noon she had seven: *Bertha L.*, *Perseverance*, *Sea Star*, *Laura K.*, *Edna C.*, and two others whose documentation was in order and whose owners had nothing more to say to her than the minimum. She wrote each entry in the column and checked it against the card file and wrote the total at the bottom of the page.

Seven. Last spring there had been nine.

*Crestview* was still in the cradle at the railway yard. Harold Oakes had gone into the Navy in January of 1942 and the boat had been on the railway since that June. She had sent two letters about the cradle fee; neither had been answered, which was not Oakes's fault. The mooring pen sat empty. *Walter D.* had been sold south to Tenants Harbor in February when the Withams made the decision they had been putting off since autumn — Lyle had taken sick and there was no one left to take the boat over. She had given that mooring pen to the *Laura K.* when Ed Croft's hip made the row from the inner float a problem for him.

Two fewer boats. The column said so.

*Sea Star* was Ray Coffin's — Fred Coffin's younger boy, who had come home from the Pacific in November with two fingers gone from the right hand and had been on the water again by April, which was not a surprise to anyone who knew the family. He signed the registration form left-handed. He wanted the mooring lines inspected before he started hauling and she made a note.

Burton Talbot on the *Edna C.* had a new inboard he was willing to explain at length. She let him explain it, asked for the documentation twice, received it the second time. New tonnage in the column. She signed the carbon.

The rest of the afternoon went the way it went: boat by boat, fee by fee, the spring talk running alongside the transactions the way it always did. She listened and wrote and handed over receipts.

By two o'clock the registration table was packed and she was at the fuel dock going over the morning log when the *Maud L.* came in.

---

Grandy Soule brought her alongside the fuel dock without ceremony — no wasted motion, the boat coming against the fender the way something comes to rest in the hands of a man who has set it down in the same place for more years than he has counted. The *Maud L.* was a thirty-two-foot lobster boat with a dull red hull that had been brighter once; Soule had bought her in 1928 and she had been working this harbor since. He was perhaps seventy, perhaps past it. His age was a harbor fact she had stopped trying to fix, the same way you stop sounding every ledge you already know is there.

He paid for his fuel in exact change from his coat pocket, signed the carbon, and stood at the dock while the tank filled. The harbor went on around them: the sound of the pump, the water working the pilings, a dragger coming in from the outer harbor and taking its mooring. Soule had his eyes on the south end of the inner pier — the double-planked float at the fish house end, the one that had needed new decking since before the war and was not going to get it this spring either.

She was finishing the log entry for the *Laura K.*

He said nothing for a while and she did not expect him to.

Then, without turning: "Saw a strange dinghy at the south pier. That October night. Before your blow."

She looked up. She kept her face. "Which pier," she said.

"South end. That last float." He moved his chin toward it. "Late. After midnight. I was driving back from Owls Head."

"What night."

"Night the weather came on, or near enough. I've thought about it." He watched the harbor. "Numbers on the transom weren't Coldwater. I didn't know them."

"Could you read them?"

"Not properly. Too dark, and I didn't stop. Might have been Portland — I couldn't say for certain. Small boat, wooden tender, not large." A pause. "One man come off her. He walked up toward the main pier."

"Did you see him come back to the dinghy?"

"No." He held the mooring line, not working it yet. "I drove on. Figured whoever it was had put in because the weather was turning — thought nothing of it." He looked at the float. "Dinghy was gone when the blow cleared. I don't know when it went. Could have gone in the storm."

She looked at the fuel gauge. Her pen was in her hand. She had written nothing since he started speaking.

"One man," she said. "That's all you saw."

"One." He began working the mooring line loose. "I heard you were still asking about that October business. Thought maybe you'd want to know."

"I would," she said. "Thank you, Grandy."

He untied the *Maud L.* and backed her out with the same economy he'd brought her in with, turned toward the harbor mouth, and was gone past the outer breakwater. The wake spread into the basin and went flat. She stood at the dock end and watched the boat go.

---

She laid what she had against what she had been carrying, the way she would lay a vessel's log against the tide tables: columns together, the matches showing you what you could confirm, the gaps showing you what you hadn't accounted for yet.

Her column: two figures on the south dock, same night, past midnight. One coming from the direction of the main pier — heavyset, town coat, moving from some distance. A second figure near the fish house end, further back, that she had taken at the time for a dock worker.

The columns met at the south pier. The man who came off the dinghy and the man she had seen from her window were the same man, or she had to explain why they weren't, and she could not.

The second figure was not in Soule's column. One man, he had said. She had seen two. Which meant the second figure had come on foot, not by water — had come separately, or had come after Soule drove on.

Two days later, with the storm in between, the body was at the breakwater elbow.

Not proof. But it had a shape.

She picked up her clipboard and walked back to the shed.

---

The harbor log was on its stand. The tide tables were pinned to the board behind the door. She hung her coat and sat at the desk. The window gave onto the inner basin — three boats still at their moorings, the outer harbor going pewter in the late-afternoon light.

She opened the middle drawer.

The letter was between the duplicate mooring assignments and the east anchorage survey notes, where she had put it in November. Coast Guard, Sprucehead station. Dated November 14, 1943. Lt. A. Mercer's signature at the bottom, the same neat hand from the October paperwork. *Should any information come to light bearing on the identity of the deceased or the circumstances of his presence in Coldwater Reach, the station would be grateful for its communication at the address above.*

She had read that sentence a dozen times since November. It said what it said.

Below it: male, fifty or older, no identifying papers, recovered at the breakwater elbow, morning of October 12, 1943. Navy wool coat with lapels, wool trousers. Probable cardiac event — no water in the lungs. No next of kin identified, no missing persons report in any register that matched. Case status: open.

She set the letter on the desk.

What the letter did not have: a dinghy at the south pier, possibly Portland registration, the night the storm came on. One man off her, walking toward the main waterfront. That was not her private information. Soule had told her at the fuel dock this afternoon in plain daylight and would confirm it if asked.

What else: two figures on the south dock, past midnight, October 10 going into the eleventh. One from the direction of the main pier — heavyset, town coat. One near the fish house end, further back, a shape in the dark she had taken for a dock worker. Her own observation, from the shed window, two hundred feet of dark harbor between the glass and the south float. She had been watching the barometric trend. She had seen what she had seen.

She had held the reason for not writing it. The reason was sound: what she had seen from the window was two shapes in the dark at a distance through glass, and two shapes in the dark at a distance through glass was not what the harbor log was for. The column had stayed blank.

Eight years in this post and she had kept the log accurate. Every entry had something under it: a document, a witness, a measurement, a recorded time.

What Soule had given her today changed the calculation. Not because he had seen the same things she had — he hadn't stayed long enough for that. Because he had put a transport under the man she had seen.

The envelope was in the right-hand drawer. She had put it there in January when the winter had seemed like enough reason to wait.

She reached for her pen.
