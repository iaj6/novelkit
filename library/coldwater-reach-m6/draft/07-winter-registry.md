# Winter Registry

The mooring roster ran to five pages for December. She laid them flat on the desk and went through them against the harbor log, one column against the other. The Ellsworth was accounted for — still on her winter run to Eastport, due back the sixteenth. Clara M. was accounted for, inner float, registration current. The Gale brothers' second boat, Ida B., was on the boatyard railway since the third of November and her entry had been updated accordingly. Ida B.'s mooring assignment would hold through the winter vacant, which was correct.

Seventeen hulls would winter in the inner harbor. She went through them one at a time.

Two of the seventeen were out of compliance on their documentation. One had let the registration lapse in October and had not responded to the September reminder; the other had moved berths without filing the change. Both were correctable by letter. She pulled paper from the center drawer and wrote both notices before she did anything else. Clean copies, dated, registered mail on the second one. She addressed the envelopes and put them in the to-mail pile at the corner of the desk.

Outside the shed window the harbor was in its December arrangement. Half the working fleet was hauled, cradles and sawhorses up in the boatyard above the high-water line, the boats sitting on their keels with a stripped and workmanlike look that was different from summer. The railway had been busy through November — she had heard it most mornings, the hauling gear running, and had noted the vessels as they came out of the water and been checked off the winter list. The cold-storage house was running on reduced hours now; Patterson had posted the December schedule on the shed door last Thursday. The inner float held what it held: Clara M., the Hartleys' double-ender, three lobster boats whose owners had calculated the winter lay-up costs and decided against it.

She went back to the roster. The outer anchorage was empty. Had been since the first week of November.

She was going through the last page when she heard boots on the fuel dock.

---

Wendell Foss ran his boat up alongside and tied the stern first. He came up the dock planks with the gas can and did not look in through the shed window or call out, because he was not a man who required an audience before he arrived. She came out to the dock.

He set the can down and reached for the pump handle.

"Heard they're putting someone from the county board to look at the harbormaster situation," he said.

He worked the pump. He had not looked at her when he said it and he did not look at her now.

"Who told you that," she said.

"Clement Ames. His cousin works for the county in some capacity." He watched the can fill. "Don't know the specifics. Just that it's under discussion."

"I'll note it."

He shut off the pump and lifted the can. He paid and went back down the dock at the same pace he'd come up. She watched him reach his boat and step aboard without looking back.

She went inside and wrote the fuel transaction in the ledger.

Then she set down the pen.

Eight years of harbor log kept to the same standard. Every vessel registration current within her authority. The mooring assignments documented and filed, the incident log accurate, the annual fuel throughput reconciled quarter by quarter. No casualty attributed to a failure in harbor management in her tenure, and no casualty that a different harbormaster's decision could have prevented either — she had run that accounting once, after the Hartley boy's accident on the outer mooring, and the conclusion was the same from every direction. The harbor income was up eleven percent over Owen's last two years. That was not a dramatic figure. It was the correct direction.

She went back to the roster.

---

The annual report for the county authority was a form she had filed every December since 1936. The columns did not change year to year: vessel count, fuel throughput, mooring revenue, incident log, capital expenditures, maintenance deferred and completed. She filled them in the same order she always did. The numbers went where they belonged.

But when she reached the incident log section she went back over it before committing the ink.

The October 12 entry was there. She had written it exactly as it stood in the harbor log: body discovered on breakwater, 0615, CG notified 0622, arrived 0711, removed 0723. Case closed by Coast Guard, October 1943. Official determination: unidentified drowning. She had copied this to the annual report with the same words.

It was accurate. The case was closed. There was nothing in the incident log that was not in the harbor log, and nothing in the harbor log that was not accurate.

She read it through a second time and left it as it was.

She sealed the envelope, stamped it, and put it on top of the mooring notices.

---

The post office was quiet at four o'clock. Two items in the box — county boilerplate and a state winter storm protocol notice she would implement before February. She added both to her coat pocket. The Western Union boy's bicycle was not against the wall. Available information, and also a form of relief.

She went out into the cold.

---

The light was almost gone when she got back to the shed. She logged the state notice and checked the to-mail pile one more time.

Then she put on her heavier coat and her cap and went out for the breakwater.

---

She walked the breakwater in November and December every year. In Owen's time it had been his walk; she had gone with him the first winters of their marriage, learning the habit before she learned what it was for. Now she did it the way she did the other maintenance work: from the shore end out, systematically, with the paper and pencil in her coat pocket for notes.

The granite was in its December state — damp on the upper face from the last tide, the cap stones dark. She walked the crown and checked the placement as she went, stopping where she needed to stop. Third cap stone from the shore end had a gap on the south face, probably from the October storm. The stone was seated; it wasn't going anywhere. She put it in the notes: spring assessment, south face, stone three. Tight gap, not urgent.

The chop off the outer face was four feet from the southeast, well short of the crown. The tide was running at mid-flood. She walked further, noting two more small settlements in the rubble face — both minor, both below the mean high-water line where the ice action would work on them through February and March. These went in the notes as well.

The boatyard was visible across the inner harbor, the railway track running up from the waterline, one hull still on the cradle. She thought it was Merton Galey's Penelope D. but the light was too flat now to be certain.

She walked to the elbow.

---

The elbow was where the breakwater turned. She came around it and stopped to check the inner face granite course. A fractional movement in one of the lower courses, probably the October storm as well — the capstone wasn't proud but the rubble below it had settled a small amount. She wrote it down. Spring assessment, elbow, inner face, course four. Not critical.

She straightened up.

The inner harbor was ahead of her — float lights on, the cold-storage office lamp still burning across the water. The south dock was visible from this angle, the pilings and the dock planking at the line of the harbor's south edge. Just visible. She looked at it the way she always looked at it from the elbow, without having decided to.

The night of October eleventh going into the twelfth. Past midnight. She had been at the shed's south window watching the barometer; the glass had been falling steadily since nine o'clock and she had wanted to see what it did at midnight. That was why she had been there.

Two men on the south dock.

The first: past middle age, solid in the shoulders, dark coat. Not a fisherman. She was certain of that from where she stood — certain from the coat and the way he stood on the dock planking, too deliberate, placed rather than easy, the way a man stands on a dock when he is not used to docks. He was looking toward the outer harbor. She could not see his face; the dock lamp was behind him. He turned and said something to the other man.

The second figure: wider across the back and through the shoulders. Standing further from the dock edge. His weight planted in a way she recognized — not a sailor's balance, a farmer's stillness, the kind that comes from years of standing in mud and cold and not moving much while you wait for something to finish. She had not seen his face.

The first man had walked to the shore end of the dock and gone up the ramp. The boatyard shadow had taken him. The second man had stayed another minute or two and then gone up the ramp the same way.

That was what she had. An image and a time: approximately half past midnight, October 12. The south dock, which the Gale brothers had vacated in September, not in regular use. Two men on it, neither one a sailor, in the hours before the storm peaked. And at the other end of three days of blow, a man with no papers in town clothes face-down at the elbow of the breakwater.

What she did not have: a vessel. A name. A corroboration. Anything that would make an entry in the harbor log rather than a statement that she had watched two men standing on a dock. The harbor log required specificity sufficient to establish the fact. It was sufficient to establish that she had seen something, which was a different entry.

She turned and walked back along the breakwater toward the shore.

---

The tide had come up while she walked. She stopped at the south ladder — the iron rungs set into the breakwater's shore face — and read the tide height off the granite marks. The water was at the first mark above mid, consistent with tonight's table. She had resurveyed the marks in October of 1940 and confirmed them against the Rockland gauge. They were accurate. They were what they said they were.

She went up the shore path to the shed.

Inside, she lit the lamp. The harbor was dark except for the float lights and the cold-storage lamp across the water. She stood at the south window for a moment. The south dock was barely visible, a line of pilings at the far edge of the float light's reach.

She had not written the dock figures. She had accepted this in October, when the image was new. She had accepted it again when Mercer came and she gave him technically-true answers that left the dock out of them. She accepted it now: two men on the dock, a time, a body at the elbow.

For now, she had told herself in October.

For now.

She turned from the window. The desk had the Cote notice on it still, and the state harbor file, and the December mooring roster with the last page not yet filed. She sat down and pulled the roster toward her and finished it. One column, then the next. The harbor in December holding what it held — seventeen hulls, the empty outer anchorage, the water doing its work at the granite marks.

The entry was wrong and would stay wrong for now.
