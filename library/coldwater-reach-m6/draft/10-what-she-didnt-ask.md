# What She Didn't Ask

The homework was not going anywhere. Hannah had the German notebook open to the conditional letter — *Ich hoffe, wir werden uns bald sehen* — and the margin question she'd made on Thursday and meant to ask Marcus on Friday, except she had not asked on Friday, and now it was the following week and the notebook was open on the kitchen table and she was looking at the question mark instead of the sentence when she looked up.

A figure on the farm road. Far end, near the cedar fence posts, walking toward the barn.

She knew it in a moment, the way you know a person's approach before you have seen their face — the particular walk, the uprightness of it, the coat that was not a farm coat. Her German teacher walked everywhere; she knew this from school, from the post office line in November, from the way the town spoke about him with a residual mild puzzlement at a man who did not own a car. He walked, and this was him walking, three miles up the Pfeiffer farm road in January.

She stayed at the table. Her mother was in the window chair — the other window, south-facing, toward the crabapple and the stone wall where the cedar waxwings came — and had not looked up. Her mother rarely looked up at anything from the road. She sat with her cup and the gray outside and whatever she was watching that was not the yard.

Hannah watched.

---

Her father came out of the barn as Marcus reached the farmyard. She couldn't hear what passed — the kitchen was closed up against the cold, storm windows on, and even in summer she would have needed the door open to catch more than a sentence from across the yard. What she had was the window, and the window gave her: her father straightening from whatever he had been doing at the barn door, turning; Marcus stopping at a distance that was polite for outdoors, the way you stopped when you did not know a place well; her father's face, turned just enough toward the house that she could see the left side of it.

Marcus was speaking. She could see the small cloud of breath, and she knew from the slow regular pace of it that it was not a long speech, not an emergency. He reached into his coat.

Her father looked at what Marcus had produced. From the size and the flatness of what passed between them she thought: an envelope. Her father took it without looking at Marcus's face, the way you look at the thing itself when the thing itself is what you have been waiting for.

He opened it. He read.

She watched him read from the kitchen window, and what she saw was this: his head still, his shoulders still, his eyes moving down the page with the careful unhurried pace of a man who already knows the shape of what he is reading and is confirming the details rather than taking in the substance. She had never seen her father read a letter before. She did not know she had thought about how he read until she saw him doing it and found she had expected something different — expected his face to open the way her own did with a letter, taking in each line as if the line were a gift or a small surprise. His face did not open. It was still in the way of a room with the door closed, and the stillness was not blankness.

She had seen this stillness in him before. It was the stillness from the first week of October — his hands stopping on the knife and fork when she had said *a man nobody knows, no papers* — and then resuming, and him eating, and saying nothing, and her filing it without knowing what column it went in. She had carried that image for three months without a place to put it. Here it was again, the same stillness, and she still did not have a column.

Her father folded the paper. He said something to Marcus. One sentence, she thought; brief enough that she could watch Marcus take it in without his face changing. Marcus said something back. Her father nodded once, the slow confirming nod he used when something was settled. Marcus turned and walked back down the farm road without lingering.

Her father stood at the barn door for a long moment with the folded paper in his hands. He looked at it once more — not reading, just looking at the thing — and then went inside. Not the barn. The house.

Hannah looked down at her notebook. *Ich hoffe.* The question mark in the margin was still there.

---

He came through the kitchen door without looking up from what he was doing, which was putting the folded paper into the inside pocket of his coat — the deeper pocket, the one he used for things he meant to keep. He went to the basin and washed his hands in the way he always washed his hands after barn work: deliberate, thorough, the same number of strokes she had watched since she could remember watching anything. He dried on the towel behind the basin and turned toward the table and then he saw her.

His face settled. It went from wherever it had been into the face he used for looking at her — not cold, not unkind, but arranged.

She stood and put the kettle on. She cut bread. Her hands did them without instruction while her attention was still partly at the window.

He sat. She put the bread and the coffee cup before him. He put both hands around the cup without pouring.

She sat across from him. Through the south window she could see the edge of her mother's chair — Magda's shoulder, the dark cardigan, the angle of her head toward the crabapple. Whether her mother was watching the birds or resting her eyes on nothing was a thing Hannah had stopped trying to determine. The silence in this kitchen had two shapes. Her father's was the kind with something arranged behind it, pressed against an interior wall. Her mother's had let go of whatever it had once been pressing against. Right now the kitchen had both.

Her father's hands were around the cup.

The folded paper was in his coat pocket.

There were things she could ask. She sorted through them while she sat with her German notebook still open in front of her, the conditional letter she had been translating before the figure appeared on the road. She could ask who that man was on the farm road — she knew who it was, her German teacher, Dr. Klein, three miles in January on foot, which was not an accident of proximity and which meant something she didn't know. She could ask what he had brought, what was in the envelope. She could ask whether her father had known anything, since October, about the man on the breakwater, the man in town clothes with no papers that the school and the diner and the post office had turned into a drunk sailor with a debt and a wife, which she had never believed, because of the hands stopping on the fork.

These questions were all available to her. She held them and did not ask them. She had been doing it for months with her mother's silence and her father's German and Karl in the farmyard and the lamp in the icehouse at night; she was practiced at it.

She asked: had the herd count been off this week, or had she miscounted on Tuesday. She had thought the number came up short by one but she might have gone too fast through the east stalls.

Her father looked at her. He was still for a moment. Then:

"No," he said. "The count is right. You miscounted."

She nodded. She had thought so.

She was beginning to frame the next thing — something about the manure spreader and the axle, which she had noticed him working on — when she stopped. Because he had answered her in English. He had said *no* and *the count is right* and *you miscounted* in his careful chosen English, the English she had not heard from him at the kitchen table since sometime in the spring, the English of a man who selected a language rather than fell into it. She had been waiting for that English for months without knowing she was waiting for it, and now it had arrived to answer a question about cows and she was so surprised she did not say anything at all.

He poured his coffee. He looked at the window.

She did not ask about the manure spreader. She did not ask anything else.

---

After supper she washed the dishes while her father sat in his chair by the lamp with the farm accounts ledger open on his knee. Magda had gone to bed early. The kitchen was quiet — the stove settling, the January wind off the bay in the eaves, a higher more even register than the October wind, sustained and without the gusts.

Hannah washed each bowl and cup and set it in the rack. The dish water was hot; her hands went pink in it. Outside the window the farmyard was dark — the icehouse, the shapes of the burdock stalks in the field beyond, the black line of the cedar fence posts along the road. No lamp in the icehouse window. The four Germans would be asleep by now or quiet, their voices settling for the night the way the barn settled after the evening feeding.

Her father's coat was on the hook by the door. The folded paper was in it; she knew it was there because she had watched him put it there and had watched him at supper make no move to take it out again, not once through the meal, just sitting with it in the coat on the hook behind him as though the coat were an ordinary coat and the paper were an ordinary paper and neither required any particular attention or explanation.

It was six feet from her hands to the hook.

She did not cross the six feet. She had thought about it once, briefly, when she was setting the table and her father was still in the barn for the evening check — the thought arrived clean and factual: *you could look at it.* She had thought: yes. And then she had set the plates and that had been that.

When the dishes were done she dried her hands, said good night, took the lamp upstairs.

---

The bedroom was cold the way it was always cold in January until the lamp had been burning for a while. She got into bed with her socks on and pulled the quilt up and lay on her back and looked at the ceiling.

She thought about Dr. Klein walking three miles in January, declining whatever easier arrangement might have existed, because the thing in the envelope required a person and not the mail. He was not a man who made gestures without considering what the gesture cost.

She thought about her father's English. *The count is right. You miscounted.* Both sentences precise and plain, the careful careful English that belonged to the occasions he had decided required exactness. She tried to count forward from the last time she had heard it at this table. She could not reach the beginning. The months since spring had accumulated into one long German winter without landmarks, and the English she had stopped expecting had arrived today to answer a question about cows.

Why that question. Why today.

There was something in it she could almost reach, a shape she could see the edge of without being able to see the whole. As if the paper in his coat had loosened something that had been held very still for a long time, and the only thing that had leaked through was one sentence in the country's language — not an explanation, not a name, not anything she could fit against the things she did not know. Just *you miscounted*, in English, and the cup held between his hands without being poured.

She had the questions. She just did not know how to ask them of a man who had closed himself so thoroughly for so long that a sentence about cows felt like something she might break if she pressed on it.

The lamp guttered; she turned it down before the oil was gone.

In the dark the questions arranged themselves in the order she had been turning them, and went on arranging themselves, and she held them with no place to put them — a letter in an inside pocket, the stillness on her father's face, the English like a door opened half an inch — and eventually she slept.
