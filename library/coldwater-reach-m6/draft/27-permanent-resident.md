# Permanent Resident

The board convenes at seven, in the room above the post office that also serves the Selectmen when the Selectmen require a room, and the consequence of this double occupancy is a layered residue of both functions: the faint staleness of cigar smoke belonging to no one present, the particular quality of air that accumulates in spaces where civic obligation is discharged by men who would generally prefer to be elsewhere, and a row of windows along the east wall looking out on the main road below, which offers at least the evidence that the world is conducting its ordinary business regardless of what is transpiring on the second floor.

Marcus arrives at six fifty-three. He has walked from the Crow house in ten minutes, the December cold of the direct variety — not damp but capable, and interested in finding the gaps in one's coat — arriving at the stair below the meeting room while his hands were still finding the temperature of the night. He has been before this board three times: the original appointment review in the spring of 1942, the first annual renewal in the spring of 1943, and tonight. He knows four of the five members well enough to read their positions from their posture before a word is spoken.

Mrs. Vance, who chairs, is the kind of administrator that mid-coast Maine produces at intervals: direct, efficient, possessed of clear opinions that she communicates without theater and pursues without cruelty. Keller and Ames are both fishing-family men who sit on the board by civic obligation and who vote, in Marcus's experience, as Mrs. Vance votes unless given a specific reason to do otherwise, which no one has yet provided them. Stowe, the pharmacist, is a well-meaning man for whom conflict functions as a form of expenditure he finds difficult to justify; he has a genuine interest in the school and a genuine incapacity for contention, which produces a reliable combination of good intentions and two-second pauses before votes. The fifth member is Holcomb, who runs the hardware store on the south end of the main road and who has been, across two and a half years of contract meetings and school board sessions, the most consistent practitioner of what Marcus has come to think of as managed indefiniteness: a form of objection carefully maintained at a temperature below the threshold at which it would require naming.

The second reading of the contract renewal is the formal item. It occupies four minutes, or would have; the contract is unchanged from the first reading, no amendments are pending, and there is no procedural basis for any delay. Mrs. Vance moves through the evening's prefatory matters — a budget item, a question about the gymnasium roof that Henry has raised through proper channels — and arrives at the renewal.

Holcomb says: "I think the board would like to understand something about the long-term intentions of the candidate regarding his place in the community."

*The candidate.* Marcus allows this to rest where it has been placed.

The room does the thing rooms do when a thing long circling has finally been said: it steadies, slightly, as though the low-grade vibration that has attended Holcomb's presence across twenty-six months has resolved into a single audible frequency and can now dissipate.

Several questions are embedded in what Holcomb has said. Some of them are the board's legitimate business: whether Marcus intends to remain in Coldwater, which is a reasonable administrative concern. Some of them are something else, a question about category and provenance that is not this board's jurisdiction and never was. Marcus answers the answerable question.

"I filed the initial declaration of intent for naturalization in November," he says. "At the federal district court in Rockland. These are the first papers — the beginning of the formal process, not the conclusion of it. I mention it because it seems relevant to the question of long-term intentions."

Holcomb's pen, which has been in his hand, descends to the table.

Mrs. Vance looks at Holcomb. She waits three seconds, which is longer than she generally waits for anything, which is its own form of statement. Then she calls the vote.

Keller: in favor. Ames: in favor. Stowe, after his characteristic pause: in favor. Holcomb: opposed. Mrs. Vance: in favor, noting same in the minutes. Renewed.

There is a minor quality of interest, Marcus thinks, in Holcomb's having voted opposed rather than abstaining — it is the first specific position the man has committed to paper across twenty-six months of board service relative to Marcus's employment, which means the first papers have had the secondary effect of finally making Holcomb legible, a function Marcus had not considered when he filed them and does not consider a loss.

The meeting adjourns at seven twenty-one. Mrs. Vance extends her hand; Marcus takes it; she releases it and turns to the minutes.

---

He does not leave by the side door. His usual entrance and exit is the side, which puts him directly on the street he walks home; tonight he takes the main corridor, which runs the length of the building and exits past the front window.

The placard has been there since November 1942. It is white-bordered, approximately twenty by thirty inches, with the heading COLDWATER REACH MEN IN SERVICE, and below it a list alphabetical by surname, two columns: name on the left, branch and theater on the right. He has read it many times without looking at it closely. Tonight he is looking for a specific entry.

In March — the tenth, a Thursday, which he can date because it was the day after the first decent break in the winter weather, when the temperature had climbed above freezing for the first time since January and the road outside the Crow house had smelled of it — Henry had come to the kitchen table before Marcus's second cup of coffee. He asked, without preface, whether Marcus thought James should be on the window list at the school.

The principal may not have thought, Henry said. Not *overlooked*; not *chose not to include*; the principal *may not have thought*. It was the most precisely accurate description of what had most likely occurred — an omission produced by a form of inattention that is not hostile, merely incomplete.

He had gone to the principal the next morning. He had said: "I notice that James Crow's name is not on the service list in the front window." The principal looked at him. "He serves in the United States Army, Pacific theater," Marcus said. The principal said he would look into it. The name appeared the following week. That was in March; it has been there nine months.

He finds it now: J. CROW, Army, Pacific. Between Cowan, J. — Navy — and Delaney, T. — Marines, Confirmed Fallen, August 1944, which is new; Delaney was missing the last time Marcus read the placard closely.

Twenty-four names. He counts.

Outside, the main road is empty in the December cold, the far streetlamp putting a yellow circle on the pavement below.

He goes out the side.

---

The town hall is on his route home — the corner of Main and the harbor road — and he passes it at seven thirty-five. The second-floor windows are dark; the building has a particular solidity that comes from the conviction, in the men who built it in 1904, that civic buildings should not be easily moved.

He had been here at four forty in the afternoon, before the evening meeting, to deliver a letter to the county clerk's office counter. The errand had taken twelve minutes.

The relief administration had been sending correspondence to the Crow household since the previous April concerning the tax status of the property assessment — correspondence that correctly identified the applicable regulation and incorrectly identified which subsection of it applied, generating a determination that was consequently wrong by a margin that had been costing Lillian Crow a specific assessable amount each quarter. The error was not large. It was not small. It had been producing further correspondence in response since April and had not resolved.

Marcus had located the applicable authority in October: Section 4(b) of the federal relief circular, read against a 1941 district court decision out of Portland that was directly on point and that Rideout's office had not cited, possibly because it had not located it, possibly for other reasons. He had drafted the letter in the first week of December. He had corrected one imprecision of phrasing in the second reading — a matter of the precise scope of the 1941 decision's holding — and then let it stand.

He had not told Henry or Lillian he was doing it. There was no reason to tell them before it was done; once it was done, they would know from the outcome.

Rideout, when Marcus presented the envelope, read the return address and then opened it and read the letter while Marcus waited at the counter. He set it on the desk to his right. He did not ask any questions. He said he would see to it. This was, in Marcus's considered assessment, an accurate statement about what would happen, given what the letter contained.

---

Past the town hall the route goes by the chandler's, which is shuttered at this hour, and the diner, whose front windows fog with steam from the coffee urns and through which Marcus can make out the shapes of three or four people at the counter. Then Holcomb's hardware, which is dark, a fact he notes without slowing. Then the Baptist church, where someone has put a cedar wreath on the door in the last day or two — it is still the bright green of recently cut cedar, which by Christmas will be the gray-green that cedar goes and then the dry brown that it stays until someone takes it down in February or leaves it for a March blow to manage.

The sidewalk has a raised seam at the quarter-block past the church. He has known this for two years and nine months.

Three things, in the span of some weeks: the name in the C's on the school window, the letter on Rideout's desk, the receipt in the drawer. They are of different scales and different orders of consequence.

He had taken the seven-fifteen bus to Rockland on a Thursday in November. The clerk's name at the filing window was Moreau, a woman in her early thirties who processed the declaration with the precision of someone for whom it is a familiar procedure. She had asked whether he intended in good faith to become a citizen of the United States. He had said yes. She date-stamped the declaration, recorded the filing number, gave him a receipt.

The receipt is in the right-hand drawer of his desk, beneath the Latin dictionary, where it has been since November.

He walks. The cold is direct. He is walking at his usual pace, which is the pace of a man who knows where the sidewalk is uneven.

He turns onto the street that the town calls Spruce Street — not its formal name, but the name it is known by, on account of two old spruce trees at the corner that have survived all the road work by the apparent fact of being too large for anyone to move. The Crow house is the fourth from the corner, with the kitchen light on.

---

The back entry is narrow — boots on the mat, extra wood stacked against the wall, the smell of cold air and pine sap caught between the outside cold and the warmth of the kitchen. He gets his coat off and his scarf and the wool hat that Lillian pressed on him in November, which is wool and dark and does not pretend to be anything other than a hat.

Lillian is at the range.

She says: "Thank you for the letter."

"You're welcome," Marcus says.

She does not turn from the stove. He goes to the cabinet where the cups live and takes one down. The coffee is on; the pot is warm. He pours and carries the cup to the table and sits.

Henry comes in from the hallway a few minutes later — from the sitting room, where he is on Tuesday evenings; Marcus can hear, faintly, that the radio has been left on. Henry takes a cup from the cabinet, pours, and sits at the table across from Marcus without speaking. The stove makes its particular sound: the low tick of the metal contracting around the fire in the way it does at this hour, when the evening has been burning down for several hours and the kitchen has reached whatever equilibrium it will hold for the night.

Lillian brings the pot to the table. She fills her cup and sits at her end — the near corner, which is hers by the accumulation of evenings rather than by assignment.

Three cups on the table. The coffee is thin — the ration book being what it has been all fall, and what they call coffee in December being forty percent coffee and the rest being chicory and good intentions — but it is hot and it is in a cup and the cup is on the table and there is no reason to be anywhere else.

Outside the window the yard is dark and still. The woodpile is a shape against the fence. The apple tree at the far corner, which Marcus has been watching since October, is bare.

The stove ticks.

He is not going anywhere.
