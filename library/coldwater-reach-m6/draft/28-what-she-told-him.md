# What She Told Him

The other two were out the door before *Bis Montag* had finished clearing the room — chairs pushed back, coats pulled on, gone in the organized rush they'd made automatic, the door swinging shut behind them and leaving the classroom to its own sounds: the radiator under the south window doing its work, the late January light coming sideways through the east windows, the faint noise of the school still in progress somewhere down the hall.

Hannah had both gloves on. Her coat was already buttoned. Her books were on the desk.

Marcus came down the rows with the folder, collecting the translation exercise, and when he got to her row she held out her paper. Then she said she had a question about the second paragraph. The word she'd translated as *collected*, she said — she'd looked at it again and thought *composed* might be closer.

He stopped and read the line. *Gelassen,* he said.

She nodded.

He said: the word comes from *lassen*. To let. To leave. *Gelassen* is the composure that arrives from having practiced letting go — the calm is made of the releasing, not the other way around. *Collected* is not wrong, he said, but it gets only the surface without the work that's underneath.

She wrote *composed — made of letting go* in the margin.

He took her paper with the rest and went back to his desk. She picked up her books.

She didn't go.

She stood at her desk while the school afternoon finished itself around her — footsteps going the other direction, a locker shutting twice before it caught, then quiet. Marcus was at his desk sorting papers, his back not quite to her, his pen uncapped. He had not told her she could leave; he also had not asked why she was still there.

She set her books back down.

She said: "Dr. Klein — there's something I thought you should know. Because of the German."

He set down his pen. When he looked at her it was the full attention, not the patient waiting-for-the-expected-answer, but the other kind she had learned to recognize over a year in this room: all the way in, nothing held in reserve.

She had been carrying this since November. The note was in her coat pocket. She left it there.

She said: "My parents have a tin box that lives on the top shelf of the wardrobe in their room. I found it last April — I was looking for the farm lease document, completely different errand — and I found it instead." The words came carefully, the way words came when you had been arranging them in your head a long time and were finally saying them out loud. "Inside there was a photograph. Two men standing in front of a building. On the back, in pen: *W. Grau, A. Pfeiffer, Bamberg, 1921.*"

Marcus was still.

"My father was younger in it," she said. "But the shape of him — the way he held his shoulders, I knew it." She held the image the way she'd held it since April: the photograph square and old, already browning at the corners the way photographs went after twenty-some years, the two figures in front of a stone building's façade in a city she'd never been to. The man who wasn't her father: wider, heavier in the shoulders, the build of someone who had done outdoor work for a long time, the neck thicker than his coat collar quite accommodated. His face was harder to keep — an ordinary face, the kind a photograph from 1921 wouldn't tell you much about — but the build had stayed with her. She had looked at it for maybe three minutes and then put it back. "Heavy in the shoulders," she said. "Laborer's build. I left the photograph in the box where I found it. But I saw it clearly."

Outside the east windows two crows were working the far end of the sports field, walking their methodical crow-walk through the winter-flattened grass. She watched them for a moment.

"In November," she said, "there was a transfer. One of the German prisoners assigned to our farm — the youngest one, Karl Reinmar, he'd been there since October of 1943 — he was reassigned to another work camp." She paused. "He was from Ansbach. It's about fifty kilometers from Bamberg. I measured it on the classroom map last April, after I found the photograph." She stopped herself. "I'm not going in order."

"There's no order required," Marcus said.

She put her hands on the back of the chair.

"In August," she said. "We were doing the hay. Karl and I had been talking most of the summer — as much as we could manage, given the language. He said a name: *Werner. Von Ansbach. Er wollte kommen.*" The German came easily here; this was a German room, and she had been saying the phrase to herself since August. "Werner, from Ansbach. He wanted to come. Already past tense when he said it — the wanting, and the not-arriving, the same fact." "I knew the name from the photograph. I understood they meant the same person. I didn't say so to Karl. I don't know if he knew I'd recognized it."

The crows outside had moved to the far corner of the field.

She put her hand into her coat pocket and took out the note. It had been folded the same way since November, along two creases that had gone soft from handling. She held it out.

Marcus came around the desk. She placed it in his hand.

He read it. He read it a second time without any expression she could read. He handed it back.

"He gave it to you at the transfer?"

"From his breast pocket." She looked at the note. "When they were loading. He came to where I was standing."

She unfolded it on the edge of his desk and smoothed it flat with two fingers.

*Werner Grau. From Ansbach. He is dead. I am sorry.*

Below that the birds — five of them, German on the left, English or Latin on the right, Karl's careful handwriting, the letters not quite American in their shape. *Seidenschwanz / Bombycilla cedrorum* at the top.

She had read it enough times that she could hold the whole thing in her head.

"He didn't say more than this," she said. "But he knew. And he must have known by then that I'd understand the name." She folded the note back along its creases. "I've been carrying it since November. I kept trying to think who to give it to. Or not give it to — who to show it to."

She put the note back in her pocket.

Then she told him about the kitchen.

"That evening," she said. "The same day as the transfer. My father was at the table. I came in from the barn." The German arrived in her mouth the way it did when her father's world was close and she was standing inside it: "*Werner Grau ist tot.*"

Marcus did not move.

"He didn't move either. His hands were on the table and he kept them there." She could see the kitchen: the lamp on the table, the supper things not yet cleared, her father's back and his hands and the stillness of him — not the stillness of someone who had not heard but the stillness of someone who was holding something down. "Maybe ten seconds. And then he said — " She said the two words with the weight her father had put in them, flat and definite and without give, like a door long since closed. "*Ich weiß.*"

She waited for more to come.

"Then he picked up his fork," she said.

The radiator worked its sound. Down the hall a door opened and closed. Outside, the crows had gone from the sports field — she didn't know when they'd left.

Marcus sat back in his chair. He put his hands flat on the desk and looked at them for a moment.

When he asked the first question it came precisely, without preamble: "The man in the photograph. You said middle-aged. Would you stand by that?"

She thought about it properly, giving it what it deserved. The photograph: the two men in front of the building, the formality of a picture taken in 1921, both of them arrested by the camera's demand into something more formal than they probably were. Werner Grau with his wide shoulders and the coat that didn't quite fit them and the face she couldn't hold perfectly — not young. The age her father would have been when she was very small, when she couldn't have said what middle-aged meant but knew it as a shape, a category of done-with-being-young and not-yet-old.

"Yes," she said. "Middle-aged."

He nodded once.

"And Karl — when he said Werner wanted to come. Did he give any sense of when? Which season?"

She went back to the hayfield in August, the long afternoon of it, Karl saying the name in the particular careful way he said things he'd been carrying: *Er wollte kommen*. And there was something else — a phrase from another time, months before the transfer, that she couldn't place exactly in the calendar but had in her: *vor dem großen Sturm*, before the big storm. She wasn't entirely certain it had been about Werner specifically, but it had been about that October. And there was only one October storm like the one in 1943.

"I think he meant before the storm," she said. "The October storm. 1943." She looked at Marcus. "I'm not certain — I can't give you the exact words. But that's what I understood."

Marcus said: "Thank you. You were right to tell me."

She picked up her books. The classroom was itself: the desks in their rows, the late January light going gray in the east windows, the map of central Europe above the door with its border lines belonging to a Europe that didn't exist anymore and hadn't for some time. She had been in this room enough times to know all of it without looking.

Marcus was still in his chair, watching her with the same even attention he'd had since she'd said *because of the German* — the full-attention look she had learned the shape of over a year, the one that held what it was given and didn't spill.

She went to the door. At the frame she stopped, her hand on the wood.

"I thought you should know," she said. "Because of the German."

Then she went.

---

The hallway was bright and mostly empty, the afternoon session over, a few students still at lockers. She walked down it: past the lockers, past the glass case with the athletic trophies, past the front window where the service placard hung in its white border, the names in two columns. She didn't stop to read it. She kept walking.

The main door was heavy with the cold behind it. She put her shoulder to it and it gave, swinging open into the January air — the clean kind, not damp, just cold, finding the collar and the wrists and the back of the hands immediately and stating its position clearly. She went down the concrete steps.

The sky was the low white of January, not quite cloud and not quite clear, the color of the afternoon waiting to become evening. The bus stop was at the bottom of the hill. She turned toward it.

On the harbor road below she could catch the harbor on the air — salt, cold water, the stillness of things that float without going anywhere in winter. She didn't look toward it. She was walking.

Her hand went into her coat pocket. Her fingers found the note: the two soft creases, the size of it. She had been reaching for it since November the way you reach for a stone in a shoe without deciding to.

She took her hand out.

The bus wasn't there yet. She stood at the stop in the cold, hands at her sides, coat buttoned to the top, and the town held its January shape around her: the church, the diner windows clouded with steam, the hardware shuttered, the harbor road turning off below the rise. She stood there and didn't reach for anything.

The bus came around the lower curve, its headlights already on against the darkening afternoon, and she got on. She found a seat on the left side. The doors closed.

The town went past the windows: the church, the diner, the main road carrying on toward the south. The harbor turnoff came and went below her. She watched it without marking it, without naming it, and the bus went on and the afternoon went on and it carried her home.
