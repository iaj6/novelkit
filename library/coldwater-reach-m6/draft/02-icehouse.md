# Icehouse

The water in the trough had a skim of ice — the kind that doesn't last past seven — and Hannah broke it with the heel of her hand before the first cows came in.

The barn smelled the way it smelled in October: cattle breath, wet straw, the good dense smell of old wood that had been absorbing winters longer than anyone in the family had been alive. The work lantern hung from the nail by the feed room door, yellow in the early dark. She had the feed sorted and the first three stanchions set, and the barn was, for a few minutes more, completely hers. Her father would be down from the upper hayloft soon; the four prisoners would come across the yard from the icehouse at quarter to seven; the day would become the day it was going to be. Until then she had this: the warmth coming off the cows in the cold barn air, and her hands that knew what to do without her needing to be present for it.

The cows came in the way they always came in. Bessie leading, then the two Guernseys whose names she still mixed up after two years, then the long line of them filling the central aisle without hurry, without improvisation, with the complete certainty of animals who have done this ten thousand mornings and see no reason to change on the ten thousand and first. She worked down the row: manger sections, water, the individual quirks of individual animals — the brown Guernsey that needed coaxing to the left side, the old one who liked to be spoken to. Her hands knew each of these things. Her mind went somewhere else while they worked, loose and not attached to anything, the way it went in the early mornings before it had been given a task.

She was on the sixth cow when the icehouse door opened across the yard.

She had learned, in two weeks, to read that sequence: the bar-latch lifting, the creak of the lower hinge that hadn't been fully planed when they rehung it in August, boots on the wooden step. The four of them came out in the same order they always came out — the two older ones first, then the one she thought of as the Stuttgarter because she had once caught him say that city's name in a sentence she couldn't follow the rest of, then Karl at the end, moving with a hitched, slightly too-careful gait of someone who hadn't slept flat or long enough.

Lonzo Reed was already outside the door. He was always already there — she had been trying to determine for two weeks whether he was an early riser by nature or simply wasn't sleeping much, without being able to settle it either way. He held the carbine in the crook of his arm and watched the four men cross the yard with the particular quality of attention that doesn't look like attention: his head didn't move, but nothing in the farmyard happened without him clocking it. She had seen him notice things before she did.

Her father came down from the hayloft with the pitchfork, nodded once at Lonzo — the same nod every morning, the same tilt of his chin — and spoke to the two older prisoners:

*Hier zuerst. Dann die Weide.*

Short. Pointing with his chin toward the east wall where the feed shovels were racked. The older man — the one she thought of as the Bavarian, which was maybe right and maybe half-right — picked up his bucket without being asked twice. He was a man who knew farm labor. She could tell by the way he held things.

Karl reached for the second bucket and took it by the side rim. Wrong — the bucket tilted on the way up and sloshed. The Bavarian looked at Karl's hands, not his face, and Karl shifted his grip to the bail without a word exchanged. He had been here two weeks and there were still things like this: tools picked up wrong, a gesture misread, the physical grammar of farm work not yet his. He was not incompetent. She had watched him closely enough to see that: he absorbed each correction without resentment, and before he did an unfamiliar thing he took one beat first — watched a man do it, checked his own translation before he committed to the page. Then he did the thing.

He looked up from the bucket and saw her watching him from the sixth stall.

He looked away first. He always looked away first.

She turned back to the cows. Their flanks were warm against her hands.

The four of them worked through the morning assignments. Her father moved among them in German, directing with few words, and the two older ones answered in the same economy. The Stuttgarter worked with the carefulness of a man who does not want to be corrected; Karl worked with the quickness of someone who doesn't mind it. She watched this from the edge of her attention the way she watched most things on the farm — not with the intention of understanding it, just taking it in, the way the barn takes in weather.

Her father handed her the feed tally clipboard when he passed her, not breaking stride. She took it and said "Good morning" to his profile.

*Danke,* he said, already past her, checking the stanchion locks.

She wrote the date in the upper right corner of the tally: *October 14.* She said to his back, in English: "The brown Guernsey is still off. Third morning."

He did not turn. He did not adjust.

---

She carried the milk pails across the farmyard in the early light. Lonzo Reed had come back to his post outside the icehouse door, which stood open now; through the opening she could see the interior — the two-tiered bunk frames along the walls, the small table with the lamp on it, a pair of boots set outside the lower frame at the near end. Then she went in the kitchen door.

The stove was cold; she had to go to the woodshed for kindling before she could get it going. When she came back her mother was already at the table.

Her mother's hands were around the tea cup — the white one with the thin blue ring — and the tea had gone to the temperature of the room. She was looking at a place on the table a foot beyond the cup, at nothing, with the attentiveness she sometimes brought to nothing.

Hannah broke two eggs into the pan and cut the bread and got the apple butter from the shelf. She put a plate together and set it at her mother's elbow, not directly in front of her. Directly in front felt like an instruction.

Her mother looked at the plate.

Hannah sat down in her father's empty chair. Through the kitchen window she could see the corner of the icehouse in the thin early light. Lonzo Reed was walking the farmyard perimeter — not going anywhere, just keeping himself to a shape. His breath came in small clouds.

Her mother's cup made a sound against the table.

After a while Hannah got up and put her own eggs on her own plate and stood at the counter and ate them, looking out the window.

---

She had to run the last stretch to the Route 1 stop, her coat half-buttoned and mud still on her boots from the barn. She got on the bus out of breath and slid into the middle section where the seats were more intact.

Ruthanne Gorey and June Pullen were two rows ahead, talking since the Gorey stop, and what they were talking about got into the bus around Hannah the way cold gets into a building — not through any one opening but through all the small ones at once.

A body on the breakwater. Ruthanne's father had heard it from Clement Ames, who'd been at the fish pier on Tuesday morning when the Coast Guard truck came down. A man from nowhere anyone knew, and no papers at all, nothing on him. Probably a sailor off a convoy, Ruthanne said — those men never carried papers on them, the papers stayed with the ship, that was what her father said. June thought maybe a fisherman swept overboard, some boat no one had reported yet. Her brother had heard something about a U-boat in the Penobscot. Ruthanne said that was last October and everyone was always saying that. They went back and forth with it, not unkindly, the way you discuss something terrible that has happened to no one you know.

Hannah listened.

*No papers.* She turned the phrase and looked at its underside. Not the body — she had no image for it, had not been near the harbor — but the condition itself. To arrive somewhere carrying nothing that said your name, your nationality, where you had come from or where you were going. To be, in the official sense, unattached to the record of yourself.

The bus was cold. She tucked her hands into her sleeves.

She sat through history and algebra and English and watched, during fourth period, as the birches at the edge of the playing field let go of their leaves in ones and twos.

---

Dinner was late. Her father had been out to the north pasture fence — a section down from the storm, needing restringing before the cows found the opening — and came in with mud to both knees. Her mother had already reset the table with the everyday dishes.

They sat.

The lamp was the brass one that had been her grandmother's. Under it her father's face looked older than it did in the barn, the lines deeper, settled into angles she recognized without being able to name. He cut his meat. Her mother worked the bread at the edge of her plate, pulling it into smaller pieces without eating it.

"I heard there was a body on the breakwater," Hannah said.

"A man nobody knows. No papers."

Her father's knife and fork stopped.

Both, together, on the plate. Three seconds — she counted. Then he cut the meat and put the piece in his mouth and chewed it and swallowed. He reached for his water glass and set it down. He looked at his plate.

He said nothing.

He did not look at her.

Her mother had not looked up from the bread.

Hannah finished her dinner. She washed the plates in the order she always washed them, hung the dish towel on the stove bar, went upstairs.

---

Her room was on the east side of the house, the window looking over the farmyard — past the barn's near corner to the icehouse beyond, its low roofline, its single window, the wooden step. Since the first night in October when the lamp appeared there, she had looked at it last thing before she slept. It had seemed wrong to her at first, that rectangle of light in the wrong place. Then less wrong. Then simply part of what the farmyard was at night.

She lay on top of the quilt with her boots still on and looked at the ceiling. The house was quiet. Her parents' door was shut. Through the east pine the wind moved in long passes, a sound she had fallen asleep under her whole life.

She was still carrying the day. Her father's knife and fork stopped for three seconds on the plate. Her mother's hands around the cold cup in the same position as when she'd left for the barn. *No papers* — the phrase turning and turning without the right question to stop it. Karl's bucket shifted from rim to bail before anyone said a word.

After a while she turned over and looked out the window.

Karl was standing outside the icehouse door.

He had on the jacket with the stenciling on the back, collar turned up against the cold. He was looking at the sky with his whole head tipped back — the stars were out tonight, more than usual, the clear dry October dark you got when the fog lifted and the cold came in from inland. He held a cigarette between two fingers, its ember small and reddish, burning down on its own.

He said something.

One word, maybe two. She had the window shut and couldn't hear it, but she could see his lips move and his breath make a small cloud and the word go out of him into the night air. Not to anyone.

He said it again, the same one.

She pressed her fingertips against the glass. His breath made another cloud. He said it a third time, and this time, in the stillness of the yard, she caught it through the cold glass: *Seidenschwanz.* She held the sound. She did not know what it meant. She had German enough to follow her father's directives and the vocabulary Dr. Klein put on the board, and this was neither of those things. It was Karl's word, and the night's word, and whatever it was naming she could not say.

He stood outside a while longer. He finished the cigarette and dropped it on the step and put his boot on it with the careful deliberateness of someone told to be careful about that. He looked up at the sky one last time, his face tilted back and still. Then he went inside.

The lamp in the icehouse window dimmed — not all at once, but down, turned low, to almost nothing.

She watched the dark rectangle.

Through the window's edge, where the seal had never been right since she'd known this room: turned earth, the October cold that comes from inland fields without any salt in it, and underneath that the faint warmth of the barn.

She reached up and pulled the window shut.

The icehouse was dark.
