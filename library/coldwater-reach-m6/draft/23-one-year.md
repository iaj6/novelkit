# One Year

He took the harbor road from the foot of Schoolhouse Hill, as he had taken it on most afternoons in two and a half years. The road ran three-quarters of a mile to the pier head, crushed stone with the grass coming through the middle where the wheels did not run; the smell of the harbor moved against him as he came down — cold salt water and diesel, and under both the smell of wet granite that he had absorbed, over the course of his time here, into what he thought of as the working inventory of the place.

It was the twelfth of October.

One year since the harbormaster's log had carried its first entry on the matter. Two and a half years since he had set his suitcase on the floor of the spare room at the top of Henry Crow's stairs. Six years since Vienna. He held these intervals as he held all precise dates: accurately, as a matter of discipline, not to be confused with what the accumulation of them was for.

The harbor came into view past the cold-storage house.

---

The cold in October had a different quality than the summer cold, which was still a marine cold, the cold of fog and damp and the offshore breeze moderated by the season's remaining warmth. October had given up on moderation. The air off the harbor at the foot of the hill was the air of what was coming rather than what had been, and it carried, mixed with the salt and the diesel, the low-tide smell of October flats: cold mud and rockweed, the harbor floor exposed an extra foot by the autumn tidal range. He had added this smell to the inventory in his second October here, filed under the heading of *this place in this particular week of autumn*, which was as specific as any entry in the inventory was required to be.

Most of the offshore fleet was hauled or preparing to haul. Three draggers were already up on the boatyard railway, their hulls in the pale wood of fresh bottom paint, their shapes oddly elevated without the sea to hold them level. The sardine carrier at the fish pier was loading its last run of the season. The lobster boats were down from their summer counts — he had been counting the mooring buoys in the inner harbor since his first October here without having decided to count them, in the way you come to know the dimensions of a room by crossing it often enough before you think to measure its walls.

The inner harbor was quiet in the way of the brief interval between the end of the season's work and the beginning of what winter made of the harbor — a week or two of stillness, the mooring chains running straight down in the slack water without motion. The water in the channel moved between what was still there without urgency. The cold was steady and without argument.

He turned at the boatyard fence toward the pier head.

---

The breakwater extended from the south headland in an L, the outer arm turning at its elbow toward open water. He had looked at it many times — from the pier head, from the harbor road in its various weathers, from the harbormaster's shed window as he passed on his afternoon walks. He had not walked it.

There was no reason he could have stated precisely for walking it today. The afternoon's marking was done; the stack of translation exercises on his desk was not going anywhere. The light was still adequate. He walked toward the breakwater's landward end and went over the low seawall step and onto the granite without having told himself he was going to.

The surface was irregular — rubble construction, the stones fitted without mortar, the faces worn smooth between the courses. His shoes found the flat planes between the higher stones and moved along them with some care. To his left the outer face dropped to the October sea: twelve or fifteen feet of granite to the water, which was the gray-green here of deep water without a bottom to lighten it. The swell was working in on a long period, lifting and falling against the outer face without violence, the sound at the base of the rock a low continuous suck and drain that was not the sound of the inner harbor but the sound of open water doing what it did against fixed stone, without particular regard for what the stone was there for.

He walked to the elbow.

---

At the elbow the cap widened where the construction doubled back, and there was a section of flat stone large enough to stand on without having to pick between the irregular courses. He stood on it.

The wind from the east had no land behind it, coming off open water without the headland's modification. It carried the cold of unobstructed distance, which was a different quality than the harbor cold — less interested in compromise. The bell buoy at the channel entrance was audible from here in a different way than from the pier head or from his room at night: not muffled by the headland's bulk, closer to its actual sound on the water, which was a lower note and longer interval than the version that arrived through walls and granite.

He looked at the October water.

He had been carrying Werner Grau for a year in the way one carries an assembled account: as a set of documented and plausible facts, each uncertainty marked, the limits of the account defined. He had put everything on a sheet of school paper in May of 1944, had drawn a circle around the phrase *by some combination*, and had locked it in the bottom desk drawer, where it had remained since.

For a year Werner Grau had existed in that drawer. The classified account. The provisional conclusions.

What Marcus had not done — what the year had apparently required without his noticing that it was requiring it — was consider Werner in the other way. Not as a set of established facts with their limits marked, but as a person who had made a set of choices.

A man from Ansbach, in Bavaria, which was a market town in a region Marcus could place precisely on any map and had no personal experience of. A man who had left Germany in 1938 or 1939, when leaving had still been possible for a person who moved with some decisiveness, and who had then managed five years in a country not his own on papers whose legitimacy had become, by 1943, a matter of administrative interpretation. Who had managed this for five years and had then decided — for reasons the letter had given Marcus only obliquely, the Pfeiffer name and the Reinmar name and the implication of a message that had waited long enough — that the time had come to make for Maine.

Marcus had made a version of this calculation himself, in Paris in the autumn of 1939, standing at his desk with a newspaper that clarified what the summer had still held in suspension. The calculation was not complicated. It had a correct answer and an incorrect answer, and the correct answer required leaving. He had the address of a colleague in London; he had money enough; he had the competence of a man who had left one country already and knew the mechanics of leaving a second. He had left in October and been in London by November.

Werner had made the same calculation. Werner had made it with less institutional support and onto a harbor dinghy on the coast of Maine at the end of September with the blow coming on. These were the variables of Werner's version, not Marcus's, and Marcus had stopped, at some point in the past year, placing them in the same column.

He had been carrying — according to what the letter implied, between the lines of a letter composed by a man who had learned to say only what needed saying and trusted the recipient to supply the rest — something in the category of message. Here is where Karl Reinmar is; here is what you should know. More than twenty years since Bamberg, where a photograph had been taken and two young men had been in the same place at the same time without any knowledge of what the next twenty years would require of either of them. Werner had come from wherever five years of managed displacement had taken him to put something directly into another man's hands, because some things could not be trusted to a letter.

He had made it to Maine. He had made it most of the way.

Marcus stood at the elbow of the breakwater for some time. The October water moved below him without hurry. The bell buoy rang its interval at the channel entrance, and then rang again.

---

He looked down.

One of the breakwater stones had broken from its course — roughly fist-sized, the fracture face still comparatively sharp, the outer surface worn smooth to the same gray-green as the cap stone around it. It had settled into the joint between two larger stones, held in place by its neighbors on three sides.

He picked it up.

Cold, in the way of granite that has been sitting in October air near October water. Heavier than its size suggested — the specific density of granite, which was what made it the material of choice for anything meant to hold its position against what the sea brought. He turned it once in his palm, looked at the rough fracture face and the worn outer surface, the gray-green that was the color of this harbor in this season.

He put it in his coat pocket.

He had the stone. He would carry it home.

---

He walked back along the breakwater.

The inner harbor opened ahead of him as he came toward the landward end: the anchorage, the boatyard with the hauled hulls on their jack stands, the cold-storage house, the pier head with the harbormaster's shed at its end and the window lit against the October dusk. Above the roofline the town held its standard configuration — the Congregational steeple the highest point, the school chimney beginning to smoke now that Henry had the boiler running again this week.

At the seawall step he paused. The bell buoy rang from the channel entrance — the same sound that came to his room on certain nights with the wind out of the east, the sound that had been part of the specific air of that room since his first week here, already filed in the inventory under the heading of *this place, this particular channel entrance, this buoy*. He heard it now without modification, from its actual position, with nothing between.

He went over the seawall step and walked back along the harbor road.

---

Henry was at the kitchen table when Marcus came in the back. A boiler fitting lay in a rag in front of him, two wrenches beside it; his hands were moving with the unhurried attention of a man who has understood the problem fully. He looked up at Marcus in the way he always looked up — taking note, not requiring commentary — and then looked back at the fitting.

Marcus hung his coat on the peg by the door — the coat with the stone in its pocket, which he would carry upstairs after supper — and washed his hands at the kitchen sink. Lillian had left a covered plate in the warming oven, as she did on evenings when the household schedules did not align in time for a shared meal. He poured coffee from the pot on the stove and sat at the kitchen table, not in Henry's way, and Henry went on working the fitting.

After supper he went upstairs.

The room was as he had left it: the window over the back garden, the desk with the exercise papers stacked and waiting, the bookshelf with its two rows of German and Latin and the three English titles he had bought in Cambridge and never returned. The chair by the window where he read on Sunday mornings when the light came in at the right angle. He took the stone from his coat pocket.

He set it on the windowsill.

It sat there as granite sits — its weight absorbed into the surface below it without demonstration or comment, without either offering or withholding. Gray-green, fist-sized, one face rough where it had broken from the course and the rest worn smooth by whatever number of years it had spent at the breakwater's elbow before this afternoon. A piece of the breakwater in the room where he slept, which was a fact of the same kind as the other facts in the room: accurate, present, making no claim about what it meant. He looked at it for a moment.

He went downstairs.

---

Through the dining room window the October dark was completing itself over the town. The streetlight at the corner came on — one of the town's fixed intervals, reliable and without reference to whether anyone was watching. The Congregational steeple held its position against what remained of the western light, which was diminishing in the way of October evenings, steadily and without drama.

Lillian passed the bread.

He took a piece. Henry was explaining the boiler fitting — whether the thread wanted redressing or whether a new washer would resolve it, a practical question with a knowable answer, the kind the evening ran on. Marcus ate. Outside the window the October dark settled over the street in the way it had settled in every October he had watched from this table.
