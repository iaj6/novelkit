# Cedar Waxwing

She broke the ice on the water trough first, the way she did every January morning — striking the base of the pail against the surface until it cracked and came away in one curved gray sheet. She pushed the pieces aside and set the pail underneath the pump. The water came up cold enough that the pail immediately fogged in the barn air.

Her father was already in the stanchion row. She could hear his voice from the far end — short sentences in German to the Stuttgarter about the feed count, *morgen, genug, nein* — and she caught the words without trying to catch them, the way she had been catching her father's German her whole life: not translation, just the sounds arriving and meaning washing in behind them.

The cold came through the board gaps on the north wall and lay along the floor in a way that was different from yard cold. Barn cold was thick with the animals, warmed and weighted by sixteen cows in their stanchions, and it carried the particular smell of January in a working barn — the bedding packed down now, worked through three months of winter use, the hay older and darker than it had been in October, the floor settled and compacted into itself. She had stopped noticing the smell the way you stop noticing a sound that doesn't change. She noticed it now because she'd been away two days at her cousin Marta's in Portland for Christmas and had come back into the smell as something new for a few minutes before it closed around her again.

The four Germans were already on the morning routine. She went to her row.

Karl was at the far end with the difficult cow, and he was not having difficulty. She caught this in the periphery. He moved through the milking lane without choosing his feet. He knew the cow: knew she went easily if you came from the left and didn't come from the right, knew the particular patience she required at the start of the morning, knew when to wait and when to push. This had taken him until December to learn. It was January now and he had it the way you have anything that's become the body's knowledge and stopped being the mind's.

She had learned to count to twelve in German from the morning counts: *eins, zwei, drei* — heard often enough that it had simply moved in without asking. Karl had twelve English words, by her count, and she had thirty German ones, and neither of them counted, and both of them had stopped noticing the asymmetry because the asymmetry was not the point.

---

She was filling the trough for the second time when the bird landed on the rim.

Cedar waxwing — the crest raised, the black mask tight across the eyes, the small yellow bracket at the tail's edge. It sat on the trough rim and looked at the water with the alert composure waxwings have, as if they have made a formal evaluation and decided to stay. She had been watching them in the old crabapple by the stone wall all December, working whatever the frozen berries still held.

Karl had come up to fill his pail. He stopped.

*Seidenschwanz,* he said. To the bird, or to himself — quietly.

She had known the word for two months. She'd found it in November in her father's bird guide — had run her finger down the *S* column until the entry was there, and had stood at the bookshelf until she understood the parts: *Seide* for silk, *Schwanz* for tail. Silk-tail. Cedar waxwing. The sound settling into the shape, finally, two months after she'd first heard it.

She said, "Cedar waxwing."

Karl looked at her. Then at the bird. Then back at her.

"Ce-dar," he said. "Wax-wing." He said it the way he said all new English — with the German vowels sitting inside the English syllables, the weight coming down a half-beat off from where American weight came down. It wasn't wrong. It was exact in its way, the way of someone learning a new material and handling each piece carefully before putting it down. He sounded each syllable as if he were measuring it, and in the measuring it became his.

She said *Seidenschwanz* back to him. The *ei* closing the way it should, or near enough. The *schw* she had been practicing privately — the German *sch* was not in English anywhere and you had to find the place in your mouth that made it.

He nodded once.

The bird sat on the trough rim for another two seconds and then was gone — up into the cold air and away toward the stone wall, a shape against the white sky, gone.

This was the form the thirty words took. They had built the vocabulary the way you build a fence: one piece at a time, where the need was, with whatever material was on hand. *Kuh, Eimer, kalt, morgen, Zaun, Tor.* Cow, bucket, cold, tomorrow, fence, gate. And now *Seidenschwanz* and cedar waxwing.

Her name in his mouth was *Hah-nah* — not the flat American version, both syllables equal and done with, but something with a shape to it, a slight lift at the first syllable as if it required a breath before it, as if a name were something worth arriving at properly. She had not corrected this. She was not going to correct it.

---

Her father came in for the grain check.

She heard the barn door and then his boots on the swept planks and she stayed at the trough, doing the work of topping off the second stall's water, not watching. He went to the bin, checked it by weight of the door, then by eye, and said something to Karl. Short sentences, the practical register, a grain count and a timeline for the afternoon: the German her father used for farm directives, which was not his full German, just the working face of it.

Karl answered. She heard the answer — the level of the grain, the afternoon plan, the correct present and future tense.

Her father did not respond immediately.

She looked up from the trough.

He was standing at the grain bin with his back to her. Not doing anything, not checking anything, just standing. The lamp threw his shadow long on the back wall. She watched the angle of his shoulders, the stillness in them — the specific stillness that was not waiting but the pause before something settled.

She had learned: fifty kilometers from Ansbach, her father had told her once, years before, in the matter-of-fact way of a person naming something factual that did not yet have weight to it. The farms, the particular look of that land in autumn. She had not done much with it at the time. It was doing something now, standing in her head while she watched the angle of her father's back, because Karl's German was not just German — it was the German of that region, those fifty kilometers, those particular vowels and the weight they were given, the sound of the place where her father had been a boy.

He had heard something in Karl's German.

Her father finished with the bin. He turned and walked back toward the door, passing her, and his face when he passed was closed in the way it had been closed for two years now, the expression stored somewhere past her reach. He did not look at her. He went out. The door swung shut.

She stood at the trough with the empty pail and listened to his boots crossing the farmyard.

Then she went back to her row.

---

At noon she brought her mother's tray upstairs — broth, bread, a dish of the stored applesauce — and set it on the table beside the window chair. Her mother looked at the tray. She picked up the spoon.

Hannah sat on the edge of the bed.

She had stopped talking at noon. It had taken most of the fall — she had been bringing the day's small reports along with the tray, offering them the way you offer something to fill a silence, and had understood eventually that the offering was for herself as much as her mother, and that her mother did not need it. She had stopped sometime in November. It hadn't been a decision. She had arrived one noon with nothing ready to say and had sat down, and it had been right in a way the talking hadn't been.

Her mother ate slowly. The window held the January afternoon — pale and flat, the kind of light that gave no warmth and asked no questions. The barn was visible, the icehouse was visible, the stone wall with the old crabapple. The bare top of it moved slightly in the wind.

Hannah had been trying to figure out for weeks whether her mother watched the farmyard or whether her eyes, when they were at the window, rested on nothing at all. She could not tell. Her mother's face when it was like this was closed the way certain skies were closed — weather in them, or not, and no way to know from outside.

Her father's silence was under pressure. It held something arranged against an interior wall, something that moved if you watched long enough. Her mother's silence was different. It had let go of something, or something had let go of it, and what was left was not waiting and was not grieving — it was simply what it was, settled at the bottom of itself. She had learned to sit inside it without trying to pull it toward her.

Her mother set the spoon down. Hannah picked up the tray.

---

The German homework was a translation exercise — two pages, a fictional letter, two people arranging a meeting. Marcus had written the assignment on the board in his careful hand: *Future and conditional: what the sentence commits to before the event. We are working on grammar. The letter is the occasion.*

She worked through the first page at the kitchen table with the stove going at her back. The words went cleanly. She could hear the grammar working — the verb held at the end of the clause, the sentence suspending all of its weight until the last piece arrived and everything resolved. She had liked this about German from the beginning: the sentence's patience with itself, the way it waited. Her father's German was like this. Karl's German was like this. Even the German she couldn't follow had this quality of holding back until the moment arrived.

Halfway through the second page she stopped.

She read back through it. The meeting was being arranged. Both people wanted to meet. The sentences were grammatically correct from beginning to end. And yet the conditional kept appearing where it didn't have to: *wenn es möglich ist, sofern die Umstände erlauben* — if it is possible, circumstances permitting. Both people used it. Both of them chose the construction that held the uncertainty open when they could have chosen the one that closed it.

She sat with this for a minute.

Marcus had given them a letter between two people who were not saying what they meant. She wasn't sure if he had done this on purpose or incidentally — if the grammar was the point and the content was just along for the grammar, or if he had chosen a sentence that meant something. She worked past it. She finished the translation and wrote her version in the exercise notebook and looked at what she had: two people arranging a meeting in the grammar of people who do not know whether they will see each other again.

She laid down the pencil. The kitchen window looked out at the farmyard, where nothing was moving except the crabapple top working slightly in the wind — the branches stripped now, nothing left on them, the movement small and reflexive, the tree going through the motion of wind without any leaves to show for it.

She closed the notebook. She would finish the last line Thursday. She would ask Marcus about the conditional if there was time after class.

---

The last barn check was after supper. She went through quickly in the cold — stove banked, water level at the second mark, latch secured against the wind. The cows were settled. She went out and latched the outer door.

Crossing the farmyard back toward the house, she passed the icehouse.

The lamp was on inside. It showed through the small oilpaper window, amber and steady, the warm color that the oilpaper gave to any light. She could hear voices through the plank wall — four of them, the low run of men talking at the end of the day. She couldn't hear the words. It was only the texture of it, four different registers going at once, the sound of men inside a room saying whatever you say when the work is finished and you have nowhere else to be.

She knew one of the voices by its position among the others. She kept walking.

---

In her room she stood at the curtain for a moment. Across the farmyard the icehouse lamp still showed — the oilpaper square holding its amber in the dark, steady, the way a lamp holds when there's no draft to move it.

The wind was in the eaves. It had been there since the December blow, the same low note running through the house frame every night, the eaves going with it and settling and going again. She had made peace with it during the daylight hours. At night it came back.

She set the exercise notebook on the dresser. Picked it up. Set it back down. She was not going to finish the last line tonight.

She got into bed.

*Seidenschwanz.* The *ei* closing in the mouth, the sound folding back on itself the way it didn't in English anywhere. She had tried it again today and been almost right — the *schw* nearly exact, the diphthong nearly closing the way his did. He had said *ce-dar wax-wing* with the German vowels inside the syllables, carrying their origin into the new language the way you carry anything long enough to become aware of the carrying.

The translation notebook was on the dresser. The last line had a question mark in the margin where she hadn't found the English equivalent that kept the right grammar and still felt like something a person would say. She would ask Thursday.

The wind went in the eaves. The lamp across the yard burned through the curtain, the amber she could feel at the edge of her vision without looking directly at it. She lay still and held the word — *Seidenschwanz*, cedar waxwing, *Seidenschwanz* — and the lamp kept its amber and the eaves kept their note, and she fell asleep with both of them going, the word and the wind and the light across the yard still burning.
