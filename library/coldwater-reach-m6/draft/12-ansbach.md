# Chapter Twelve — Ansbach

The fourth cow on his row was the old one, the one that would kick if you came at her from the left. He came from the right, settled on the three-legged stool, and began. The milk came down in the rhythm he had learned. This had surprised him — that his hands could learn a new herd. His father kept Simmental on the farm in Ansbach, brown and cream, and these were Holstein, black and white, and the cold here was a different cold, harder at its edge, without the softness the Franconian hills put into winter. But the hands had learned without needing to be told.

Four months. He held the number the way you hold a stone: not warm yet, not simply part of the weight you carry, but manageable.

He did not count toward an end. There was no end to count toward. The war would end when it ended. Until then there was this row of cows and the pail going heavier and the barn smelling of hay and dung and animal-warm, which smelled the same in Maine as in Bavaria. This sameness was not comfort.

The bakery on Karolinenstraße opened at five. By six the smell reached the kitchen window — the sweetness of *Hefegebäck* in morning cold — and his mother would call from the back of the house. Not urgently. Just the daily calling of his name from a known direction, which meant: you are here, it is morning, this is still the place.

He held this for a moment. Then he set it down.

Not buried. Set down at his side.

---

Through the barn door he could see across the farmyard. The sky had begun to lighten at its far edge — not sunrise yet, but what comes before it: the sky deciding. Hannah Pfeiffer crossed from the henhouse toward the feed shed, her coat buttoned to the collar, her breath a white cloud, a pail in each hand.

He watched.

She did not look through him. Fischer and Bauer and Hentschel were present — men in the same difficulty, which was a kind of presence — and Reed the guard was present as a watcher: noting him, accounting for him, not seeing him. Old Pfeiffer moved between barn and house with the quality of a man who had placed himself somewhere by accident and had found no procedure for revising it. The Army men who came each month with the supply truck were a form, a function, a category.

Hannah looked at him like a person at a specific person.

There had been the bird — *Seidenschwanz* — and she had said the English back, and after that she had said his name. *Karl.* He had been called many things. That word, said that way, was not common.

He did not have German or English for what this was. He knew it was not nothing.

---

The waxwing arrived at the fence post at the barn door without any announcement, the way they always arrived: already there when you looked. Black mask, yellow tail-tip, the small red wax of the secondary feathers.

"*Seidenschwanz*," he said. Quietly, so as not to startle it.

He kept a count. Forty-one birds since October, each given its German name and held in some interior list he had not meant to start keeping. It had begun with this one — the waxwing in the autumn, the first bird he had recognized at a distance in a country where none of the field-names matched anything he carried. And then the others, one by one: *Meisen* for the chickadees, black cap and white cheek, the same birds you found in any German woodlot; *Graureiher* for the great blue heron that came to the drainage ditch at the low end of the east field; *Bussard* for the red-tailed hawk that worked the north stone wall in the afternoon light. The small brown ones that moved through the meadow in January flocks he had not been able to name with certainty — he thought *Ammern*, something in the bunting family, but he was not sure.

It was not nostalgia.

The waxwing turned its head and looked at nothing in particular.

---

Fischer had said it last Tuesday, quietly, in the icehouse after supper. Not to Karl specifically — Fischer's way was to make announcements to the air and leave the air to distribute them. He had been a *Feldwebel*, a sergeant, and he kept his accounts.

*"Der Mann, der dich besuchen sollte. Er ist nicht gekommen."*

The man who was supposed to visit you. He didn't come.

Karl had known this already. He had known it since November, though in November he had been calling it something else — delay, the slowness of wartime mail. By December he had stopped calling it anything. It had become a shape: a gap in a fence line where the rail is missing. You know the gap when you reach it.

In September, a scrap of paper had come folded inside his mother's monthly letter. Three sentences in German, no name signed. Werner Grau, who had known Karl's father from the years on Karolinenstraße, was now in America. Werner hoped, if circumstances allowed, to arrange some contact. If it was possible.

He had read those three sentences until he could say them in his sleep.

October: nothing. November: nothing. December: nothing. January, and Fischer's voice in the icehouse, making the accounting that Fischer always made.

Karl had nodded at the air and returned to his bread. There was nothing to say in return. Fischer had not known Werner or Karl's father and had only heard something through whatever channel Fischer used for such things.

---

What he turned in his mind now — he moved to the next cow in the row and sat and set his hands to the work and let the thought run alongside it — was not the absence of contact itself. Men in wartime did not keep appointments. Men in wartime died or were arrested or crossed a border at the wrong moment and went into some process that had no window in it. He had been away long enough to know this; he had seen the gap in the fence from both sides.

The thing he kept turning was this: Werner Grau had come all this way. *Amerika.* Not by accident. He had made a plan and the plan had included Maine and had been close enough to completion that the three sentences could be sent, that the unsigned note could travel inside his mother's letter to this barn, to this farm, to a boy milking cows who was also from Karolinenstraße and would understand who Werner Grau was.

And then the October storm. Karl had watched it from the icehouse window: three days of wind and rain from the northeast, beginning the night of the eleventh or the twelfth, the lower field going to mud, the hay bent flat along the ground. He had watched it and not thought anything particular about it at the time — it had been weather, the weather of this place, harder than what he knew.

He did not think Werner had simply been lost in it. He thought Werner had found something, or come close to finding it, and that the storm had been the last thing in a sequence Karl could not see from inside the icehouse. He thought the gap in the fence was Werner — Werner who had crossed the Atlantic when crossing the Atlantic was not a small thing, who had found his way to Maine, who had known where the farm was and who was on it, and who had not come.

Karl did not know what had happened. He knew the shape. He knew the shape and he knew he was not going to learn the rest of it from here, in this barn, in a language of twelve words.

He set the pail down and moved to the last cow on his row.

---

The waxwing lifted from the fence post. It crossed the farmyard in a low arc, unhurried, and disappeared into the woodlot at the far edge of the field.

He watched the treeline for a moment. The woodlot held the bird — inside and invisible, already become a sound among sounds.

The barn was warm with the bodies of cows. Not the warmth a stove makes, arriving from one direction and weakening with distance, but the warmth of animals: patient, surrounding, the same in any country. His breath still showed. Outside the cold had not changed. But inside the warmth the work continued and the cold was only a fact about the air beyond the door, and he could hold that.

He heard Hannah cross the farmyard again. The empty pail swung from her hand and caught the fence post with a small sound. She did not look toward the barn.

*Seidenschwanz.* Gone.

He set his hands back to the work.
