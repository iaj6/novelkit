# Chapter Fourteen — The Photograph

The tin box lived on the shelf in her parents' room, above the folded shirts — had lived there, as far as Hannah could trace, since before she was old enough to notice things on shelves. Small and dark, the paint worn through at the corners to bright metal, a small dent near the clasp that she had pressed her thumbnail into many times without ever asking how it got there. She had moved the box dozens of times to reach the shirts. She had never opened it.

Her mother was downstairs. The room had the particular cold of upstairs rooms in April, heated only by what rose from the kitchen below, which in April was not much. The smell: cedar from the blocks Magda kept in the bottom drawer with the wool things, and beneath that something fainter — her father's shaving soap, or the wood of the floor still releasing winter, or simply the close particular smell of a room where two people have slept for twenty years. The bureau along the north wall had a painted border she had traced with her finger since she was four. The quilt on the bed was one her mother had made the winter before Hannah was born, eighteen years old and not looking it.

She set the laundry basket on the bed and began to sort.

Her mother's room, her mother's order. She moved with the particular care of a person who has learned to take up only the space she has been given. Her mother's blouses, her father's good shirts, the household linens. When she lifted the stack of folded shirts to place them on the shelf, the tin box came forward against her hands.

She set it on the bedspread.

The same as it had always been. The bright corners, the dent near the clasp. She held it a moment, the weight of it in both hands — lighter than it looked — and then she lifted the clasp and opened it.

Letters, folded to fit the box. She could see without reading them that they were in German — her father's hand, the cramped vertical script, the particular way he formed his capital *S* that she had known before she learned to write herself. She didn't unfold them. They were her parents' letters; that was enough. There were papers beneath, creased many times over, that might have been documents; she touched the edge of one and let it go. At the bottom, held together with a rubber band that had gone dry and left a faint brown mark on the photograph on top, a small stack of pictures.

She looked.

The first was a woman she didn't know — older, perhaps a grandmother, seated before a painted studio backdrop wearing the expression of someone enduring a long exposure. No name on the back. The second was a group of men outside a building she couldn't place, European, with a low-pitched slate roof. Nobody she recognized.

She came to the third photograph and stopped.

Two men before a stone doorway. A carved lintel above them, heavy and deliberate — civic, she thought, or a church entrance, the kind of archwork that had been standing for two or three centuries and didn't expect to stop. Both men in their thirties. Wool coats, hats held in their hands: they had taken the hats off for the photograph, which was what men did when they wanted the photograph to count. Both squinting. The shadows on the paving were short, the light full and direct, a summer afternoon or early autumn with the summer still in it. A quality to the light she couldn't name exactly, except that it was not the light here — not the flat particular grey of coastal Maine but something more direct, more central, a light that knew where it was from.

One of the men was her father.

She knew him by the jaw and the set of his shoulders and the way he held the hat brim, which hadn't changed. He looked younger than she had ever seen him — not happy exactly, but present. Unguarded. His face was simply a face, open to the bright day, not yet arranged into whatever it had since become arranged into.

The other man she looked at for a long time.

Heavy in the shoulders. A laborer's build, the neck thicker than her father's, the coat a little tight across the chest. She guessed he was her father's age in the photograph, early thirties. His face was ordinary — she looked at it and found nothing to catch on. Not unkind, not cold, not worried or proud or marked by any particular feeling. Just the face of a man who happened to be standing in front of a stone doorway on a bright day. The kind of face that comes up ahead of you in a line somewhere and by the time he's gone you couldn't say whether his eyes were light or dark. She kept looking, trying to fix it in memory, and the harder she looked the more it seemed to come apart, until what she was seeing was features in a rough vicinity of each other rather than a person. The squinting might have been concentration. It might have been the sun.

She turned the photograph over.

Her father's pencil. His vertical German hand.

*A. Pfeiffer. W. Grau. Bamberg, 1921.*

She stood at the edge of the bed and held the photograph and thought about Bamberg. Bavaria — she was fairly sure. Somewhere in the middle of the country, north of Munich. Not where her father's family was from; they had been from south of there, nearer the Austrian border. But not far from Ansbach either, she thought, and the thought came and went before she decided whether to catch it. The photograph was from 1921. The year before her parents came to Maine.

*W. Grau.* She had never heard the name. *Grau*: in German, grey. A man named Grey, standing beside her father in the afternoon light of a stone doorway, twenty-three years ago.

She put the photograph back with the others. Replaced the rubber band. Closed the box and latched it. Set it on the shelf above the shirts where it had always been.

She picked up the laundry basket and went back to her work.

---

The afternoon was farm work, which it had been every April of her life.

She filled the near trough and opened the gate to the far pasture, which had been closed since November. The mud on the track between the barn and the gate was deep and cold and smelled of the earth beginning to come back — different from summer mud, more mineral than animal, with something green underneath it. Two cows came out and stood in the new grass looking at it with their usual expression, which was the expression they had for everything. She worked the pump handle and let the water run. In the north field she could hear the four Germans — the sound of tools in turned ground, a word or two carried on the wind, Lonzo Reed's voice in its usual even tone and then quiet. She didn't look that way.

She split kindling. The axe came down and the cedar split clean, the smell sharp in the cold air. She stacked it under the overhang and worked until her shoulders told her to stop.

*W. Grau.*

The name was there through all of it, not urgently. The way a half-heard phrase stays with you during repetitive work — you think about something else and there it is again, unchanged. She tried to hold the face from the photograph and it kept going. Her father's face stayed because her father was here and she saw the same face every day. But the man beside him dissolved each time she turned her attention away, until what came back was only the shape of the shoulders and the squinting and the stone archway behind them, and not the face itself.

He had written both names on the back in pencil — in his own vertical German hand, a name for each face so neither would slip without the other. He had wanted to remember.

October arrived in her, the way it kept arriving: dinner, three weeks or so after the storm, her saying *they found a body on the breakwater* in the same tone she carried any news home from school, and her father's hands going still on his utensils. She had said something about the milking instead, because not asking had seemed the right thing, the careful thing, the choice that maintained the particular equilibrium of silence her household ran on.

She wasn't sure anymore that it had been.

She went inside.

Her mother was at the kitchen window, standing with her arms at her sides, looking out at the east field. Hannah had learned, over the better part of a year, not to try to read those stillnesses. She measured flour. She put the kettle on. Magda moved to the table and sat down, and neither of them spoke, which was the usual arrangement and worked well enough.

She kneaded the dough longer than she needed to and covered it with a cloth and sat at the table with her library book and read two pages three times without them settling into anything.

The letters in the tin box were in German; she hadn't read them. The papers she hadn't unfolded. Whatever her parents had kept from before — before Maine, before she was born, before whatever the before was that had become this household — it was in that box on the shelf in their room, and she had opened it today for the first time, and she was seventeen years old and had found a name she had never heard.

She took the loaves out when the kitchen smelled right and went to set the table.

---

Her father came in from the fields at half past five, which was when he always came in. His boots on the porch step, the pump, the towel on its nail. He sat down. Magda brought the soup. Hannah set the bread on the board, the pickles, the salt.

The lamp was on the table between them. The ceiling wiring had been unreliable since the spring rain two nights ago and her father didn't trust it after wet weather, so the oil lamp, which made the kitchen smaller and closer, the corners going to warm shadow, the window above the sink dark and reflecting the room back at itself.

Her father bowed his head.

*Komm, Herr Jesu, sei unser Gast.* The same four lines he had always said, always in German, at every meal, since before she was old enough to know what grace was. She moved her lips with the words without making sound — habit, and also she didn't want to forget them entirely. Her mother was still. Then her father raised his head and picked up his spoon.

The soup: potatoes, the last of the winter's dried beans, a hamhock stretched to its limit. There was bread. The lamp flame shifted in a brief draft from the hallway and stilled. Her father broke bread. Her mother moved her spoon through the broth.

She set her spoon down.

"Papa." Not carefully, not carelessly. A normal tone of voice, the tone she would use to ask about the fields or the feed. "Who is W. Grau?"

Her father's hands stopped.

She watched them. His fork was raised, and the piece of bread he had broken in his other hand, and both stopped where they were — two or three inches above the plate, suspended. The lamp flame moved again in the same draft. One second. Two. The flame stilled. Three. Then her father's hands came down. He set the bread on the side of his plate and picked up his spoon.

He said nothing.

She looked at her mother.

Magda had looked up.

Magda, who for months had kept her eyes to the table or the window or the interior distance she had been moving toward since the start of the war — Magda had looked up. Quickly. Her eyes found Hannah's across the lamp and the table.

What was in them was not alarm. Not the particular flatness of the depression that had been Magda's face for the better part of a year. Something older than that, something worn smooth by being held a long time — not bitterness, not grief exactly, though grief was somewhere underneath it. The look of a woman who has been sitting beside a door for a long time without knowing when it will open, and has now heard the latch.

Two seconds. Then Magda's eyes went back to her bowl.

Her father said nothing. He ate. He drank from his water glass. He broke the bread and ate the bread and did not look at Hannah and did not answer.

---

Hannah washed the dishes. Magda dried, her hands moving from bowl to towel to shelf with the efficiency of someone for whom the task has long since stopped requiring thought. Through the window above the sink the farmyard had gone dark. The barn roof flat against the sky. At the base of the icehouse door, a thin line of yellow light showed, steady and faint.

She handed the last plate to her mother and dried her own hands.

Her father had moved to the accounts book. The table was as it always was at this hour: book open to the usual page, his pen in his hand, the bread plate at the side, the salt beside it, the lamp moved to give him light on the columns. His pen made its small sounds. He did not look up when she passed.

She went upstairs.

Her room was dark. She sat on the edge of the bed without lighting the lamp and listened to the house — the pen, faintly; her mother's chair; outside, the April wind working at the window frame, still with winter in it at this hour. Down the hall, through the wall, the shelf above the shirts.

She tried once more to hold the face from the photograph. The shoulders, the squinting, the ordinary arrangement of features she had looked at and looked at and couldn't keep. She held it for a moment. Then it was gone, the same as it had gone every time.

But her father's hands had stopped.

She lay down in her coat. Downstairs, the pen moved in its columns. Outside the window, the wind worked at the frame.
