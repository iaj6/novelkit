# The Cove

The survey ran north to south: Heron Point first, then Seal Neck, then the two unnamed pockets between the outer heads, then the south cove behind the headland. She did this every September before the season closed. The work was not difficult. She had run the same route for eight years and the harbor launch knew the shape of it.

She went out at the morning ebb. The ebb pulled the water off the rock and showed what the season had deposited — pots and hawsers and storm-blown floats, the gear that accumulated where the harbor's circulation said it would accumulate. The pattern was reliable. Drifted things moved in a direction that could be predicted given the prevailing set of the tides and the shape of the headlands. She had mapped the pattern in her first years by noting what turned up where, and the map had held.

Heron Point: two lobster pots with the surface buoys worn blank. She logged the positions and the condition and went on. The gannet working the tide rip off the point was nothing to log; she logged it anyway, a habit from her early years when she was still learning what went into the record and what did not, a habit she had since decided to keep.

Seal Neck: a length of commercial hawser above the tide line, fifty feet or better, flagged for the boatyard to collect at their convenience. The small pocket east of Seal Neck: a mooring float she had been carrying in the spring records since the line parted in April. She hooked it with the boat hook and brought it aboard and wrote the return in the log. The second unnamed pocket: a section of fish net from some farm operation, three seasons old if she was reading the growth right, too far gone to save.

The light in September was flat and honest. Not the glare of August that showed the water's surface and pressed everything into a shimmer, and not the November light that compressed the whole harbor into gray distance. September put things at their actual size. She could see what was there.

She came around the south headland at half-ebb.

---

The cove behind the south headland was a hundred yards end to end. Its entrance faced east, which set it across the prevailing swell; what came in on the northeast blow came in, and what drifted on the tidal circulation from around the headland came in and stayed. She had learned the cove's habits in her second year and added it to the survey route in her third. In eight years she had found in it, among other things, a salmon net from a farm forty miles up the bay, a fuel drum still three-quarters full, and two sections of the old outer marker that the Navy had replaced and apparently lost track of. She expected gear.

The dinghy was not gear.

She saw it before she was within fifty yards: a white hull, discolored, the shape unfamiliar. She brought the launch down to drift and worked in toward the cove's north end. The dinghy had come to rest bow-on to the granite, the bow caught on a ledge a foot above the current waterline with the stern swung around on the ebb. Its freeboard on the port side was low — the hull was down amidships where something had gone wrong with it. She put the launch alongside the rock and looked.

Twelve feet, twelve and a half at most. A pulling boat with a flat bottom, the kind built for a sheltered harbor and nothing past the heads. The damage was to the port side amidships: planking crushed inward, the frames behind it broken, the seam at the garboard open on both sides of the break. The hull had taken a hard blow or had been ground hard against something with weight behind it. Below the damaged section, the boat had shipped water enough to settle it. Above the damage, the hull cleared the surface, held up by the bow on its ledge.

She put her hand on the rock and steadied the launch against it and looked at the waterline.

The weed was the record. It grew at a predictable rate on a hull left in one place; it stopped at the surface; it did not lie about the time elapsed. The line of growth on this hull ran six inches above the cove's present waterline, which meant the hull had come to rest here when the tide was running six inches higher than today's ebb — a normal tidal variation, nothing unusual. The barnacle growth started below the weed line and went down into the murk. She estimated ten months. Maybe eleven. Late autumn or early winter, which put the arrival somewhere in the months after the October blow.

She shipped the oars and stepped onto the rock.

---

The bow ring was brass, tarnished to near-black, but solid. She got both hands through it and pulled back, working her grip down toward the waterline, bracing her feet against a lower ledge. The hull was heavy — waterlogged through and through, the planking saturated, the broken section adding to the weight by holding water in the bilge. She set her weight against it and worked it up the rock in stages until the transom lifted clear of the water.

She held it there and looked at the transom.

The paint was gone. Where it had been heaviest it had left a primer stain on the wood; where it had been thin it was down to bare grain. There had been a name painted on the board, centered, in large letters and a dark color. Most of it was unreadable. The middle section — the heart of the letters, where the wood had worked most through the wetting and drying of eleven months — had come down to bare board. What remained was at the right end of the painted surface: four characters at the edge of where the name had ended. *STEN,* in the trace of a pigment that had held on a little better than the rest.

That was all the name had left.

She held the bow up with one hand and got the log out of her coat pocket with the other. Below the remnant of the name, set into the lower edge of the transom on a rectangular plate, was the registration. The bronze had gone green at the edges but the center was still readable: the Portland Harbor Authority's district and vessel number, stamped in standard format, the registration year clear at the right. She wrote the number in the log against the date and the position.

She let the bow down. The dinghy swung back on its ledge.

She spent another minute on the hull: looking at the damage, reading the angles of the broken frames. A rock in the cove could have done it, or a dock edge, or a piling in a blow. She could not say what had made the wound or when, except that it was at least as old as the growth on the waterline, which meant it had happened before the hull came to rest here or at the same time. She could not say more than that.

She stepped back to the launch and rowed out of the cove.

---

She wrote up the outer cove survey in the afternoon. Six items: the two Heron Point pots, the Seal Neck hawser, the recovered mooring float, the old fish net, and the dinghy. For the dinghy she noted the position, the condition, the estimated time the hull had been in place, and the registration number, and flagged the entry for a Portland inquiry.

The inquiry form was standard. She had submitted three in eight years, all for vessels that had come adrift and ended up where they did not belong. Registration number, found position, condition on discovery, date. She was the reporting officer; she signed her name. She addressed the envelope to the Portland Harbor Authority, Commercial Street, and put it in the outgoing tray.

Portland kept good records. She would have a reply within the week.

---

She did not think much about the dinghy in the three days between sending the letter and receiving the reply. The survey's other items wanted attention: the mooring float needed a return notation against the spring record; the boatyard needed the authorization for the autumn railway haul-out, which she had been building since August and could finalize now that the season's last draggers had scheduled their maintenance dates; the winter mooring allocation list wanted revision now that two of the summer vessels had declared they were laying up in Portland rather than Coldwater. The harbor did not stop.

The reply came Thursday. One page, the Portland Harbor Authority heading, a brief paragraph below it.

She read it.

The registration number submitted belonged to a chandlery on Commercial Street, Portland, operating a rental service for small harbor craft — dinghies and similar vessels, civilian use. The registered vessel had last been carried in the active rental fleet as of late September 1943. At that time it had been rented and not returned. The chandlery's record listed the matter as unresolved. The last rental entry showed the vessel released on the twenty-eighth of September, 1943.

The customer at time of rental had provided a name and an address.

*W. Grau, 114 Franklin Street, Portland, Maine.*

---

She read it twice.

W. Grau. She turned the paper over as if the back would offer something, which it did not. The initial was nothing by itself. The last name was nothing she recognized: not a Coldwater name, not a harbor name, not a name from any registration or manifest she had handled in eight years at this post. Portland was sixty miles south. Portland men worked the outer grounds; they had business everywhere on the coast.

But a rental dinghy out of Portland on the twenty-eighth of September, 1943.

She did not need to calculate. The sequence was there: the dinghy went out from Portland at the end of September; the three-day blow ran the ninth through the eleventh of October; the body arrived on the breakwater on the morning of the twelfth; the dinghy came to rest in the south cove in the months after. The middle was not visible. The two ends were visible and they pointed toward each other.

She did not write any of this in the log.

She opened the middle desk drawer.

The CG form was as she had left it: Mercer's case number at the top, the date October 1943, the blank for identifying information still blank, the blank for additional observations below her final logged entry still empty.

She set the Portland letter on top of the CG form and looked at them together.

W. Grau, 114 Franklin Street, Portland. The name on the rental form was not necessarily the name of the man who had used the boat; a man could give any name at a chandler's counter, and a man with a reason for one name had a reason for another. But a man with a reason gave the name he gave, and the name he gave went into the record, and the record was in her desk drawer now alongside the unanswered form.

The column without a label had more in it now.

---

She closed the drawer.

The harbor was quiet in September. The offshore fleet had been coming in since the first of the month — the season declaring itself over in the way it always did, gradually and then at once. The boatyard was on maintenance schedule. The cold-storage house was running reduced hours. The summer traffic was done and the winter's rhythm had not yet started, and between those two states the harbor had its annual brief stillness, a week or two when the pier held fewer boats than at any other time of year and the water in the channel was flat and clear.

She looked at the desk: the open log, the fuel ledger, the stacked forms in the outgoing tray for the mail boat. The middle drawer was closed.

She had a name. The name had an address. The address was in Portland, which was a city with a post office and a record of residents, and a harbormaster who knew what a harbormaster knew. She was not certain it made anything possible except what she was already doing, which was holding pieces and waiting for them to accumulate into something that could be entered in the correct column.

She turned back to the log.

The channel held the September light. The mooring chains ran straight down to their blocks with no motion in them — full slack at this hour, the fleet riding easy. The two outer buoys were in their positions at the harbor mouth, the red to port and the green to starboard, visible from the pier head window where she had watched them through eight years of whatever the harbor had brought.

She wrote the afternoon's log. She wrote it correctly.

The drawer stayed closed.
