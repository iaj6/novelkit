# May

The route to school: three years and two months of it now, the same four blocks from Spruce Street to the post office corner and the turn north past the Congregational church to the brick building on the main road. He knew each house he passed by its season — which gardens had been turned over since the thaw and which had not, which put light in their windows at seven in the morning, which did not. The Garfield house at the corner had the kitchen garden already in motion: stakes in the ground, the earth turned dark, the row markers placed. He had learned that certain households acted in April and certain households waited, and the Garfields were a household that acted. The family next door had a cat that had established, over three years, that Marcus constituted no reasonable occasion for alarm, and confirmed this from the fence post as he passed.

The Henderson family's dog had made the same determination approximately six months into Marcus's tenure, which remained, in his estimation, among the small achievements of his residence here. It lifted its head from the step, confirmed, and set it back down.

The lilacs along the Miller fence were four or five days from opening. He had been watching them since the end of the previous week — the buds advancing through their green toward the first color, still held back by what April in this latitude permitted. He was familiar now with what spring looked like here: the crocuses first, then the mud, then the particular quality of the light, then the lilacs. He had been in Coldwater for three springs. He had thought, at the beginning of each of them, that he would be leaving before the next one. He had been wrong about this three times, and was no longer forming the thought.

He looked back once at the turn: Lillian was at the kitchen window of the Crow house, fourth from the corner. She lifted a hand; he lifted his. She had been at that window, at approximately this time, for most of three years. He did not know when this had become the texture of the ordinary.

---

Before the road turned south there was a short street that ran toward the harbor approach, and at its end the outer reach was briefly visible between the houses — perhaps a hundred yards of sight line before the angle closed. The water had the quality late April gave it: not the flat steel of November, not the heavy stillness of January, but something that held light at its surface rather than absorbing it, clear and directed. The breakwater was a gray horizontal line across the harbor mouth. He turned before it was more than glimpsed, and it was gone.

---

He had been thinking, in recent weeks, about Werner Grau.

Not in the way he had thought about him during the first year, when each piece had required locating within the structure of what was known and what remained provisional, when the question of what to do with the assembled account was still a question in progress — but in the way he now thought about matters that had been correctly placed: without needing to disturb them.

He held what he was likely always to hold. The letter — *Lieber Albrecht* — arriving in October 1943 in his school mail because a postal clerk in the county sorting office had made a plausible guess at the address and been wrong. The plan to come to Coldwater in October; the news about the Reinmar boy at the Pfeiffer farm; the information Werner would not commit to paper and wanted to deliver in person. Marcus understood something of that restraint, or believed he did: certain things require the face across a table, the particular register of two men from the same region of Bavaria hearing each other in a language that does not require translation, in a country that belongs to neither of them. And then the face Albrecht Pfeiffer had shown when Marcus came to the farm door in January 1944: not surprise but the adjustment of a man incorporating a confirmation into something already established. Albrecht had already known Werner had come to Coldwater. This was what his face had said, precisely, without saying anything.

Karl Reinmar, from Ansbach — fifty kilometers from where Albrecht Pfeiffer had been born, from where Werner had come. The note Karl had written before the Army transport, which Hannah had read aloud on Marcus's desk in January, smoothing it flat with her hand: *Werner Grau. From Ansbach. He is dead. I am sorry.* The photograph in the Pfeiffer tin box: two men before a stone building, Bamberg, 1921 — Werner with his painter's and laborer's shoulders, the face Hannah had described as ordinary and impossible to fix in memory. And then Albrecht's reply, that autumn, to his daughter's past tense: *Ich weiß.* I know.

Werner Grau had come to Coldwater in October 1943, in deteriorating weather, in a small boat from Portland, carrying news he had been holding for months — about Karl, about the Reinmar family, about things the letter had named only as things that would not go to paper. He had been in the country without legal standing since 1940. He had come because the connection he was seeking had become more pressing than the risk: a man who has been carrying something across that long a distance will eventually put it down, in person, with the person who can receive it. Whether Werner had reached the farm that night, what had passed between him and Albrecht before the storm peaked, what brought Werner to the harbor and what he encountered there — this was not in the account and would not be. Albrecht had said *Ich weiß* and picked up his fork.

Marcus was not going to report it. He had decided this in the spring of 1944, and the decision had not shifted. To complete the form and file it would be to deliver Werner Grau to the category available: undocumented alien, probable drowning, case long closed. This was accurate so far as it went. Werner's name would acquire an addendum in a file that had been closed for a year and a half, and the file would close again. Marcus knew when a form served a purpose and when it did not. The account was in the drawer, written in German, in his own hand. Werner Grau had existed; he had been, in specific and considerable detail, a person; his story was not entirely lost.

---

He said good morning to Mrs. Voisin at her step, and to the Kelleher boy walking his bicycle up the road with the resigned expression of someone who had concluded that the front tire was committed to its own position. He was at the school at ten past eight. The corridor: chalk dust, old wood, the compound the custodians used on the floors, which he knew now as instinctively as he had once known the steam heat and old paper of his department in Vienna. Henry would have had the boiler running from six. The radiators ticked.

He passed the service placard in the main corridor without stopping. Twenty-four names: J. CROW, Army, Pacific, between Cowan J. (Navy) and Delaney T. (Marines, Confirmed Fallen August 1944). It had been there since March of 1944 — since the week after Henry had said *the principal may not have thought*, which had been the week before Marcus had taken the question to the principal's office — and it was there now, as it would continue to be, without requiring his attention each time.

His classroom, second floor, east-facing. He unlocked the door — the key's brass worn to a particular smoothness after two years of the same hand and the same pressure — and set his briefcase on the desk. The morning light lay in the windows, doing what April light did to old painted walls.

---

He had brought the stone from his room at the Crow house in January, at the beginning of the new semester, and placed it on the classroom windowsill between the latch and the corner of the frame. It was a piece of granite from the breakwater's elbow — small enough to close one hand around entirely, unremarkable in the way of coastal Maine granite: gray, close-grained, slightly heavier than its size suggested. He had picked it up on the twelfth of October, 1944, standing at the place where the body had been found a year before, and had carried it home and then brought it here, because it had seemed then like the right thing to do and had not since seemed otherwise. He picked it up now, felt its familiar weight in his palm, and set it back down. It was a piece of coastal Maine granite from a place where a man had died, that wanted to be on the windowsill, and was.

He went to the window and opened it.

The spring came in: salt from the harbor, cold mud from the south fields, and from the Bartlett property next door, the lilacs nearly open — not yet the full smell but the note that preceded it, which was its own distinct thing and not a lesser one.

The morning was quiet enough to hear the bell buoy at the channel entrance. It rang on the swell: twice, at irregular intervals, each ring distinct.

In approximately two weeks there would be news from Europe. He was not waiting for it with any particular impatience. It would come; the war would end; and he would be here, in this room, in this building on the main road in this town.

Hannah Pfeiffer's translation papers were on the desk in a stack of eleven: her working draft of the Rilke sequence Marcus had assigned to the third-year class in March, with marginal notes in both German and English. The handwriting was careful and slightly impatient, the quality of someone who is not uncertain about the word and is nonetheless holding it up to the light. She had understood, without being instructed, that what a word held in German could only be approximated in English, and that the approximation was the work itself, not a failure of it. She had been his student for nearly two years. He had something to tell her about the Rilke — about what she was doing with it, specifically — and was waiting for the translation that would show her this herself, because that was the better way.

He sat down.

Outside the open window: salt, cold mud, the lilacs at their threshold. The bell buoy, twice, in the morning quiet.

The stone on the sill.

He took out his pen and began to mark.
