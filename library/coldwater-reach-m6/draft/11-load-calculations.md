# Chapter Eleven — Load Calculations

She was three entries into the December fuel receipts when the door opened. The man said his name — Tarbell, Amos Tarbell — and the county harbor authority, and that he was here for the routine inspection.

Eira put down her pen. "Harbormaster Bowman," she said. "I've been expecting someone from the county."

He had a clipboard with a county authority form already clipped to the board and a brown wool overcoat that had been brushed clean. Mid-thirties. His politeness had the quality of a specific instruction carefully followed.

"How long have you held this position?" he asked.

"Eight years."

"Prior experience in harbor operations?"

"Assistant harbormaster. Under the previous harbormaster." She let him write. "Owen Bowman. My husband."

He looked up once. Then he looked back at his clipboard.

"How long as assistant?"

"Four years."

"You currently have no assistant of your own."

"Since January 1942. He enlisted." She kept her voice even. "The county authority hasn't authorized a replacement. I've managed without one."

He wrote something on his form. She could not read it from across the desk.

She had laid the winter documents out on the table before he came: the mooring registry open to the current page, the fuel allocation ledger beside it, the vessel registration file beneath. The safety inspection folder was on the shelf; she would take it down when they reached it.

She walked him through the mooring registry. Each vessel currently on a winter mooring in the inner harbor: the lobster boats Ida May, Sadie H., and Clara B., and three others on the west float; the dragger East Wind; the cold storage skiff; the Ellsworth against the east float on her winter berth. Seventeen vessels in total. She named each one, its registration status, its current mooring position. He checked them against his form. He did not find a discrepancy, because there was not one.

The fuel allocation ledger was next. Wartime supplemental allocation for harbor operations: every vessel drawing against the harbor's quarterly allotment from the Department of the Interior was entered by vessel name, date, and quantity in gallons, each entry initialed. The column balanced to the gallon.

"The authorization document?" Tarbell asked.

She took it from the folder beneath the ledger. He read through it. He handed it back without comment.

They went outside.

February cold, flat gray sky, the harbor on an ebbing tide. The dragger that worked the offshore grounds had gone out at first light and would not be back until afternoon. The Ellsworth sat against the east float with her forward lines gone slack in the ebb. The compressor at the cold storage house ran in a steady cycle. The inner harbor water was undisturbed except where the tide drew over the submerged ledge at the harbor mouth.

Tarbell walked the length of the pier to the breakwater end. She walked beside him. He asked about the depth at the elbow at mean low water, the height of the inner float at mean high water, the fuel storage configuration. He asked whether the tank was inside the hundred-foot setback from the waterline.

"Inside," she said. "By eight feet. The wartime exception was filed in June of 1942."

"Section four."

"Subsection B."

He wrote it down. At the pier head he stopped and looked at the harbor for a moment without writing anything. She stood beside him and looked at the same water. The sky was low and the gray ran without break from the cloud to the harbor surface.

"Everything here appears to be in order," he said.

"Yes," she said.

They went back inside.

The vessel registration file came next, then the safety inspection folder. Forty-two active vessel registrations, all current, no lapses, no outstanding renewals. The safety inspections for the winter-berthed vessels complete; the summer boats scheduled for spring before the season opened, the schedule printed on the folder's back page. He read the schedule. He made a note. His form was nearly full.

Tarbell sat down across the desk. He set his pencil on the clipboard.

"I want to ask you about the formal basis of your appointment," he said. "The — transitional nature of it."

She opened the bottom drawer and took out a folder she had kept there since 1935. Inside was a single typed letter on county authority letterhead. She set it on the table in front of him.

"November 21, 1935," she said. "Signed by Director Pearce. It appoints me harbormaster of Coldwater Reach pending formal review by the county harbor authority board."

Tarbell picked up the letter. He read it. He had the look of a man reading something he had already been briefed on — the eyes moving at the speed of confirmation.

"This review," he said. "It was scheduled?"

"December 1935. Then rescheduled. Then not rescheduled again."

"You never pressed for it."

"I did the work," she said. "The review would have confirmed what the work showed. I took it to be the board's matter to pursue."

He turned the letter over. The back was blank. He set it face up.

"You might have requested formal appointment on your own initiative," he said.

"I might have," she said. "What I did instead was manage the harbor."

She let that sit. Outside, the compressor cycled off and ran. The last of the ebb moved through the pilings.

"There is one more thing I'd like to show you," she said.

She went to the north shelf and took down the harbor log for 1935. Green board cover, the year inked on the spine in Owen's hand — a forward-leaning cursive with compressed vowels and the particular t-crossings that ran slightly below the stalk. The log she had opened more times than she had ever counted, for reasons she did not examine. She set it on the desk and opened it to November.

November 7. *Barometer 29.4, NE backing N, Beaufort 5 outer harbor. Doubled all moorings afternoon watch. Advised small craft haul 1600. E. Anderson dory on A-ring 1645. Harbor secure 1800.* Owen's entries were that way: terse, accurate, complete. She had been reading them for eight years and could still hear his attention in the abbreviations — the way a man writes when he is doing the actual work and the writing is the record of the work, not a separate act.

November 8 was blank.

November 9 was hers. *Barometer 29.2, NW 8, seas 4–6 outer harbor, abating. O. Bowman, harbormaster, missing evening 7 November. Search conducted CG Sprucehead and harbor tender. Body not yet recovered. Harbor open. All moorings secure.* Her own hand, smaller and more vertical than his.

"A continuity of record," she said. "Unbroken."

Tarbell looked at both entries for longer than he had looked at anything else in the shed. He read November 7 and then November 9. He looked at the blank November 8 in between them. He set his pencil down flat on the clipboard.

"November 7 to November 9," he said.

"Forty hours."

He picked up the pencil and wrote something on his form. It was the longest entry he had made all morning. She did not try to read it.

There was a pause. The cold storage compressor ran outside. The harbor was quiet the way it was always quiet in February — the boats that were left had already found their winter arrangement; the pier held only the sounds of the tide.

"I want to tell you," Tarbell said, "that there are some parties on the county board who have expressed concerns. About the long-term stability of the arrangement."

"Stability," she said.

"The formal basis of your appointment." He stopped. He started again. "They believe the matter of formal confirmation should be addressed."

"What would constitute a satisfactory confirmation?" she asked. "In the board's view."

He turned the pencil between his fingers. He was choosing words. He chose them and they did not constitute a direct answer.

"The board's preference," he said, "is that the appointment be placed on a formally established footing."

"What process would satisfy the board?"

He looked at the county authority letter on the desk. He looked at his clipboard. He looked at the window.

He did not answer.

She gave him one more moment. He did not fill it.

"I see," she said.

Tarbell gathered his clipboard and reached for the county authority letter. He asked whether he could take it to copy for his file; she said yes, and asked him to return it when he was done. He put it in his briefcase. He stood. He said he would file his report with the county authority office by the end of the week. He thanked her for her time.

"Thank you for coming," she said.

She walked him to the door. She stood in the doorway and watched him cross the pier and turn up the road toward town. The tide had turned; the Ellsworth's forward lines had gone taut again. Twenty past eleven.

Inside, she put the 1935 harbor log back on the north shelf. She returned the fuel ledger and the vessel registration file and the safety inspection folder to their places. She straightened the table.

Then she put the kettle on.

The harbor lay in February quiet: the dragger out, the Ellsworth cold at the east float, the lobster boats on their moorings with their bow lines leading east into the turned current. Ice on the float cleats, thin enough to break with a boot. The cold storage house ran steady. The sky was flat and would not change before dark.

She looked at the north shelf. The harbor logs ran left to right across it — the years in Owen's hand, then the 1935 volume with both their hands in it, then hers from 1936 on. Eight spines in a row, identical green board.

The water came to the boil. She poured her cup and went back to the December fuel receipts.
