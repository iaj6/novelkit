# Werner Grau

He laid the papers in order before he sat down.

The letter first: W. Grau to A. Pfeiffer, September 28, 1943, in the original envelope — the delivery error penciled at the upper left in a postal clerk's hand, not his. He positioned it at the left of the available space, which was how he organized working materials, and smoothed the crease with his thumb. Beside it, to the right: Sondheimer's reply — one page, typed, the Columbia letterhead at the top, dated February 28, returned here in the second week of March. And in the center, the sheet of school composition paper he had been adding to since December: five items written out in ink at different times, the last dated March 6, and below the fifth item a space he had been leaving for the concluding sentence.

He sat. The rain had been coming down since before he woke — the steady, considered rain of Maine in May, not February's cold driving kind but something more deliberate, as though the season had decided to be thorough about it. Henry had brought the porch rocker inside the night before. Lillian was in the kitchen, two floors below, the sound of her muffled and occasional. Otherwise the house was quiet. It was Sunday.

He read the letter again, though he did not need to. Werner Grau had written it on lined notepaper in the vertical German hand that Marcus recognized as belonging to men of a certain education and a certain generation — the same penmanship taught at the gymnasium in Vienna, though Werner's was slightly looser, less attended to, the hand of a man who wrote for communication rather than for record. *Lieber Albrecht.* The salutation of an old friendship, long enough that ceremony would have been an insult. He had written from Portland, and the return address was a street number and nothing else — no district, no institution. He had been planning to come to Coldwater in October. There were things he could not commit to paper, which was why he was making the journey in person. He had news of the Reinmars, whom he had been carrying for months and would deliver himself. *Ihr alter Freund, Werner Grau.*

The Sondheimer reply began with what the records confirmed: entry at Boston, March 1936, arts-organization visa sponsored by the Boston Alliance for the Arts, a cultural exchange program for painters and printmakers whose funding had redirected the following year. Renewed once, in March 1937. Expired March 1940. After the expiry, the record had three fields where information might exist — renewal, deportation proceeding, departure — and in each field the same notation: *no record.* Sondheimer had added, at the close, that the records were incomplete, which was a professional courtesy and also a precise observation.

Marcus looked at his own sheet.

The five items had been written at different times; the ink varied from entry to entry because he had not used the same pen. Item one: *W. Grau — German national, born approximately 1890–1900 (inference: letter's tone; A. Pfeiffer's approximate age as peer; quality of handwriting — a man in his middle years, educated).* Item two: *In the United States, March 1936 to October 1943. Legal standing: valid through March 1940. After: no record of any kind. Duration without standing: approximately three and a half years.* Item three: *Occupation: painter and printmaker (Boston Alliance for the Arts, cultural exchange program, 1936). Last known address: Portland, Maine — street number only, no institution.* Item four: *Known associations: A. Pfeiffer, Bamberg, before 1922; Reinmar family, Ansbach (reference in letter to 'Reinmars Junge' — Karl Reinmar, nineteen, currently at Pfeiffer farm under the federal POW labor program, Camp Houlton satellite).* Item five, added March 6: *No legal standing from spring 1940 onward. No record thereafter of any kind.*

And below: the blank space.

---

He had decided that morning, when he woke, that he would write the concluding paragraph — not because new evidence had arrived, but because the evidence he held was not going to change, and the space had been waiting long enough to have become itself a kind of position. He had learned at the university that an unfinished argument was worse than a precisely imprecise one, provided the imprecision was named. He would name it.

He wrote at the top of the remaining space: *Plausible account:* He wrote it as a heading, because what followed was an inference, not a finding, and the distinction mattered to him.

*W. Grau rented or borrowed a vessel in Portland — a small boat, capable of coastal travel in fair conditions. He traveled north along the coast to Coldwater Reach, arriving on the night of October 11–12, 1943. He was on the south waterfront at some point during that night. He attempted to locate A. Pfeiffer, or located him. Before the storm's peak on the morning of October 12, he died — by drowning, by cardiac event, by the storm, by some combination of these things.*

He stopped. He read what he had written. Then he drew a careful circle around *by some combination of these things* — not to mark it for revision but because it was the account's honest center, the claim the evidence would not close over. The medical finding he had heard in October, second-hand through two conversations at the school: no water in the lungs. Which meant the storm had been the occasion but not necessarily the cause. What had preceded the storm — what Werner Grau had been doing on the south waterfront, whom he had met or not met, what physical condition he had been in when the weather came on — was not in any document Marcus held. What Albrecht Pfeiffer's face had shown him, across a cold farmyard in January, was recognition without surprise — which meant Albrecht had known Werner came to Coldwater, had perhaps seen him, had perhaps been the last person to see him. What had passed between them on the night of October 11, if anything passed between them, Marcus did not have.

He circled *by some combination of these things* and left it.

---

Werner Grau in 1940 had been, by the law of the country in which he was living, what Marcus Klein had been in 1940 by the law of a different one: a German-category national without valid status in a country that considered itself at war, or shortly would, with the country named on his papers. The category had been applied to both of them on the same grounds — not the politics of either man, not his conduct or his intentions, but the accident of the nation in which he had been born and the timing of his departure from it. Whether the man had left under duress; what he had left behind; what he thought about what the nation on his papers was doing in his name — these had not been the relevant questions. The category was the category.

In Canada, the category had produced an internment camp at Sherbrooke, Quebec: approximately ten months, the administration correct in the main, the library inadequate, the company of approximately thirty other men in similar circumstances, the Emergency Committee placement in Cambridge that had come through in 1941 as it might not have come through in 1942. He had lost a year. He had lost access to his books, his correspondence, his professional record, and the ordinary continuity of a working life. He had come out with those losses and a particular watchfulness about paperwork that had not entirely left him.

In Portland, the same category had apparently produced something different. Werner had been painting, or working, or doing whatever a man without legal standing does in a port city large enough for a marginal economy to absorb him — painting, renting a room, keeping an address on a street with no institutional affiliation. He had not been found, or he had been found and tolerated, or he had been, for three and a half years, invisible in the specific way such economies make people invisible. He had been alive and planning a journey in September 1943.

The difference between them was not character. Not capacity, not intention, not the content of any file any authority had ever compiled on either of them.

It was, partly, the country. Canada had interned; America — or this corner of America — had looked away. And it was, more than partly, luck, a category Marcus had spent a number of years trying to name accurately: without the false comfort of fate and without the false precision of something that could have been otherwise if he had simply been more careful.

He knew this, and had known it since March. He held it at that.

---

The account required things of him that he could not do.

He could not bring it to the Coast Guard station at Sprucehead. The CG's record on the body was, as far as he knew, still open — no papers, no identification, no next of kin, case status unresolved. A man arriving at Sprucehead with a name to attach to that record would also be arriving, necessarily, with an immigration status: no legal standing for three and a half years at the time of death. This would not remain confined to Werner Grau. Albrecht Pfeiffer had been on the south waterfront the same night, or Marcus had strong grounds for believing so; any inquiry beginning with Werner Grau's name would not stop at Werner Grau. Karl Reinmar was at the Pfeiffer farm under an arrangement that depended on the smooth administration of federal programs that did not benefit from additional scrutiny.

He could not bring it to anyone official. He was not, himself, fully official — his appointment had been authorized under wartime provisions and renewed twice by the school board; his own papers were in order; but *official,* as a designation for the kind of standing that would give such a delivery any weight, was a word he had learned to apply to himself with care.

He would not suppress it. This was also a fact about his situation, though of a different kind — a constraint of a different order than the ones above, which were practical, and this one was not. Suppression required performing, for some imagined audience, a version of himself who had not assembled these five items and their plausible conclusion, who did not hold the account he held. That performance was not available to him. He held the account; the account was accurate; he would hold it accurately. That was the verb he had.

Whether this constituted doing something — whether it constituted not doing something — whether the distinction had operational meaning from inside his particular position — this was a question he had been sitting with since January, and he had concluded, provisionally, that it was not a question he could answer from where he stood. He left it circled, alongside the cause of Werner Grau's death, marked present and unclosed.

---

He stacked the documents: letter, Sondheimer's reply, the sheet of school paper. He folded the Sondheimer reply in thirds, as it had arrived. He folded the sheet in half on the horizontal axis and then once more, so it came to roughly the letter's dimensions, and tucked it inside the envelope with the reply. The letter to Albrecht Pfeiffer he returned to its original envelope, which he resealed with a strip of paper and paste — not because he expected anyone to open the drawer, but because the envelope was the document's proper housing and the document deserved its proper housing.

He opened the bottom drawer. The Latin dictionary had been resting on top of the folder since October, when he had placed it there because the dictionary's weight kept the papers flat and because the Latin dictionary was the thing most like a private object he owned in this room; it had seemed correct that it should be the one to lie over the rest. He put the new envelope alongside the earlier materials, arranged everything so it lay flat, and set the Latin dictionary back on top.

He locked the drawer and put the key in the pocket of his coat, which was on the hook by the door.

---

Outside, the rain was unchanged. He put on his coat and hat and went down through the back hall and out past the entrance to Henry's boiler room, where the mop handle Henry had propped against the wall three days ago still leaned, apparently permanent now. Around the side of the house to the street.

The street was quiet with the Sunday-morning quiet that a small town in rain takes on — traffic in, doors closed, the surface of things reduced to its own sound. The maple at the Gifford corner had leafed out in the early way of May, the green still light and tentative, not yet committed to summer. He had been walking this street three or four times a day since March 1942; he knew it in every weather now, knew the way the gravel at the road edge shifted after frost, the way the Gifford daffodils came and went in the second week of April.

He walked to the north end where the street met the road that went inland, toward the drumlins and the hay fields, the direction of the Pfeiffer farm. He stood where the pavement gave onto the dirt section and looked at the road going out into the flat country. The rain darkened its surface evenly. After a moment he turned and walked back, the full length of the street, past the house and past the maple, to the south end where the road bent toward the main road and from there down to the waterfront. Nine minutes from Henry's front door to the pier head — he had measured it in the first week, as he measured all distances in a new place. He could hear the harbor from here: a mooring chain, something working the outer basin, the particular patience of water against wood.

He stood at the bend.

Then he walked back.

Upstairs, he sat at the desk with the papers gone. The window gave onto the back of the house, not the harbor, but the harbor smell came through regardless — three streets off, window closed, the salt and diesel that was the specific smell of this air, in this place, on a Sunday in May. He had been aware of it since his first week here, when it had seemed one foreign element among a landscape composed entirely of foreign elements. He was aware of it now in a different register: not foreign, not local exactly, but as the smell this air had, here, with the rain coming down and the drawer locked and Werner Grau in it.
