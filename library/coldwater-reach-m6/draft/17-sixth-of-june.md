# The Sixth of June

The radio was on downstairs.

She heard it before she heard him — the sound coming up through the boards as a man's voice, steady and formal, saying something she couldn't separate into words from the top of the stairs. Then underneath it her father's voice, low, not calling her.

She came down in her socks, holding the banister in the dark.

The kitchen was lit only by the orange of the radio dial and the first gray beginning at the window above the sink. The clock on the shelf read ten past four. Her father was standing at the table with his barn coat over the chair behind him, hands at his sides. He had been there for some time. She could tell by the particular settled quality of his stillness — the way a person stands when they have already done the hard work of arriving at a thing and are now simply inside it.

She stood by the cold stove and listened.

*—Allied forces have landed on the coast of northern France, in the region known as Normandy—*

Her father did not look at her. The voice on the radio was deliberate, each sentence placed as if placement were a form of accuracy. She tried to find Normandy on the wall map Mr. Klein kept in the classroom — a physical geography map of Europe, mountain ranges rendered as hatch marks — and she could find France in her mind generally but could not find the northern coast of it, could not locate the region.

*—General Eisenhower has confirmed that operations are continuing and that Allied forces have established positions along multiple beach approaches—*

The signal dropped for a moment and came back at a different pitch. Another voice, lower, reading something that sounded like an official statement. *Beachhead. Sustained operations. Allied forces.* She was hearing the shapes of words rather than their meaning.

He had not made coffee. She looked at his hands on the table and did not ask.

---

Her mother came down at twenty past four. Hannah heard the careful step on the stairs — her mother's way of walking when she was tired or deliberate — and then Magda was in the doorway, in her robe, hair loose over her shoulders. She stood looking at the radio.

She did not come into the kitchen.

The signal had shifted; it was speaking from London now, someone said. It came in slightly cleaner before full dawn, the way foreign signals sometimes did at that hour.

Hannah made coffee. She knew where they stood with the ration book; she had checked two days before. She decided it was worth it. The match smell, the soft sound of the gas, the small flame catching. She stood by the stove and waited and looked at her mother in the doorway. Her mother was watching the radio itself, not her father — the cabinet, as if the signal were visible inside it.

When the coffee was ready she poured two cups. She set one at her father's place. He looked at it for a moment and then picked it up with both hands. She brought the second to the doorway and her mother took it, both hands around the cup, and held it without drinking.

They listened.

The voice named towns. It named a number of ships in a tone that meant the number was very large. The word *paratroopers.* The phrase *naval bombardment* said in the same even register as everything else. She listened for her father's face to change and it did not change in any way she could name. He had shifted his hands; they were flat on the table now, weight forward. He knows where Normandy is. He can hear those town names and put them on the coast. She watched his face for the look of a person tracking something through space and she thought she could see it in the set of his jaw, the forward lean.

The clock moved from four-thirty to five. The June light came in fast — the barn materialized out of the dark, then the fence posts along the east field, then the hay fields themselves in the early flat gray. Birds started outside, a few at first and then more of them, and by five minutes before five it was a full sound with the radio running underneath it.

She made herself useful. She set the oatmeal to soak for when they would want it. She straightened the dish towels that did not need straightening. Her mother had not moved from her chair; her father had not moved from the table. The radio continued — a fourth voice now, a correspondent somewhere, saying something about the men who had gone in before dawn, the ones who had gone by parachute into the dark.

She tried to make the distance real and could not. The voice was coming out of the BC-1206 the same way it always came out, filling the kitchen the same way the weather report filled it, and somewhere behind the voice there were men coming off boats onto a beach in France and she could not picture the beach.

Her father said, in English: "It will be over soon."

He said it to the room. Not to her, not to her mother — to the kitchen, to the radio, to the window where the June morning was showing itself. She had not heard a full English sentence from him since — she searched backward, through months — since January. Maybe longer. His English had not gone away; she had never believed it had. It was still in him the way a radio signal was always traveling even when the set was off.

Not relief exactly. She was watching his face and there was nothing in it that looked like the American kind, the easy kind, the kind that made you exhale loudly or put your hand over your eyes. Something else: the look of a man who has been watching for a specific thing to begin, and believes it has begun.

Her mother moved from the doorway.

One step onto the linoleum. Then another. She crossed to the table in three steps and she pulled out the chair and she sat. She folded both hands around the cup. She looked at the tabletop.

She did not speak. She was in the kitchen. She was at the table, which was not the doorway, and Hannah did not look at it too long.

*Erleichterung.* She had the German and not the English for it, because German was the language she had been made in, and the German word held the shape of what it named. Not relief — that word was too clean, too finished at its edges. More like: a weight becoming less heavy. Something that has been pressing, without stopping, for a very long time — longer than she had understood the full shape of it — beginning, on this specific morning in June, to ease by one small degree.

She did not say the word.

The radio went on.

An hour later her father put on his barn coat and boots and went out. The screen door. Hannah washed the cups. Her mother stayed at the table, hands folded, not sleeping, not reading, just there in the new morning light. After a while Magda reached over and turned the radio down one notch — not off, just quieter — and Hannah left her there.

She milked. She fed. She hauled water and swept the milking parlor and split a quarter-cord of kindling from the pile behind the barn that had been waiting two weeks for someone to get to it. The work went the way the work always went: the weight of the pails, the patience of the cows, the rhythm of the axe in the June morning. Somewhere in the middle of it, there were kitchens all up the road where people were having the versions of these hours that belonged to them — people with sons on those beaches, people who could follow the town names on the coast. She didn't have any of that. She had her father's hands flat on the table and the sentence he had said into the kitchen with no one in particular to hear it.

---

That afternoon the radio was everywhere in town. She heard it from the diner when she stopped the truck at the intersection for the feed run — someone had turned the set up high and the voice came through the screen door and across the sidewalk. At the post office two women stood in the open doorway with their arms folded against their chests, listening to the set inside. Three men on the sidewalk in front of the hardware store, standing in the June sun, not talking to each other.

She loaded the feed and drove back on the south road. The afternoon had come in with the first real summer warmth — the kind that settles in the open fields and won't move until after supper, the kind that means the season has made up its mind. The fields on the south side of the road were deep green and tall. She was thinking about their hay. The south field had been ready for two weeks; her father would cut it this week if the weather held.

At the farm, Lonzo had his portable radio on the icehouse step — a small set with a black handle, volume turned up enough to carry inside. She could hear it as she crossed the yard with the first water bucket, the afternoon broadcast, the same careful voices. The door was propped open. She heard Lonzo's chair shift inside.

She put the bucket down and went back for the second.

---

Karl was on the step.

He had come outside while she was in the barn. He was sitting with his elbows on his knees, looking at the fence line and the hay field beyond it. The hay was at its full summer height — she had been watching it get tall for two weeks — silver-green in the afternoon light, moving sometimes in the way tall grass moves when there is no particular wind, the stalks making small adjustments to their own weight. His forearms were tanned from three weeks of fieldwork. He was not looking at her.

She had been going to walk past to the pump.

She stopped.

Four or five yards from the step. Lonzo was inside; she could hear the radio still going, hear his chair. Her father's sentence had been in her head since five that morning. It had ridden with her through the barn work and the drive to town and through the afternoon, and it was there now in the June heat in this specific piece of yard where she was not supposed to stop.

She said it before she had decided what she was doing.

"It will be over soon."

She said it quietly, looking at the fence past his shoulder. The way her father had said it — not to anyone in the room in particular, just the sentence released into the air.

Karl looked at her.

He looked at her for a moment and then he nodded. Once, slow. Not yes, not agreement. The nod she had seen him give Lonzo when Lonzo explained something Karl had already worked out for himself. *I hear you.* He looked back at the fields.

She stayed where she was with the pail.

He said, in English: "My home."

Not a question. The two words and then quiet, his hands loose between his knees, his eyes on the fields.

She did not ask what he thought he would find there.

She stood one moment longer. Then she picked up the pail and turned and walked toward the pump.

---

She heard him say something behind her. She was already several steps away — the radio going in the icehouse, the heat and the distance — and she caught only the shape of the words, not the words themselves.

*Es wird bald vorbei sein.*

The hay field beyond the fence held its full summer height. The smell of turned earth came across from the south field where her father had been working since before dawn, before the radio, before the sentence he had said into the kitchen. She kept walking. The pump handle at the corner of the barn would be warm from sitting in the afternoon sun.
