# Harbor Traffic

The *Edna C.* came in off the grounds at two-fifty-eight, heavier than she looked. Talbot brought her in clean through the afternoon chop, which was running south-southeast at two feet and building. Eira logged the time and estimated the catch from the boxes on deck — Talbot would want to correct the tonnage entry against the scale once he'd offloaded, but there was enough in the hold to see from the pier head and she put down a working number. She could revise it in the morning.

Four more vessels to account for before dark. She turned back to the fuel log.

The Navy tender at the outer anchorage had run its weekly fueling at nine that morning, and the allocation she'd authorized — drawn against the defense supplemental, not the commercial reserve — had taken a larger bite out of the shared dock supply than she'd anticipated. Pete Alley had come to her before noon wanting to know whether his allotment was going to cover his run to the outer grounds. She'd checked the figures and it wouldn't, not without an adjustment. She made the adjustment: signed the form, moved the amount from the supplemental line, wrote her authorization in the margin. She did not ask anyone's approval because the handbook did not require it. Alley took the form and filled his tank and went.

In the summer of 1942, this same move would have required a county sign-off and three days. She did not think about it often. When she did, it was as a log entry: what the column shows this year against what it showed before.

---

The afternoon was full. June kept long hours, the light staying good until past seven, which meant the traffic did not thin before six and on some evenings she was still logging at dusk. She did not object to this. The harbor in summer was the harbor at its most itself: boats on the water, fuel in the tanks, money moving, the cold-storage house working around the clock. The harbor in December was harder to manage and quieter, which was not the same as easier.

Grandy Soule had the *Maud L.* at the east float on account of a sports boat from Portland tying in his usual slip without permission. She'd had him at her elbow at noon, not saying much but standing in a way that said plenty. She'd walked down the ramp and told the man in the sports boat's cockpit that the slip was not available. He had a dock hand's tan and a new sportsman's jacket and the particular patience of a man who expected his arrangements to be accommodated. He looked at her for the length of time it took to understand that she had said what she meant. Then he cast off his lines and moved to slip fourteen, which she had indicated.

Soule, when she came back up the ramp, said nothing. She said nothing. She went back to the log.

The second dispute of the afternoon was between Burton Talbot and Ray Coffin's boy over a question of priority at the fuel dock — both had radioed at the same hour, and Coffin had gotten there first. She looked at the log and told them the order of the log was the order of the dock. Coffin fueled. Talbot waited. Both left without arguing. The argument had lasted four minutes and she had used twelve words, which seemed like the right ratio.

She was making a note about the outer berth allotment when Talbot came back up from the dock.

He had his hat in his hand. Talbot did not take his hat off on the dock as a rule.

"My sister's boy is in it," he said. He meant Normandy. It had been three weeks.

"How old is he."

"Nineteen."

She put the pen down and looked at him. She had known Burton Talbot for eight years.

"All right," she said.

He put his hat back on and went up the pier.

She wrote the tonnage entry and the time.

---

That was the quality of the harbor since the sixth of June — the particular way the men spoke now. Before June the waiting had been accumulative: each week adding to the prior week, the not-yet getting larger. Since June there was still waiting, but at a fixed position. The thing had been identified; it had a location and a direction. You could track it now. This was not the same as better. Osgood's boy had been missing in Normandy since the fourteenth and his mother came to the post office every morning. The telegrams had not stopped. But the quality of what people were waiting with had changed, and she could feel it in the way they stood at the fuel dock and in the number of words they used.

She could not write any of this in the harbor log. She kept track of it elsewhere, in the same place she kept track of other things she couldn't put in the official record.

---

The afternoon turned still around four. This happened in late June on certain days: the wind dropped off and the harbor went flat, the water the color of pewter, the pilings showing their reflections straight down. The cold-storage house was quiet between runs. The three lobstermen still out on the grounds would be thinking about coming in.

She checked the clock on the shed wall and checked her watch against it. Four-forty-three, which was accurate.

She made a note in the weather log: flat calm at 1643, channel at slack. She had been making these notes for eight years, in the same format, with the same shorthand. The notes would not change the weather. They were the record.

---

Marcus Klein came along the harbor road at five minutes past five.

She had seen him on this road at this hour for two years going on three — the same walk, the same pace, the same slight forward lean that was his particular posture, as if he were attending to something at chest height that the rest of the road was not showing. He was not a large man and he dressed for the season: a good hat in winter, shirtsleeves in June. She had seen him in all weathers. He walked the way someone walked who had no car and had decided not to mind it, which was to say at a deliberate pace and without any appearance of hurry.

Most days he came even with the pier entrance, nodded in her direction — she may or may not have been visible; it made no difference to the nod — and continued south toward the Crow house on the hill. She had come to expect this the way she expected the position of the channel markers: a regular fixed point in the afternoon's accounting.

Today he stopped.

He came a few steps through the gate. "Good afternoon," he said.

"Afternoon."

He looked at the pier and then at her. "Are the Pfeiffer family managing all right, do you think?"

The phrasing was assembled — not casual, not a question asked in passing to fill a silence, but a question whose specific terms had been chosen before it was asked. *The Pfeiffer family,* not the Pfeiffers. *Managing all right,* not *how are they* or *any trouble out there.* It was a question designed to be answerable without requiring her to say what she actually knew, which told her enough.

"As far as I know," she said.

Which was accurate. The farm was running; the county had raised no complaint about the work arrangement; the daughter came to the post office like any other family in town; Albrecht Pfeiffer had been in for his feed run three weeks ago. She knew nothing more specific than this. She said what she knew.

He nodded once. "Thank you," he said.

He went on down the road.

---

She watched him walk the harbor road to the south bend and go around it, out of sight.

The exchange had taken perhaps forty seconds. She went back to the pier head and picked up her pen.

The *Sea Star* would be in by six. She had the form ready.

She did not write anything about Marcus Klein. There was nothing to write — a man had asked a question and she had answered it.

She held it in the back of the afternoon's accounting the way she held any piece of information that did not have a column yet.

He had been walking this road for going on three years. In that time he had said perhaps twenty words to her. She had always read him as someone who chose his words with attention — the particular precision of a man who had learned to be careful with language. He was not a man who stopped at pier gates to ask after inland farm families without a reason for it.

He was the German teacher. The Pfeiffer daughter was in his advanced German class; she knew that much from conversation at the post office. Whether he had any other connection to the farm she did not know.

She turned the question over. No obvious answer presented itself. If he had asked the question behind the question, it would have been: *is anything wrong out there.* She could not account for what had put that question in him. She did not have enough to build a theory.

There was one other piece she was carrying that she had no column for. The dock figures from the October night — the two shapes on the south float before the blow, one with something under his arm, the pale coat. She had been keeping that entry for eight months without a label, because she still did not have enough to classify it. Soule's account in April had come part of the way, had corroborated the dinghy and one figure, and she had reached for the pen and then set it down again. She knew what she'd seen and she knew what Soule had seen and she knew the two accounts met at the south float, and she still did not have a label for what that column was.

The column now had two entries in it.

She put the pen down and did not invent a label. The column was there; she knew it was there; both entries were in it correctly and were going to stay there until she had enough to name what the column was. Not longer. Not shorter.

---

The *Sea Star* came in at six-twelve. Coffin's boy ran her through the channel entrance with economy, and she logged the time and the catch and wrote the weather note for the outer grounds. He said four words about the chop past Seal Island and she wrote that too, because the chop past Seal Island in late June could matter to the morning forecast.

She transferred the afternoon to the permanent record: fuel allotments, berth assignments, arrival times, departures, tonnages. Alley's supplemental form and her authorization. Talbot's working tonnage figure and the notation to revise in the morning. The two berth disputes resolved. Everything in its column, every column labeled correctly.

She put the pen down and put the log on its shelf.

The last light was running at a long angle off the channel, making the water look shallow even where it wasn't. The outer anchorage tender was riding easy and dark. The lobster boats were resting at their lines, hulls knocking gently against the fenders, the particular sound the harbor made in the slack after a working day.

She turned off the shed light and went out.

The harbor would manage the night without her. It was good at that.
