# Lonzo Reed

The chair was a wooden kitchen chair he had brought from the supply room in the icehouse's second week, when he understood that standing post was going to mean sitting post. The carbine lay across his thighs with the barrel pointed east. He was not going to need the carbine. He had not needed it in ten months and he was not going to need it today. He kept it across his thighs because the Army said to keep it across his thighs, and Lonzo Reed had, at sixty-two, arrived at a useful understanding of which rules were weight-bearing and which were decoration. This one was decoration. He kept it anyway.

The chair sat on packed dirt in front of the icehouse door. The afternoon light came long across the farmyard from the west, and behind him he could hear Dieter and Rolf working the near stall — the steady rhythm of their work, which was unhurried and efficient in the way of men who have decided to get through the day and no more. He had understood this about them in the first month.

He knew this piece of ground the way he knew certain things: by having stood in it long enough that the knowing had become its own kind of muscle. The layout of the farmyard. The sound the barn door made when the crossbar was not set. The way the shadow fell off the icehouse roof by the hour. The pattern of the Pfeiffer family's movements through the day — the father up before light, the mother appearing at the kitchen window at intervals, the girl out to the field or the barn at whatever the day required. He knew the names of the cows and the rhythm of their milking. He knew the smell of the late-season pasture and the cut-grass smell still carrying from last week and underneath both the standing smell of a dairy farm that had been a dairy farm longer than anyone on it could say.

He had been watching all his life. There were good reasons for it, and the watching had been its own education.

---

He had read the four Germans in the first month.

Dieter and Rolf and Hans: older men, all past forty, who had come to this war through years of a certain kind of obedience and understood the obedience required of them here in exactly the same way. Not dangerous. Not interested in making trouble. Interested in getting through the day and eventually through the war, which was all most men wanted from a war when they had any sense. He had managed them without incident by being exactly what he was: a sixty-two-year-old man with a carbine who was not going to do anything stupid and expected the same. The arrangement had held through ten months without a problem that required anything beyond a look and, once, a word.

Karl was different only in the way that nineteen was different from forty-three.

He had started calling the boy the waxwing boy sometime in the winter, after he spent an afternoon watching Karl watch the field above the fence. Not doing nothing — watching. Cataloguing. Lonzo had watched birds himself, in the way of a man who spent long hours in a chair and learned what to do with his eyes. He knew the cedar waxwing: the yellow tip of the tail, the crest, the red wax-colored spots on the wing that gave the bird its name. He had watched Karl standing at the pasture fence for the length of an afternoon and seen what the boy was doing and filed it.

He watched the girl too. He watched her write in the notebook the first time she brought it out, and he watched the notebook reappear through the winter and the spring and this summer. He watched the two of them build their back-and-forth of names for things in the way of people building something and not stopping until it was built.

He watched them the way he had watched most things: without announcing himself, without pretending not to watch. The kind of attention the girl had — the kind that knew it was paying attention — he had recognized that in October and tracked it through ten months. The regulations said no fraternizing, and what they had assembled between them — the notebook and the bird names and the words they had built from what the farm asked of them — was something those regulations had not been written to see.

He was not approving. He was not alarmed. He was watching to make sure it stayed inside the range of what he could permit.

It had stayed there.

---

His sister's boy Desmond was in the Army. Had been since March 1943. The Pacific.

The letters came to Desmond's mother, not to Lonzo, because Lonzo was not the kind of family that went on the letter list. He was the uncle. The one who had moved up from Georgia in '29 when the bottom fell out, who had wired money back when he had it, who had shown up in three different towns in fifteen years before landing here. He did not have a household. He had people, and Desmond was one of them.

The letters were getting shorter.

He didn't dwell on it. He noted it the way he noted other things: as a fact that had to be held somewhere, and that he was the holder of. The Pacific was a long way from a farmyard in Maine. He was sixty-two years old and there was no version of this where he was not standing in a farmyard in Maine while his sister's boy got shorter in a letter.

---

In October, in the post's first week, he had been driving back from the post office in town later than his usual time. A personal matter — a letter from Desmond's mother, shorter than the one before it, and then the time it took him to write a reply and find the right envelope. By the time he was on the road it was near eleven.

The farm road off Route 1 was not lit. He drove it twice a day and knew it at night by memory. The two bends. The low place where water stood after rain. The spot where the fence came close enough to the shoulder that he had to swing wide with the truck.

He was about a quarter mile from the front gate when the headlights picked up a man walking on the shoulder.

He slowed.

The man was heavyset, not young. Town clothes, not farm clothes — a coat with lapels, substantial cloth. He was moving toward the farm at a deliberate pace, not hurrying, not looking behind him, the pace of a man who had come a long way and was not going to stop short of where he was going.

By the time Lonzo pulled even with where the man had been, he was off the shoulder. He had turned onto the field track that ran alongside the east pasture fence — the track that went behind the hayfield, not the farm's proper entrance road. Lonzo could see the shape of him moving away into the dark, past the reach of the headlights, following the fence line.

Lonzo did not stop. It was a public road. The man was not on the farm property. His post was the icehouse and the four prisoners inside it. He drove on through the gate and parked the truck and went to the icehouse and found them all asleep.

He had thought about it more than once since.

He made the connection in February or March, when the body story was still going around town. A man found on the breakwater in October, heavyset, middle-aged, no papers. Nobody local. Nobody claiming him. He had heard it in the feed store and then at the post office. The Coast Guard had closed it without a name.

He could not be certain — not from what he had. A man on this road on that night, moving toward this farm. That was all of it. It was enough to put in front of someone.

He had been deciding who someone was for going on six months.

He knew how the conversation went. He had been in that room before, or rooms close enough to it that the difference didn't matter. The explaining of who he was. The questions that started before he finished. The weight put on one part of what he said and not the rest. He was not naive about what a sixty-two-year-old Coastal Patrol private's account of a man on a road in the dark at eleven o'clock would look like in an official record.

He had the piece. He was deciding what a piece was worth doing with.

---

The shadows were coming long across the farmyard when Karl came out of the icehouse.

He sat on the step the way he sometimes did in the late afternoon, his forearms on his knees, and looked up at the sky.

The sky was clear. The late-August light had that quality of the far end of the season — not the white-hot light of July but something going long and gold and slant. Karl was doing the thing he did: looking up with the particular quality of attention he used when he was looking for something to name.

A bird crossed the far edge of the field. Small, quick. Gone before Lonzo had fixed it.

Karl watched the air where it had been.

Lonzo watched Karl.

He thought about the man on the farm road. Heavyset, not young, moving toward the farm in the October dark at the pace of a man carrying something that mattered to him. He thought about what the man had found when he got to wherever the field track took him, and whether any of it had come to anything before the storm.

He did not know.

Karl looked at the sky a moment more. Then he went back through the icehouse door.

Lonzo watched the door.

The waxwing boy at his post. The man on the farm road heading toward it in October. One inside the icehouse; one on the breakwater by morning. He didn't know what was between those two facts. He was not certain there was anything.

He had not decided.
