# Continental Educator

The morning walk from the Crow house to the school was fifteen minutes, a calculation Marcus had verified repeatedly over nineteen months and continued to verify, partly out of habit and partly because he was a man who understood the difference between a fact that had been established and a fact that merely appeared to have been established. The one variable he had not succeeded in eliminating was the intersection at the bottom of Elm Street, where the road made its north turn and the harbor appeared briefly between two buildings — a flat gray angle of water, the breakwater's outer arm, no vessels at the south dock this morning. Some mornings he stopped. Today he did not.

Main Street at half past seven: the hardware store's windows still dark, the bakery lit, a truck outside the post office with its engine running and the driver standing beside it, watching his exhaust cloud with the peaceable attention of a man whose morning was going according to plan. Marcus nodded. The driver nodded. This exchange, which had occurred in some variation for nineteen months, had arrived at a precision of mutual acknowledgment that Marcus found, if not warm, then accurate — and in his experience accuracy was a form of warmth, or at least its structural equivalent.

The school sat back from the road behind a low iron fence and four large elms that had not yet been touched by whatever was moving through the New England elms. Three stories, dark brick, the year 1912 cut into the cornerstone — he had read it once and it had stayed, the way dates stayed, without being asked to. He went up the main steps and through the door into the smell of chalk dust and boiler heat: Henry had the building to the right temperature, as he always had it to the right temperature. Marcus hung his coat on the hook inside the teachers' entry and went upstairs.

---

His first class was Latin. This year the Latin class was three students — Cornelia Foss and two boys named Pratt and Heywood, none of whom had elected the course voluntarily but all of whom had been placed in it by the vice-principal on a theory Marcus considered neither obviously correct nor obviously wrong. He had them, and they had him, and in nineteen months of this arrangement both parties had arrived at a working understanding of what was expected.

The classroom was on the third floor, north-facing, with windows that admitted the particular flat gray light of coastal Maine in late October: not winter, not autumn, not quite either, a light without strong claims on the hour or the mood. Three desks arranged in the front half of the room, which was the correct number of desks for three students and which had the effect of making the back half of the room into a kind of reserve space — not empty, exactly, but waiting. Marcus had learned not to think about the back half of the room.

He wrote a sentence on the board. It was from Cicero's *De Amicitia*, the kind of sentence Cicero wrote when he was working at full capacity: the main clause withheld while the subordinate architecture assembled itself, so that by the time the verb arrived you had been holding the opening phrase in your mind long enough to know what weight the closing would need to carry. He underlined the subordinate clause, set the chalk in the tray, and turned.

"Don't translate it," he said.

He had been saying this in four languages, across more years and more classrooms than he intended to calculate, and what he meant by it was always the same: sit with the sentence before you reach for the English. Learn the architecture before the furniture. A translation that arrives too quickly has not understood what it has translated.

The three of them sat with it. He could read, from the quality of each student's stillness, which one was reading and which two were waiting for rescue. Cornelia Foss's stillness had a forward quality — she was inside the sentence, working. The two boys produced the carefully arranged blankness of students who have learned to display attention without generating it. The ratio — one out of three with the forward stillness — was, in Marcus's experience, approximately a constant, across Latin and German and French and any other language that required this particular quality of sitting. He found it neither discouraging nor encouraging. It was a distribution.

After a moment: "Miss Foss."

She looked up.

"Tell me what the sentence is doing."

She paused. She was the kind of mind that checked an answer before it committed to it, which Marcus had identified early and respected, it being his own habit. "It's waiting," she said. "The whole thing — it holds open until the end. You can't close it until you have all of it."

"Yes."

He did not add to this. In his experience, the correctly made observation was its own sufficient reward; adding praise introduced an economy of approval that distorted the thing being observed. He let it be. She looked back at the board, and after a moment the two boys looked back at the board, and the lesson continued.

---

The period between his Latin class and the first German section was thirty-five minutes, which Marcus divided between the staffroom on the second floor and the set of translation exercises he had marked the previous night and had comments remaining for. The staffroom coffee was, in his considered opinion, the worst he had encountered in any English-speaking institution in two continents, and he had encountered it in a number of such institutions over the past several years. He drank it.

Stillman came in. He taught geography in rooms 201 and 202, moved through the building with the slightly compressed energy of someone who had recently been somewhere else and not quite fully arrived here. He poured coffee and stood at the counter.

"Odd business down at the harbor," he said. He said most things from across a room, as though physical distance were a conversational courtesy.

Marcus set down his pen.

"What kind of business?"

"Man washed in on the breakwater after the blow last week. No papers. Nothing on him at all. CG took the body and that's the last officially."

"Town clothes, they said? Not a fisherman?"

"That's what I heard." Stillman shrugged with one shoulder. "Nobody from here. Nobody from anywhere anyone knows about. Clement Ames was down at the fish pier when the CG truck came in."

Marcus turned this over in the way he handled incomplete information: not with appetite for conclusion, not with the urgency that transforms the unverified into the presumed, but with the steady attention of a man who had learned over many years that the significant quality of incomplete information was its incompleteness, and that the correct response was to note it and wait. A man with no papers, washed in from a three-day storm. The verified facts were few. The range of explanations was large and at present unnarrowed. He put it in the part of his mind that held things requiring more data before they could become anything — a part that had, over the past five years, accumulated considerably.

"Has anyone put out a description?" he said.

"Not that I know of." Stillman finished his coffee. "Sailor off a convoy is what most people are saying."

Marcus nodded and returned to the translation exercises.

---

He passed the principal's open door at twenty past nine. The door stood open, as it nearly always did — the principal being the kind of administrator who managed through visibility, the open door a performance of nothing to hide. On the desk, partially in view from the hallway, was a folder with Marcus's name on the tab, and in the line below the name, in the school secretary's round and deliberate hand, the notation the town meeting had appended to his original hire: *Continental educator.*

He had seen this folder before. He had sat across from it in March of 1942, when the principal had gone through the courses Marcus would be assuming, and again the following spring, when the question of the advanced German section had required a committee meeting at which the folder had been present as a kind of documentation — a note explaining to any future questioner why the school had hired a Viennese Jewish émigré in a town of eight hundred people two years into a war. *Continental educator* accounted for him without requiring anyone to decide what they thought about it. He had found, broadly, that this was how things were explained in Coldwater Reach, and that the explanation was neither hostile nor warm, but administrative — which was also, in its way, a form of accuracy.

He kept walking.

---

The afternoon walk covered the same ground reversed. Main Street had the different character of four-thirty: a line of women outside the butcher's door with their ration books, a delivery bicycle leaned against the diner's steps, three junior-school boys occupying the hardware store's stoop until the store owner appeared in the doorway and they relocated to the next block.

The Western Union boy's bicycle was leaned against the post office wall.

He kept walking.

He had been in Coldwater ten months before he watched, from approximately this distance, a woman receive a telegram at the post office door. It had been January — clear and cold, the kind of January light that made everything look precise and final — and he had been far enough away that he saw only the shape of it: the boy, the envelope, the woman's hands, and the quality of stillness that followed the exchange. He had known what was in the envelope before it was opened. The shape was unmistakable, once you had seen it in one country, in one administrative language or another. He had kept walking that day also.

This afternoon the bicycle stood unattended. Nobody came in or out of the post office while he passed. The empty doorway, the bicycle, the ordinary afternoon continuing on either side of them.

He walked down Elm Street. At the corner he did not stop, and the harbor appeared briefly and was flat and gray, the south dock still empty, and he turned north.

He thought of Sherbrooke for perhaps ten seconds. Not of the camp, not of the months — the dormitory, the other men from fifteen countries assembled by their common classification as the same kind of enemy, none of them having been in a position to do much about being Austrian in 1940 — but of the specific moment of arrival: the gate intake desk, the clerk's hand lifting the document from Marcus's coat pocket and placing it on the far side of the desk, and Marcus's own hands afterward, standing in the cold with no paper in them, not knowing quite what to do with themselves. He thought of this for ten seconds. Then he was at the Crow house, and he put his hand on the gate latch and lifted it and went in.

---

The kitchen was warm. Lillian was at the stove, the iron pan over the low burner, bread already cut on the board, the tea things arranged on the counter because there was not enough counter for the tea things and anything else. She heard him come in and did not turn.

"Cold getting into it," she said.

"Yes."

She set a plate on the table without asking what he wanted. She had not asked what he wanted in approximately eighteen months, because at some point in the first season of his residence his preferences had been established and were since treated as given facts, the way Lillian treated most things she had once established to her satisfaction. He hung his coat on the hook inside the door and sat down.

Below, Henry was in the boiler room. The sounds came up through the floor in the order Marcus had learned to follow across two winters: the knock of the pipe fitting, the steady sound of something turned, the occasional deep report of the boiler accepting what it had been given. The sounds of competent work, which Marcus had found were the same sounds regardless of the object they were applied to, and which were, in some way he had never fully articulated, reassuring — not because the boiler required reassurance, but because the sounds placed a capable person in the room below.

"How did school go?" Lillian said. She was not facing him.

"We read Cicero. More precisely, one student read Cicero and two students sat in the general area."

The sound she made was not quite a laugh but was in the same family. "German any better?"

"Somewhat."

She set the tea down and did not follow up the question. Marcus had learned, in this house, to understand the question asked and let go as a form of consideration rather than disinterest: she asked; she received; she moved on. The household had a register, and he had learned it the way he had learned every register in every house and institution he had occupied, and it was now, mostly, natural.

---

On the kitchen wall, hung from a nail above the window that looked toward the backyard: James Crow's Army photograph.

Formal. The kind taken before deployment: the uniform precise, the studio background a neutral gray, the set of his shoulders in the jacket partly the jacket's structure and partly the young man's own. He had a sergeant's formality in the way he held himself. Marcus looked at the photograph the way he looked at it each time he sat down at this table — with the attention that a face deserves and that absence makes available. He had never seen James Crow in motion. He knew the photograph, and the letters that arrived for Lillian with Pacific postmarks, and the particular adjustment in Henry's pace on the days when such a letter appeared.

He did not know James Crow. He had a static image and the outline of an absence and the sounds of a father working steadily in the room below, which was perhaps, at this distance, the truest version of a person available to him.

Henry came up through the basement door partway through, crossed to the sink, turned on the tap.

"Fitting on the south loop's going to want replacing before December," he said.

"Will that be a difficult part to find?"

"Need to order it." Henry dried his hands on the side towel, looked at Marcus. "School all right?"

"Yes."

Henry nodded and went back downstairs.

---

His room was on the second floor, the window facing east over the yard. The curtain was heavy dark wool, the desk against the wall, the shelf holding more books than it had held in March of 1942, which was a kind of record. He sat on the edge of the bed with a book open across his knee and did not read it.

After a while came the sound of Henry closing the basement door — the sequence of steps from the back of the house forward — and then the house settled into its night disposition: the known sounds, the positioned quiet, nothing requiring response.

*Continental educator.* He had been carrying the phrase since mid-morning with the steady, absent quality he brought to things that had been said about him and that were not precisely wrong. In Vienna he had been Dr. Klein — a form of address, a specific weight in a room. In Paris he had been the Austrian, which was precisely if not unkindly meant. In London he had been the refugee, which was a category. In Sherbrooke he had been classified as the enemy alien, which was at least a position of known standing, even if the standing was wrongly arrived at. In Cambridge he had been the displaced scholar, which was accurate and was also, in its gentle way, a form of storage: a useful object held for later placement. In Coldwater he was the Continental educator, a phrase on a folder on a desk, which was all of these and was none of them.

He was, he thought, practicing something. The walk down Elm Street was a practice. The staffroom coffee was a practice. The kitchen downstairs and Lillian's plate set without asking and the sound of Henry's work coming up through the floor: all of this was practice. He was not certain — and he was, where he could be, precise about his certainties — whether what he was practicing was Coldwater or himself.

He closed the book and reached over and turned off the lamp.
