# The Visit

The letter had been in his desk drawer since November — center drawer, under the Latin dictionary, which was where he had put it and where it had remained while he decided, over the course of six weeks, what to do. That morning he had taken it out before the first class, held it in both hands for a moment, and placed it in his coat pocket. The act of placing it in his coat was the decision itself; the walking would settle what remained.

Henry had offered the truck the previous evening. Marcus had said it was no trouble, he would walk, and Henry had received this with the quiet attention he gave to information that did not quite add up and had chosen not to press it. He was not wrong not to press it. The reason Marcus had for declining was difficult to explain without sounding like a man who made things more effortful than they needed to be, which, in this instance, was essentially accurate. Movement was how he prepared for difficult things. It had been this in Vienna — he had walked the Ringstrasse before faculty meetings that required a particular positioning of himself — and it remained so in Coldwater, a town whose geography was flat enough to accommodate the habit without much planning.

He went out the back gate at quarter past eight, between the first class and the second, through a morning that was cold with a particular Maine January coldness: still air, temperatures well below freezing but without the sharp edge that wind would have given it, the sky an even dull white from the horizon up. He walked the harbor road to the bridge and then north on Route 1, where the town gave out within half a mile — the last storefront, a gap, a fence row, and then only the land.

He had three miles.

What he knew: Werner Grau had written from Portland in late September 1943, planning to travel up the coast to Coldwater in October, carrying news of a family in Ansbach that he would not commit to paper. What he knew less certainly, from secondhand accounts filtered through the staffroom and one conversation with Stillman: a man in town clothes had come in on the breakwater after the October storm, no papers, and the Coast Guard had classified it as an unidentified drowning and closed the case. What he had assembled, and kept assembled, over the weeks since November: the plausible connection between these two facts. Plausible was not proof. He had been careful, in his private accounting, to keep the distinction exact.

He knew also what he did not intend to say. He was not going to Albrecht Pfeiffer's farm to investigate. He had no standing for investigation and would not have known what to do with standing if it had been offered. He was going because the letter in his coat pocket was Albrecht Pfeiffer's letter — had always been Albrecht Pfeiffer's letter — and had come to Marcus only through a postal error and a school secretary's pragmatic decision, and it was not his to hold indefinitely. The misdelivery could be corrected. He was correcting it. If Albrecht Pfeiffer's response to receiving his letter told Marcus something he had not known before, Marcus would receive that information in whatever form it arrived and decide then what to do with it.

He would not, under any circumstances, mention Werner Grau's name or the body on the breakwater unless Albrecht opened that door. It was not his door.

The land past the last woodlots was drumlin country — long grass-covered hills left by the glaciers, lying in parallel ridges under their January white, the hay fields flat between them. He had walked this road once before, in his first spring, when the walking had been a kind of inventory of the place he had landed in, and he had found the agricultural plainness of it after coastal terrain surprising and then not surprising. Inland land looked like what it was for. There was a satisfaction in that.

The road bent west at a line of cedar fence posts. He found the farm road, which was dirt and hard and showed no tire tracks from the last few days, and turned onto it.

---

The farm materialized from behind a stand of red maples — leafless, the trunks dark with recent moisture, the lower branches still holding some snow. Then the barn came into view: gray board siding weathered to the particular silver of very old wood, the gambrel roof carrying a good eighteen inches of packed white on its south-facing pitch, the whole structure giving the impression — familiar to Marcus from a different part of the world, from the villages northeast of Vienna where his maternal grandmother had had relations — of something built with the expectation of lasting several lifetimes and now well along in delivering on that expectation.

He read the cornerstone as he entered the farmyard. 1763, half-covered in ice, the numerals carved in the flat-ended strokes of the period. Pre-Revolutionary. Built before the choices the Revolution required, by people who had not yet had to make them.

In the dooryard between the barn and the farmhouse, dry stalks stood through the snow at waist height, stripped by autumn to their skeletons, each branching terminal forked and bearing the dried ruin of seed heads — burred, compact, architecturally complete in their stripped state. *Arctium.* He had the Latin name before he had the English. He always did; it was an inconvenience of his training and he had long since stopped apologizing for it to himself.

The icehouse bunkhouse was visible past the barn's north corner — a small structure with a new wooden step and a bar-latch door, currently closed. As Marcus came across the frozen farmyard a POW emerged from the barn carrying a metal bucket, moving at the measured unhurried pace of a man who has found the exact ratio of effort to temperature and holds to it without thinking. He was one of the older ones — not Karl Reinmar; this man was in his mid-thirties or beyond, with the build of a man grown by agricultural work over many years, broad through the back and settled in his movement. He stopped when he saw Marcus. The stop was brief — two seconds at most — and it was the stop of a man conducting an assessment, which Marcus recognized because it was the stop of a man who has learned to assess strangers as a matter of course and to do it without display. Having completed the assessment, the POW went on with the bucket, neither hurried nor slow, without looking back.

He had been assessed this way in more than one country by men in a range of circumstances, and he knew its grammar. He nodded, once, at the POW's back, and continued toward the barn door.

---

Albrecht Pfeiffer was crouching at the wheel of a manure spreader — or rather at the axle housing, which he was examining with the close attentiveness of a man who has identified a problem and is deciding its complexity before deciding his response to it. He heard Marcus's footfall at approximately ten feet and straightened and turned.

He was in his middle fifties, thick through the shoulders, his face weathered by outdoor work beyond what his age alone would account for — not aged but used, in the way that outdoor faces in cold climates were used, the lines reading as information about the weather rather than as complaint. He looked at Marcus with a direct, unhurried attention that Marcus had encountered before in this part of the world: the attention of a man who was ready to hear a thing and had not yet decided whether the particular thing now arriving was the one he had been readying himself for.

Marcus introduced himself carefully. He taught at the school — German and Latin. He knew Hannah, who was a student in his advanced German section. In the fall, a piece of correspondence had been misdelivered to the school — addressed to Albrecht, in German — and the school secretary had passed it to Marcus on the grounds that it was in German and he would be best placed to handle it. He had been meaning to see it properly delivered. He reached into his coat and produced the envelope.

He watched Albrecht look at the envelope.

There was a small tightening around the eyes — something that moved quickly, a contraction and release, less than a second. Albrecht took the envelope from Marcus's hand with a steadiness that required, Marcus thought, a small effort to sustain. He looked at the return address. He looked at the postmark. He turned the envelope over, examined the back, and then opened it, drawing the letter out by its fold.

He read.

Marcus stood and did not look away. He had made a study, across the course of a life spent among many languages, of reading faces. A person receiving something they did not know had a particular quality of attention: the eyes moved carefully from line to line, the face open in the way of a container being filled, something held in readiness to adjust. A person receiving something they already knew had a different quality entirely. The attention was concentrated but abbreviated; the face still, and still in the way of a closed room rather than an open one. The pace of the eyes was quicker, not because the words were simpler but because the words were confirming rather than informing, and confirmation moved faster than discovery.

Albrecht Pfeiffer's face, reading Werner Grau's letter, was moving at the second rate.

There was a moment — midway through the first side, Marcus thought; Werner had used his full name in the middle of a sentence, not merely at the close — where something crossed Albrecht's face that Marcus found he could name precisely only after it had passed. It was not surprise. It was not grief, though grief was in the neighborhood of it. It was the expression of a man receiving what he had expected to receive and finding, as one always found, that the expectation had not been sufficient preparation for the arrival. Recognition of a specific kind: the kind that says *I knew this was coming*, and the confirmation says *yes*, and the *yes* requires something from the person hearing it that the waiting had not yet required.

Marcus had seen this face before. He had seen it in Vienna in 1938, on people reading the news, when the thing that could not happen had happened and their faces had shifted from the look of a person watching a storm gather to the look of a person on whom the storm had broken. He had worn some version of it himself — in Sherbrooke, when the intake clerk had placed his document on the far side of the desk and he had understood what the gesture meant before anyone had used words. Albrecht Pfeiffer was not reading news about a political catastrophe. He was reading a letter from a man who had planned to come to Coldwater in October, in a January that was three months past that October, and the letter was not information. It was Werner's voice, arriving after Werner.

Albrecht folded the letter. He held it across its center, both hands, and was still for a moment. Then he looked at Marcus.

"When did this come to you?" His English was careful — chosen, unhurried, the English of a man who was selecting a language rather than falling into it. Slightly formal, slightly stilted. Not the English of his household; the English of an occasion.

"November," Marcus said.

Albrecht nodded once. It was a slow nod, a full acknowledgment, not a mere receipt of information.

"Thank you," he said.

The two words were complete. They had in them a quality that Marcus recognized from other languages and other contexts: the quality of a door being closed from the inside. Not locked, not unfriendly — the hostility of a door slammed was entirely absent — but latched. The conversation was over. The question of whether there was anything further to say had been decided, quietly, without Marcus being consulted, and the decision had been reached before the question was raised, probably before Albrecht Pfeiffer had finished the letter's second side. *Thank you.* A full sentence. The space after it was not a pause; it was a close.

Marcus said it was no trouble. He did not say anything else. He did not say the things he was holding — the questions arranged behind the letter, the sequence he had assembled in six weeks of carrying the connection from November's discovery to this January morning. He had come with a letter. The letter was now Albrecht Pfeiffer's. What stood behind the letter was not Marcus's to present.

He turned and walked back down the farm road.

---

He did not look back. The red maples thinned and fell away behind him and the road ran straight between the drumlin fields under the white sky.

He ran the sequence.

Albrecht Pfeiffer had read Werner Grau's letter and had not been surprised.

He had not been surprised by the name. He had not been surprised by the date or the contents or the fact of the letter's existence. He had read it with the face of a man receiving a confirmation rather than a discovery, and the confirmation he had received was not the contents of the letter — which were Werner's plans for a visit in October, plans now three months past — but the existence of the letter itself: Werner had written. Werner had planned to come. Werner had been Werner, three months ago and twenty years before that, Werner enough to write to Albrecht after however many years of American silence and to sign himself *Ihr alter Freund.*

Which meant — Marcus slowed on the iced rut without noticing he had slowed, the cold exact on his face, Route 1's utility poles visible now ahead in the distance — which meant that Albrecht Pfeiffer knew, had known since October, that Werner Grau had come to Coldwater and had not come back. He had been sitting with this knowledge through the fall and through December and into January, with no one to give it to and no apparent intention of giving it to anyone. The letter arriving was not news to him. It was Werner's voice, which he already knew had gone silent, speaking once more from the month before it went silent.

Marcus could not prove this. He had inference and a face, and the face was not courtroom evidence and would not be. But he had been reading faces and language for most of a lifetime, and the reading he had done at the barn door was the reading of a man who knows what he has seen.

Albrecht Pfeiffer had known Werner Grau had come to Coldwater.

Which meant he had seen Werner — or received word that Werner had come — before the storm.

He had been carrying this since October.

Marcus walked north. Route 1 came up and he turned onto it without deciding to. The cold was steady in his coat. He thought about the letter's weight — its actual physical weight, which had been slight, two pages written on a single sheet — and about the different weight he had been holding since November, the inferential weight, and about the way Albrecht Pfeiffer had folded the letter across its center and held it with both hands. There was a way a person held something when they had been waiting for it. The hands did not know how to be casual about it, whatever the face had decided.

He did not know what had happened on the night Werner Grau had died. He knew what was plausible; he knew, now, that Albrecht Pfeiffer knew more than was plausible. He did not know whether Werner had reached the farm that night or not. He did not know what Albrecht knew about the hours between Werner's arrival in Coldwater and the storm. He did not know and had no way to know, and had made no move, at the barn door, to ask — because he had not been offered the right to ask and would not have received an answer if he had taken the right uninvited.

He held what he had. It was not nothing. It was not enough to do anything with, and he was not at all certain what *do* would mean here, in any case.

The town came back around him gradually: the first outbuildings, then the fence rows tightening, then the first storefront with its wooden sign. He walked up Main Street and turned onto Elm and went up the hill toward the house.

---

Henry's truck was not in the yard. The front of the house was dark, the curtains in the parlor where Marcus never went drawn against the afternoon cold. He came around through the back gate, which was his entrance, and up the back step.

Lillian was at the stove, the lid of the soup pot sitting slightly ajar, the smell of it beginning to reach the room. She looked up when the door opened and looked back at the pot. This was her form of greeting, which Marcus had received nearly every evening for almost two years: *you are in, the meal is going, you are accounted for.* He had learned to receive it as what it was — not a performance of welcome but welcome itself, in the practical form it took in this household.

He took off his coat and hung it on the hook by the door.

The pocket, empty now, had the shape of its former contents — or he was giving it that shape, which amounted to the same thing. Three months the letter had been in his desk drawer. Three months he had been holding a plausible connection that was nobody else's business and that he had no authorized use for. He had carried it this morning from the drawer to his coat and down the farm road and into the farmyard and across the conversation that had consisted of an introduction, a delivery, and two short sentences, and he had left the letter in Albrecht Pfeiffer's hands, where it had always belonged. He had given the one thing over that he had had any standing to give.

He stood at the hook.

What Marcus held now was not the letter. He held the inference — the sequence, the face, the folded paper held across its center — and these were not a thing he could put in a desk drawer, and he understood, standing at the coat hook with Lillian at the stove and the winter afternoon going dark outside the kitchen window, that he had known this before he had started walking.

Lillian set two plates on the table.

He sat down.
