# Another Year

The hardware store had put out its summer display — aluminum pails, a coil of garden hose in a shade of green that seemed to belong somewhere less austere — and Marcus stopped to regard it before walking on. The diner's screen door was propped with a brick, as it had been propped since May, when the evenings finally warranted it. He had lived in Coldwater for two years and four months. He knew the brick. He knew the diner owner's habit of playing the radio at a volume that seemed to assume the building was empty, and he knew, having verified it over the course of two summers, that this was simply her volume — not a statement about the building, not an invitation, simply the way the sound occupied the space she was in. He knew which three houses on the north block had dogs and in which order they would announce his passage. He knew the bakery's exhaust vent, open in summer, which emitted a smell of something with sugar in it that he had spent several weeks identifying before he thought to simply look at the building's south wall. He knew the cat in the upper right window of the gray house at the far end, which watched him daily without sound, with a consistency that was, he had come to understand, its form of acknowledgment.

He extended his walk tonight past the usual turning point at the harbor road's south bend, continuing down to the pier end and along the last quarter mile toward the south headland, where the road ended at the boatyard's locked gate. The boatyard was closed for the evening, its equipment organized in the particular way of a yard whose foreman considered the arrangement to be its own kind of order. The Ellsworth was in her summer berth. The lobster boats from the outer grounds had come in and the men were gone. The harbor in July was a different harbor from the harbor in October or in February: more traffic, longer light, but no urgency in it — the work was the same work, but the weather was forgiving enough that it had stopped being the kind of work that could make errors fatal, and this gave the working waterfront a different quality, almost relaxed, which was a word he would not have used for it in November.

He had been thinking about the letter for five weeks.

It was not, precisely, from Sondheimer. Sondheimer was at Columbia and had been at Columbia since 1942, answering his telephone before it finished ringing and placing scholars with the efficiency of a man running a clearing operation under deadline. The letter was from a colleague of Sondheimer's — a man named Althaus, affiliated with the Emergency Committee in some ancillary capacity — who had a position to fill at a small college outside Boston. German literature, beginning September 1944. The letter was professional, appropriately cordial, and had been composed by someone who was confident the offer would be received as welcome. The library would be better than whatever Marcus had access to in coastal Maine. The salary was better. The department had two other members, both scholars of some reputation; there was the prospect of colleagues, in the professional sense, rather than a schoolroom he shared with the supply closet and a staffroom whose only other daily occupant was the trigonometry teacher. The letter noted, with some care, that there was a German émigré community of substance in the Cambridge area. It did not say *you must be lonely*, but it arranged its facts in a manner that made the inference available.

He had been considering it at one remove, with the discipline of a man who had learned to examine choices from a slight distance rather than from within their orbit. When he tried to imagine Boston — the apartment he would need to locate, the departmental room with the other two faculty, the particular October quality of a college library's reading room in the afternoon — the image that came was not quite Boston. It was a composite assembled from memory of other institutions: reading rooms in Vienna, in Paris, the brief unhappy months in London. An accurate image of the category and a generic image of the particular. The Boston apartment had no street address. The departmental room had no windows he could place. He had tried for five weeks to make the image specific and he could not do it.

When he tried to imagine Coldwater in September — the boiler starting in the school's basement, the harbor road going its autumn gray, Hannah Pfeiffer's translation papers on his desk with their marginalia in two languages, Henry's coffee at six in the morning, the sound of Henry's footstep on the stair distinguishable from any other footstep in the building — the image was specific without requiring effort. He could render it in accurate detail. The boiler's particular sound when it came on was different from any boiler he had known before, and he knew it. The distinction between the two qualities of imagination — the generic and the specific, the category and the particular — seemed to him like data of some kind, though he had not yet decided what it was evidence for.

The breakwater was visible from the south end of the road, its lower arm running out toward the elbow in the evening light. He looked at it for a moment, as he looked at it most evenings when he was at this end of the street. Then he looked away and turned back toward Henry's house.

Through the kitchen window, as he came up the street, he could see Lillian setting the table. She moved through the kitchen with the particular efficiency of a person in their own space — adjusting for the cabinet door she knew without looking, setting the plates down in a way he recognized from three hundred evenings in this house. He had a room here; he had a key; he paid his rent on the first of each month and had been doing so since the spring of 1942. He had always understood this relationship correctly, in his own accounting: he was a tenant, occupying a room in a house that belonged to Henry and Lillian in the way that only a house thirty years lived in belongs to people. He had, for two years and four months, thought of it as Henry's house, in the same way he had thought of every room he had rented since 1938 as belonging to whoever owned or kept it — a form of precision that was also, he recognized, a form of self-protection, since a room that belongs to someone else is a room you can leave without leaving something that is yours.

Coming up the street toward it now, the summer evening very long and the kitchen light very certain in the dimming July light, he was not walking toward a room he was occupying. He was going back to a place.

He did not examine this in the middle of the street. He filed it and walked on.

---

*That evening.*

He sat at the desk in his room and wrote the reply to Althaus. It was shorter than the original. He thanked Althaus, noted that the position was well suited to his qualifications and that he had no doubt the college would find an excellent candidate for it, and stated that he was committed to his current position in Coldwater and found it — here he chose the word *suited* with care, the way he chose all his words in professional correspondence, for precision rather than warmth — suited to his capacities in the ways that currently mattered. He offered to recommend colleagues from the Emergency Committee rolls who might be available for the September start. He closed with appropriate formality.

He did not write *at least one further academic year*, though that was the phrase in his own mind, the honest accounting of a man who did not wish to make claims that exceeded his current state of resolution. What the letter said was *committed* to Coldwater. He was clear about the distinction. The "at least" was in his own mind, where he could keep it accurate and revisit it as conditions required, without having communicated a hedged commitment to a professional contact who had no means of interpreting the hedge and no reason to receive it charitably. He was not lying to Althaus. He was simply not exporting the hedge. This seemed to him defensible.

He sealed the envelope and wrote the address in his usual hand and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, which was on the chair, for the morning mail.

He stayed at the desk. The window was open. Henry and Lillian had gone upstairs an hour ago, and the house had the quality of a house in which two people were already settled for the night — a particular sense of an occupied building at rest, familiar to him now in a way it had not been in the first winter, when the settling sounds of the structure had woken him more than once.

Then, from outside, carried on the harbor breeze that was moving in from the southeast, he heard the bell buoy.

---

He had heard it before, at intervals, on other quiet nights from this window. It was the bell buoy at the channel entrance, placed by the Coast Guard to mark the deep line of the approach, and it rang on the swell in the rhythm of the water rather than the rhythm of a clock — neither metronomic nor erratic, but in the particular irregular pattern of a float responding to what was actually there. He had heard it as he heard most of the harbor's ongoing sounds: as acoustic texture, as part of the category of harbor sounds that occupied the audible background of this street, along with the diesel engines at five in the morning and the gulls over the fish pier and the occasional sound of a dragger coming in off the outer grounds after dark. He had heard it and not distinguished it.

He distinguished it now because the night was quiet and because he was at his desk with the window open and a coat on the chair with a letter in the pocket, and the buoy's sound was not the sound of harbor-in-general but the sound of the bell buoy at the entrance to the channel at Coldwater Reach — a specific sound in a specific place, which was this place. It could not be heard from Althaus's small college outside Boston. It could not be heard from Cambridge or London or Sherbrooke or Paris or Vienna. It could be heard from this room, with this window open at this angle to the southeast, on nights when the harbor breeze came from the right direction and the town was quiet enough to let it through.

He was not persuaded by this observation. He was aware that sentiment could be constructed from almost any available material, and that the sentiment of *this specific sound* was available to any man who had spent sufficient time in proximity to a harbor with a bell buoy in it.

The grammar interested him more.

The difference between *I am staying* and *I have stopped leaving* was a matter of aspect: the first located the action in the present, ongoing, as though staying were an activity requiring continuous effort of will; the second registered a prior event whose effects persisted into the present without requiring continuous maintenance. Both produced the same present-tense situation — a man in a room in Coldwater in July 1944. What they differed on was what each attributed the situation to. *I am staying* implied a decision being renewed. *I have stopped leaving* implied a decision already made, no longer under active reconsideration.

He was not certain which was accurate. He suspected that neither was, exactly — that the distinction between choice and inertia, when applied to the accumulation of two years in a specific place, was not as clean as the grammar required. He had made no formal decision to leave Coldwater and had accordingly never formally stopped leaving. He had, on several occasions, been presented with the option of leaving — first after his first winter, when Sondheimer had written about a high school position in Hartford; then in the spring of 1943, when there had been a brief inquiry about translation work in Washington that had come to nothing; and now this, from Althaus, which was the most substantial of the three. He had declined each one without, so far as he could identify, a sense of sacrifice. The question was whether a pattern of not-leaving, accumulated over three iterations, constituted a form of staying. He thought it might, observed from a sufficient distance. He was not certain he was in a position to observe it from a sufficient distance yet.

The buoy rang three times in close succession, then twice more, then the interval extended.

He was not going anywhere — not this September, and, he thought, not the September after. He had not decided this. He had written a letter and addressed it and put it in his coat for the morning mail, and the letter said *committed* with no hedge in it, and the hedge in his own mind was the honest ledger of a man who knew himself well enough to know that the present moment's resolution was provisional in the way all present-moment resolutions were provisional. But the provisionality of the resolution was not the same as the resolution being absent. The letter was in the coat. He would mail it in the morning.

The harbor outside went on with its business without requiring any acknowledgment from him. The bell buoy rang at the entrance to the channel in the rhythm of the water and not of any clock. The summer night in Coldwater continued in the usual way of summer nights in Coldwater, which was to say: it went on past the time when most of the houses were dark, steady and unhurried.
