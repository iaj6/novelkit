# Transfer

The truck came down the farm road at nine and she heard it before she saw it — the engine note through the bare tree line at the gate, the particular sound of a loaded vehicle finding its way over the ruts her father had not had time to fill. November had come fully in the week since the transfer order arrived; the cold was in the ground now, in the flattened grass, in the particular quality of morning air off the back fields that was not yet winter-cold but had given up on anything else. She had been in the farmyard since a quarter past eight.

There was no more milking to do. She had done it before five, moving cow to cow in the dark with the kerosene lamp hanging from its nail, doing the work thoroughly. She had fed afterward and swept and split a week's worth of kindling from the pile behind the barn, in the dark, by feel, until the kindling pile was stacked and there was nothing left to do with her hands. Then she had come out to the farmyard and stood.

The four Germans had come out of the icehouse at ten minutes to nine.

Each carried a kit bag — not much in any of them; she knew the shape of these bags by now, the rough khaki canvas with the stenciled letters, the few things that had accumulated over thirteen months: issued clothes, extra socks, whatever photographs or letters had made their way in. Dieter and Rolf stood near the gate with their bags over their shoulders, talking low about something she could not hear. Hans was squatting over his bag adjusting the clasp. Karl was standing at the fence corner.

He had something folded in his hand. She looked at it once — a list, parallel lines, two across — and then looked at the gate.

Lonzo was at the icehouse door without the carbine. He had left it inside on its rack, or she assumed he had; it was not at his side and not in his hands. He stood with his arms loosely folded and watched the top of the gatepost.

The truck came through.

---

Two MPs in the cab, a younger one driving and an older one with a clipboard. The older one got out and stood by the truck door and read four names from the list in the carrying voice of a man who has done this enough times that he no longer hears the names as names: *Reinmar. Steinbach. Huber. Bauer.* Each man answered. The MP made his marks. That was all of the official proceeding — it took less time than she had spent in the farmyard waiting for it.

The tailgate came down.

Dieter swung himself up first, easy with it, a man accustomed to loading equipment. Then Hans, then Rolf. Karl picked up his kit bag and turned.

She was already moving.

She had not decided to move. Her feet were crossing the farmyard and her hand had the notebook in it — drawn from her apron pocket in the same motion.

Lonzo had turned to look at the cab. He was looking at the cab with the complete and particular quality of a person who has decided something is not their business — so fully decided it that the looking-away was as deliberate as if he had said the words aloud.

She had perhaps four minutes.

---

She held out the notebook.

He looked at it — the soft worn covers, the water stain at the lower back, the shape that had lived in her apron pocket all through the summer and now lived in her outstretched hand. He took it with both hands: one at the cover and one at the spine.

He held it against his chest.

He reached into his breast pocket.

She had not expected it. But she could see immediately that it had been waiting — the paper came out with the ease of something placed there in advance, folded with the deliberate care of something put in the breast pocket to stay. Behind the note, briefly visible, the edge of his own list: the parallel columns, the two-language record she had only glimpsed and never held. He drew the note out and held it toward her with one hand, the other still pressing the notebook to his chest.

She took the note.

She put it in her apron pocket without reading it, because reading was not what you did with four minutes, and she would read it when she had a room and a lamp and the time to hold whatever it said.

---

They stood with a foot of November air between them.

Thirteen months. The first October, the word *Seidenschwanz* coming out of his mouth in the dark and going nowhere, not knowing where to go; then her writing it in the margin of her homework at the kitchen table because if she wrote it down it would stay. January, the grain bin, *Schnee* back and forth in the cold, the word for snow from a country that had twenty kinds of need for it. The cedar waxwing landing on the water trough in the early thaw and both of them saying its name at the same moment and neither one having noticed, until that instant, that they both had it. August, the barn, the haywork smell, *Werner. Von Ansbach. Er wollte kommen.* His hands resting on the stacked bales. The tense.

His face had settled into something she found harder to read now than in the first weeks — not harder in the way of concealment, but harder in the way of depth, of something that had gone farther in. Twenty, and the year in it: the outdoor cold, the fieldwork weight, the year of being somewhere that was not home and would not become it. She had been watching this face since October of last year, across a water bucket and a fence post and a notebook open on a fence rail, and she could account for most of its particulars: the line of his jaw, the set of his eyes, the small scar at the outer edge of his right eyebrow she had first noticed in February. She looked at all of it.

She did not have the German for what was in his face. She did not have the English. The grammar they had built between them — the nouns and the birds and the weather words and the verbs of the farm — had been assembled around exactly this kind of thing without being adequate to name it.

He said: *Hah-nah.*

The shape he had always given her name: the first syllable lifted, the *a* broader and lower than the American version, the second syllable brief and final. She had not corrected it in thirteen months and did not now.

Behind her the MP said something.

Karl looked at her for one more second.

Then he stepped up into the truck bed, sat on the bench along the wall, set the notebook on his knee. The tailgate went up.

---

She stood in the farmyard and watched the truck turn.

It was slow through the yard, finding its way through the ruts and the gate and the cattle guard. Through the slats she could see them on the bench: Dieter with his arms crossed and his eyes closed, Hans looking at the truck bed boards, Rolf with his hands on his kit bag. Karl had the notebook flat on his knee and his eyes on the farmyard. She could not tell, through the slats and the November gray, whether he could see her where she stood.

The truck went through the gate and the cattle guard slowed it again and then it straightened onto the farm road and the bare tree line swallowed it.

She turned.

Her father was on the porch.

She had not heard him come out — he had been in the barn before light, in the milking parlor, in the equipment shed before that; she had not tracked his morning and had not expected to find him here. He was standing on the top porch step in his barn coat with his hands at his sides, watching the gate where the truck had been. She stood in the farmyard. He stood on the porch. The farm was quieter already than it had been at eight o'clock — a quality in the air, an absence with a specific sound to it.

His face had the quality she had spent her life learning to read. Not empty — her father's face had not been empty since she could first remember looking at it carefully enough to know. Closed, in the way of a man who has been holding something for a long time and carries the shape of the holding in his expression the way a hand carries the shape of whatever it has gripped.

He watched the gate a moment longer.

Then he went inside.

---

*That evening.*

She sat at the kitchen table after supper and took the note from her apron pocket and unfolded it on the oilcloth.

The paper was the back of an envelope. Through the reverse she could see the faint impression of the address, canceled through once with a pen line. His handwriting: the English formed in the European way, each letter separated and upright, shaped with the specific care of a man working in a language not his own. She understood that he had written this in English because it was for her — in German it would have been easier.

*Werner Grau. From Ansbach. He is dead. I am sorry.*

She read it. She read it again.

Below the four sentences, in two columns, five birds in the format she had used in the notebook: the German name on the left, the Latin on the right, the letters smaller and more careful than the sentences above.

*Seidenschwanz — Bombycilla cedrorum*
*Rotkehlchen — Turdus migratorius*
*Blaumeise — Poecile atricapillus*
*Habicht — Accipiter gentilis*
*Sperber — Falco sparverius*

The cedar waxwing at the top of the list, and the four others below it.

She looked at the list for a long time.

He had known since before August. Longer — she thought since the winter, the way his hands had stilled on the bale that morning in August before he spoke the name, the stillness of someone saying a thing they have carried through several seasons and are only now finding the occasion for. He had been carrying it in the same breast pocket as the bird list, the two things together through the months of fieldwork and the months of cold: the names of the birds they had made between them, and the name of the man.

The apology — *I am sorry* — was not for Werner's death. It was for this: for the knowledge now in her hands, full weight, past tense, Werner Grau who had come from Ansbach and made it most of the way and the rest of which she had been holding in pieces since October of last year. Karl had kept his piece alone until August, until the barn, until the four minutes this morning, and the apology was for the landing of it.

She folded the note on its original lines.

She put it in her pocket.

---

She found her father in the sitting room with his accounts.

The ledger was open on the small table by the window, the lamp lit at the table's edge, and his pen was moving in the columns. He did not look up when she came to the doorway. The lamp made its small circle on the ledger and on his hands; the rest of the room was the dark of a November evening, the corners of the furniture going gray and soft, the rag rug at the foot of the stairs visible only as its shape.

She stood in the doorway.

The German came not because she chose it. It was the language the sentence required. The language of the household's keeping. The one they had been keeping it in, without naming the keeping, for over a year: in German at the grain bin in January, in German at the dinner table the night her father's hands stopped above his plate, in German in the tin box where the photograph had been waiting with its two faces and the year below them.

*Werner Grau ist tot.*

Her father did not move.

The pen was still. His other hand, flat on the open ledger, was still also. She counted without choosing to count: one, two, three, four, five. The pen above the paper. His hand flat on the page. Six, seven, eight, nine, ten.

He looked up at her.

*Ich weiß.*

Two words. The voice level, the cadence of something said that has been carried for a long time and was less heavy in the saying than in the holding. He said it simply, and he looked at her with it in his face — not relief, exactly; not any clean word she had for it.

He held her eyes a moment.

Then he looked back at the ledger.

---

She did not ask what happened.

The question did not come. There was no German for it that arrived at the doorway, no English, nothing in the grammar they shared or didn't share that wanted to be the next thing said. The silence after *Ich weiß* had the quality of a silence that closes rather than opens. This one was finished.

She nodded once.

Then she turned and went back to the kitchen.

The water in the pot was still warm from supper. She filled the basin and did the dishes — the supper plates first, then the cups, then the pot — working through them in the order she always worked through them, the warm water and the sound of it. Her hands in the basin. The dish towel. When the last cup was on the drying rack she dried her hands and stood at the window above the sink.

Through the glass she could see the farmyard.

The icehouse was dark.

Not the low settled dark of a lamp turned down for the night — the kind of light that had been visible through those windows on any night she had looked toward the icehouse since October of last year, the steady small warmth of the bunkhouse occupied. Dark in the way it had not been dark in thirteen months: the windows blank, the lamp not lit, nobody in the bunkhouse and no light to say that someone was there. The icehouse stood where it had always stood, the same gray boards and the same roofline against the November sky, and the farm was quieter than it had been before nine o'clock that morning.

The lamp was not lit.
