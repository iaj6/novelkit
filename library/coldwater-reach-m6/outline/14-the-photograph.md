# Chapter 14 — The Photograph

**One-line summary**: Hannah finds a photograph in her father's tin box labeled "W. Grau, A. Pfeiffer, Bamberg 1921"; at dinner she says the name aloud and her father goes still.

**POV**: Hannah Pfeiffer  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Setting**: Pfeiffer farmhouse — upstairs rooms, kitchen  
**Time**: April 1944 (Hannah is 17, about to turn 18)  
**Target words**: ~3,000 (intensive chapter — the inciting moment for Hannah's thread of the mystery)

**Chapter shape**: Single scene  
**Ending mode**: Turn

---

## Goal
Give Hannah her piece of the mystery: the photograph proves Werner Grau knew Albrecht before America; the dinner scene proves Albrecht knows who Werner Grau is and what happened to him. This is the chapter where Hannah moves from passive noticing to active carrying.

## Obstacle
Hannah is doing ordinary housework (laundry sorting) in her parents' room — a completely ordinary activity. What she finds is unexpected and unasked-for. The obstacle is not finding the photograph; it is what to do with it, and then: she says the name at dinner before she has decided what to do, and the result frightens her.

---

## Opening texture
Observation: Hannah in her parents' room, sorting laundry from the basket. Ordinary task, authorized presence. She is reaching for a stack of folded shirts when the tin box underneath it shifts.

---

## Action beats

1. **The laundry sorting**: Hannah is methodical in her mother's room — this is her mother's domain; she enters it with the particular care of a child who has learned to take up no more space than she needs. Magda is downstairs. The room smells of cedar and the particular cold of an upstairs in winter that has been heated sparingly.

2. **The tin box**: The tin box falls out from under the shifted stack — not dramatically, just the ordinary physics of a poorly balanced shelf. She catches it, sets it on the bed. She has seen it before; it has always been there; she has never opened it.

3. **What's inside**: Letters, a few documents, and at the bottom a small stack of photographs. She tells herself she is making sure nothing is damaged. She looks.

4. **The photograph**: Two men in front of what appears to be a building in a European city — a civic building, or a church entrance. Both in their early thirties, European dress. Penciled on the back: "Bamberg, 1921." And below that, in her father's hand: two names. One she recognizes immediately: *A. Pfeiffer.* The other she does not recognize: *W. Grau.*

5. **The ordinary man**: She looks at the face of W. Grau. He is middle-aged even then — perhaps older than her father in the photograph. He has a laborer's build. His face is ordinary: neither kind nor unkind, neither remarkable nor forgettable. She will not be able to pick it out of a crowd. She puts the photograph back, puts everything back, closes the tin box, replaces it.

6. **All day**: She carries the name all day. W. Grau. It means nothing to her. No face, no story, no connection to anything. Just a name from 1921 in Bamberg. She has never heard her father mention it.

7. **Dinner**: Albrecht, Magda, Hannah. The usual. And then, not because she planned to, Hannah says it aloud: "Papa — who is W. Grau?" She isn't sure why she said it; perhaps because she has been carrying it all day and the weight of a held question is different from the weight of silence.

8. **Albrecht goes still**: Her father's hands stop on his utensils. Not for long — a second, two seconds. He resumes eating. He does not answer. Magda, who almost never looks up, looks up. Her eyes find Hannah's. Something passes through Magda's look that Hannah cannot read — not accusation, not grief; perhaps simply the look of a woman who has been waiting for this question for a long time.

---

## Outcome / turn
Albrecht has not answered the question. But his stillness is an answer of a kind. W. Grau is not a name from a casual friendship. His hands stopped. Magda looked up. Hannah has introduced a name into the household that was already there, invisible, pressing against the walls.

---

## Ending (turn)
No one speaks for the rest of dinner. Hannah does the dishes. When she passes the table, the bread plate is where it always is. Her father's accounts book is open at the usual page. Nothing is different. Everything is different. She has said a name and something moved.

---

## New question raised
Why does that name stop Albrecht cold — and what does Magda know that she looked up for?

---

## Continuity notes
- The photograph: "W. Grau, A. Pfeiffer, Bamberg, 1921" — these details are established and must be consistent when referenced in later chapters (ch. 20, ch. 28)
- Hannah does NOT know who W. Grau is relative to the body on the breakwater; she simply found a name and said it; the connection is not available to her yet
- Magda's look: a rare moment of presence from Magda; it should be noted and left; do not explain it; Magda knows about Werner Grau and has been carrying this since October; this look is her only acknowledgment in the novel
- Hannah's age: she will turn 18 in the spring/summer of 1944; this chapter is April, so she is still 17; her vulnerability and her courage in the dinner scene are both present
- The tin box is in her PARENTS' room, not Hannah's; she does not take the photograph; she looks at it and replaces it; she holds only the name and the image
- The ordinary face of Werner Grau: do not make him striking or memorable; he is a man whose importance came from his connections, not his appearance
