# Chapter 13 — Displaced Scholars

**One-line summary**: Marcus researches Werner Grau through a Columbia colleague; James Crow's letter arrives; Grau's illegal status as of 1940 is confirmed.

**POV**: Dr. Marcus Klein  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Setting**: Coldwater Reach High School (classroom after hours) → Henry and Lillian's house  
**Time**: March 1944  
**Target words**: ~2,600

**Chapter shape**: Two-scene with transition  
**Ending mode**: Turn

---

## Goal
Advance Marcus's thread of the mystery: he establishes Werner Grau's identity through archival-adjacent means (a letter to a colleague who would know); receives the confirmation that Grau was illegal after 1940; and plants the parallel between Grau's situation and his own wartime illegality. Also: James Crow's letter and the household warmth of Henry and Lillian.

## Obstacle
Marcus has no legitimate access to government or military records. He can only ask someone who might know someone who kept track of the displaced. This indirection is both his limitation and his particular expertise — he is a man of networks and letters, not of official channels.

---

## Opening texture
Routine-procedure: Marcus after school, marking papers. Hannah's translation paper is on top of the stack; he registers its quality precisely. The chapter opens on the work before the private research.

---

## Action beats (Scene 1 — classroom)

1. **Marking papers**: Marcus works through the Advanced German translations; Hannah's is notable for a particular quality — she has found the right subordination structure, not just the correct words. He writes a marginal note: *The grammar of the subordinate clause is not decoration — it is the shape of what is being withheld.* He is, slightly, talking about himself.

2. **Writing to Sondheimer**: He writes a careful letter to Dr. Heinrich Sondheimer at Columbia — a man he knows from the Emergency Committee rolls, who was on the intake end of the rescue network and might have records, or access to records, of displaced European scholars and artists who passed through the system. Marcus describes what he is looking for: a Werner Grau, German national, last known address Portland, Maine; he is trying to establish whether the man was ever on the Committee rolls, or any similar register, and whether his status in America was documented. He frames it as a scholarly question of administrative record. It is not entirely a lie.

3. **The parallel he notices**: Writing the letter, he becomes aware that he is asking, about Werner Grau, approximately the same question that could be asked about Marcus Klein in 1940 — a man without the right papers, in a country not designed for him, trying to find a connection before the connection becomes impossible. He does not write this in the letter to Sondheimer. He does not write it anywhere. He seals the letter.

---

## Transition

*Evening at Henry and Lillian's.* Marcus comes in to find a letter from James on the kitchen table. Lillian has read it; Henry has read it; both of them are at the kitchen table with the particular quality of relief that is also anxiety — James is well, somewhere in the Pacific that cannot be named, the operations are going forward. The tone of James's letters has been careful but readable to his parents, who know the space between what is said and what is not.

---

## Action beats (Scene 2 — kitchen)

4. **James's letter**: Lillian reads a portion of it aloud — the safe parts. A description of food. A joke about the mail delivery. An inquiry about the boiler. Marcus listens and thinks: this is the shape of family at distance — the maintenance of the ordinary against what cannot be named. He has written letters like this.

5. **Henry and the photograph**: Henry puts James's letter under the kitchen clock, where letters are kept. He looks at the photograph on the wall — James in his uniform. Henry does not say anything. This is, for Marcus, one of the most expressive silences he has observed: a man looking at his son's photograph and saying nothing because everything that could be said is in the looking.

6. **Sondheimer's reply**: (A few days later, same chapter.) Marcus's inquiry to Sondheimer was answered faster than expected — Sondheimer made a telephone call to someone who made another, and the answer came back: Werner Grau, German national, painter/laborer, entered the United States in 1936 on a visa sponsored by an arts organization in Boston. The visa expired in 1940. After that: no renewal, no deportation, no record. He has been in the country without legal status for four years.

---

## Outcome / turn
Marcus has confirmation: Werner Grau was an illegal resident of the United States as of 1940. A man who had been living here, without papers, and came to Coldwater in October 1943, and died. The parallel between Grau and Marcus's own Sherbrooke internment — enemy alien, uncertain status, the condition of being in a country that had not decided what to do with you — is now not just felt but concrete.

---

## Ending (turn)
He puts Sondheimer's reply in the desk drawer with the original Grau letter. He sits with the drawer closed. Werner Grau was illegal. This is a fact. This is also the kind of fact that, if reported to anyone official, would explain everything about the body on the breakwater except what matters. Marcus is not going to report it anywhere. He has not yet decided if this constitutes his doing something.

---

## New question raised
Is Marcus's decision not to report Werner Grau's status an act of solidarity, an act of cowardice, or simply an act of a man who knows that what matters about Werner Grau is not his legal status?

---

## Continuity notes
- Dr. Heinrich Sondheimer: named figure at Columbia, Emergency Committee connection; appears only in letters; does not need further development
- The Grau illegal-status fact: Werner's visa expired 1940; this is now established in Marcus's knowledge; it means if the Coast Guard had identified the body, Grau's status would have become an issue
- The parallel to Sherbrooke: Marcus was interned as an "enemy alien" (Austrian = German territory); his status was also uncertain; he does not say this parallel aloud, but it is the chapter's emotional undertow
- James Crow's letter: warm, present, full of what is not said — this is the first time we see James's voice (through quotation); his relationship with his parents should feel real; his situation in the Pacific is not dramatized (the war is in the background)
- Henry's silence looking at the photograph: do not gloss it; leave it as image
