# Chapter 25 — The Proceeding

**One-line summary**: Formal harbor authority proceeding imminent; Eira prepares eight years of records the night before; the vote is two weeks away.

**POV**: Eira Bowman  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Setting**: Harbormaster's shed  
**Time**: November 1944  
**Target words**: ~2,500

**Chapter shape**: Single scene  
**Ending mode**: Mid-action

---

## Goal
Raise the harbor authority proceeding to its formal and imminent stage; show Eira preparing with the total competence she has always brought to her work; leave her on the night before (metaphorically) — the proceeding itself happens in ch. 29. This chapter is about the preparation, and about what the preparation costs her that she does not acknowledge.

## Obstacle
The proceeding is formally about her "appointment status" — a procedural challenge — but its real stakes are the eight years of work she has done without formal acknowledgment. She cannot address the real challenge in the proceeding room; she can only address the procedural one and hope the procedural answer is enough.

---

## Opening texture
Single scene: Eira in the shed, after receiving the formal notice. This is not a day of harbor activity; it is an evening of preparation. She is alone with her records and what they represent.

---

## Action beats

1. **The formal notice**: Eira has received a letter from the county harbor authority — the county board has convened a formal proceeding to review her "acting" appointment. The language is careful: it is a "review," not a challenge; the outcome is not predetermined; she has the right to present her record. The date is two weeks away.

2. **What the proceeding actually means**: She has been the harbormaster for eight years. In those eight years: no casualty attributable to her decisions; the harbor income has increased; the vessel safety record is the best in the county. None of this was asked for; she did it because it was her job. The proceeding exists because someone with political weight wants the post for someone else, and the procedural vehicle available to them is the never-formalized appointment.

3. **Preparing the record**: She lays out eight years of harbor logs on the desk. She goes through them: the mooring records, the fuel ledgers, the safety inspection files, the registry. Everything in order. She prepares a summary document.

4. **Owen's log**: She gets to 1935. Owen's last entry; her first. She reads them again — not sentimentally, but for the record: the continuity. She will bring this to the proceeding. Not as an emotional argument; as a document.

5. **The drawer**: She opens the middle drawer to get a blank form. The Portland letter and the CG form are there: W. Grau, 114 Franklin Street, Portland. She looks at them for a moment, then puts them aside. They are not related to the proceeding — except that they are: both are things sitting in the wrong column, and the proceeding is the same thing in a different register. Something that should have been settled and wasn't. Something she has to answer for without having done anything wrong.

6. **What she does not let herself think**: She does not let herself think about what happens if she loses. She does not have the capacity for that calculation tonight. She does the preparation. She stacks the records. She checks the summary document. She is not done.

---

## Ending (mid-action)
The oil lamp is low; she refills it. Outside, November on the harbor — dark water, the bell buoy faint from here. She spreads the summary document on the desk and picks up her pen. She is not done preparing. The proceeding is two weeks away. She works.

---

## New question raised
What will she say in the proceeding — and is the record enough to answer a challenge that is not really about the record?

---

## Continuity notes
- The formal proceeding notice: now it is official, dated, two weeks away; the proceeding itself happens in ch. 29
- The summary document: Eira is preparing her own brief for the proceeding; this is consistent with her competence and her way of working
- The Portland letter (W. Grau): its presence in the drawer alongside her harbor records is deliberate — two unlabeled columns, both of which she will resolve in ch. 29 when she makes the harbor log entry
- Owen's 1935 log: established in ch. 04 (she chose not to write the dock figures) and ch. 11 (she showed it to Tarbell); now she reads it again as documentation; it carries its weight without comment
- Mid-action ending: this is one of the novel's two mid-action endings (the guidance allows ~2); it is placed here because the proceeding's resolution is deferred to ch. 29, and the chapter should end inside the preparation rather than at a natural pause
