# Chapter 20 — Field Birds

**One-line summary**: Late summer haywork; Karl and Hannah's pidgin at its fullest; Karl says "Werner. Von Ansbach" and uses the past tense; Hannah understands.

**POV**: Hannah Pfeiffer  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Setting**: Pfeiffer farm — hayfields, barn  
**Time**: August 1944  
**Target words**: ~2,600

**Chapter shape**: Two-scene with transition  
**Ending mode**: Turn

---

## Goal
The Hannah/Karl thread reaches its emotional peak: in the context of the haywork and their established pidgin, Karl names Werner Grau — and Hannah's response to the past tense she hears connects what Karl is saying to the name in her father's tin box. This is the chapter where the two threads (Hannah's photograph, Karl's private knowledge) converge.

## Obstacle
The language barrier is the obstacle — and also the vehicle. Karl can barely say what he means in English; Hannah can barely say what she means in German. The name comes through this barrier, and the past tense comes through, and that is enough.

---

## Opening texture
Routine-procedure: hay harvest in August. Physical, sweaty, loud with the machinery's noise. Hannah and the POWs working in the same field. The summer heat and the smell of cut grass. This is one of the chapter's best textures — the sensory fullness of the farm in August.

---

## Action beats (Scene 1 — the hayfield)

1. **Harvest work**: The haying is underway; Albrecht drives the tractor; the POWs pitch and stack; Hannah runs the water bucket. The work is too loud for anything but gesture and short words. The August heat is complete.

2. **The bird taxonomy, expanded**: During a water break, Hannah shows Karl the notebook she has been keeping — German bird names with English equivalents, now twenty-some entries, accumulated over ten months. He looks through it with a quality of attention she has not seen in him before: something like recognition. These are his words, rendered in her hand, in both languages, in a notebook.

3. **The language between them**: Their pidgin now has verbs; she can say *kommen* and he can say "come"; they have assembled, not from textbooks but from necessity, approximately eighty words. She teaches him "hawk." He teaches her *Sperber* (sparrow hawk). They compare. The comparison is, as always, a kind of equality.

4. **What he notices about the notebook**: He looks at the first entry: *Seidenschwanz / Cedar Waxwing.* He remembers saying it in the dark, to himself. He looks at her. She says: "October." One word; she means: she heard it in October 1943, the first month, and wrote it down. He understands.

---

## Transition

*Late afternoon, after the last hayload.* The barn, cooling down. The other POWs have gone back to the icehouse. Lonzo is twenty yards away, by the fence. Hannah and Karl are stacking hay bales on the wagon — close enough for a quiet exchange that is not, quite, within Lonzo's clear hearing.

---

## Action beats (Scene 2 — the barn)

5. **Werner. Von Ansbach.**: Karl says it looking at the fence — not at Hannah. Two words, a name: "Werner. Von Ansbach." He does not say: I know something. He does not say: my family knew him. He says the name and the place, and then, after a pause: *Er wollte kommen.* He wanted to come. The past tense.

6. **Hannah's receipt**: She does not know this name, in this moment — and then she does: *W. Grau. A. Pfeiffer. Bamberg, 1921.* W. Grau. Werner Grau. Werner, from Ansbach. She does not show what she is assembling, but her stillness shows it. Karl is watching the fence and does not see her face change.

7. **What she does**: She says, in German: *Du weißt.* You know. Not a question. Karl nods once. He looks at the fence. She does not ask what he knows, because she understands that they are both using the past tense for the same person, and both of them know why.

8. **The field birds**: A meadowlark lands on the fence post where Karl is looking. Neither of them says its name. It lifts and is gone.

---

## Ending (turn)
Hannah walks back to the house. The bird notebook is in her apron pocket, the weight of it light and specific. What she is carrying is no longer a name and a photograph. It is a person — Werner Grau, who wanted to come, who came and didn't come back, whose name stopped her father's hands in October 1943 and again in April 1944 at the dinner table. Turn: she knows now that Karl knew him. She knows now that her father and Karl are connected through a man who died on the breakwater.

---

## New question raised
What is Karl carrying about Werner Grau — grief? Guilt? And what does Hannah do with this connection: does she go to her father again, or does she wait?

---

## Continuity notes
- "Werner. Von Ansbach." — Karl says first name and provenance; Hannah connects it to "W. Grau" from the photograph because she knows Werner Grau was from near Ansbach (Bamberg is close; same region); the connection is sound even though the full name isn't given yet
- Karl says *Er wollte kommen* (past tense: "he wanted to come"); Hannah hears the past tense and understands it as confirmation of what she suspects
- The bird notebook: established in ch. 08 (she begins it) and ch. 05 (she writes *Seidenschwanz*); in this chapter it is the physical token of their relationship — the shared vocabulary, rendered in both languages, in her hand
- Lonzo is twenty yards away; the exchange is just outside his clear hearing; he is aware something is passing but does not intervene; his own chapter (ch. 21) follows next, and he has his own private knowledge
- Hannah does NOT go to her father in this chapter; the confrontation with Albrecht comes in ch. 24 (Transfer), after Karl's departure, mediated through Karl's written note
