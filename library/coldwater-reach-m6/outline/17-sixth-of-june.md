# Chapter 17 — Sixth of June

**One-line summary**: D-Day on the farm radio; Albrecht says, in English, "It will be over soon"; Hannah repeats his words to Karl through the icehouse door.

**POV**: Hannah Pfeiffer  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Setting**: Pfeiffer farmhouse kitchen → icehouse bunkhouse  
**Time**: June 6–7, 1944  
**Target words**: ~2,500

**Chapter shape**: Two-scene with transition  
**Ending mode**: Literary fade

---

## Goal
The D-Day news as it reaches the farm — not as history lesson but as the specific emotional texture it has for each person in this household. Albrecht's return to English, however briefly. Hannah's transmission of his words to Karl: what it means to carry a message between two people who cannot speak to each other.

## Obstacle
Hannah does not know what D-Day means for the war's end, or for Karl, or for her parents. She is eighteen now. She has been living inside a conflict whose human dimensions she has only partially apprehended. This chapter is the moment when the war's far distance collapses briefly into the kitchen.

---

## Opening texture
Summons: the radio before dawn. Hannah wakes to her father's voice from the kitchen — not calling her, just speaking, which is unusual enough to wake her. She comes downstairs to find the BC-1206 on.

---

## Action beats (Scene 1 — kitchen)

1. **Before dawn**: Hannah comes downstairs in the dark to find her father standing at the kitchen table, the radio on. The voice on the radio is formal and steady: the landings in Normandy are confirmed; Allied forces have established beachheads; operations are continuing. Her father is in his work clothes; he has been up since before her.

2. **Magda arrives**: Her mother comes down ten minutes later, drawn by the sound. She stands in the doorway. She does not move into the kitchen. The radio goes on.

3. **Albrecht's sentence**: They listen for perhaps forty minutes before her father says, in English — the first sustained English she has heard from him in months: "It will be over soon." He does not say this to either of them specifically; he says it into the room. Not a celebration; not relief exactly. The sentence of a man who has been waiting for something specific to happen, and now believes it is happening.

4. **Magda's response**: Magda looks at Albrecht. She says nothing. But she moves from the doorway into the kitchen — a step, two steps. She sits down at the table. This is the furthest she has come out of herself in a very long time.

5. **What Hannah feels**: She does not have the English for what she feels watching her parents in the early morning over the radio news. She has German: *Erleichterung* (relief, but also lightening — a weight lifted). She does not say it aloud.

---

## Transition

*That afternoon.* The radio has been on most of the day in the diner and the post office; the world is full of news. At the farm, Lonzo has a portable radio and the POWs have been permitted to hear the news. Hannah walks past the icehouse in the afternoon — she is not supposed to stop; she does not stop. But Karl is outside the door, sitting on the step. He looks at her.

---

## Action beats (Scene 2 — icehouse)

6. **The words between them**: Hannah stops a few yards from the icehouse step. She says — not knowing why she is saying it, saying her father's words, the one sentence in English he has offered in months: "It will be over soon." She says it quietly, not to Lonzo (who is inside), just to Karl.

7. **Karl's face**: He looks at her for a long moment. He nods once. He does not say what "over" means from where he is sitting. The same word means different things in different directions: for Albrecht, over means the Reich; for Karl, over means something whose shape he cannot quite see.

8. **The gesture**: Karl says, in the careful English of their pidgin: "My home." A statement, not a question. He is thinking about Ansbach. She understands this.

---

## Ending (literary fade)
The afternoon light in the icehouse doorway. The smell of turned earth from the fields, the first real summer heat. Hannah turns and walks back toward the barn. Behind her, she hears Karl say something in German — not to her, to himself. She cannot hear the word. *Es wird bald vorbei sein.* It will be over soon. The long summer grass in the fields beyond the fence.

---

## New question raised
What will "over" mean for Karl, for Albrecht, for the farm — and who decides?

---

## Continuity notes
- D-Day is June 6, 1944; Hannah is now 18 (turned 18 sometime in spring/early summer 1944)
- Albrecht's return to English: brief and significant; he says one sentence; it is not a resolution of his withdrawal, just a crack; he will not sustain it
- Magda's movement from the doorway to the table: similarly brief; the only moment of visible return in Magda's arc; it should be rendered without commentary
- Karl's "My home" in English: he is thinking about Ansbach; not whether Germany will win — that is not what he is carrying; he is thinking about whether home will be there
- Do NOT have this chapter make a historical statement about D-Day; the event is delivered through the radio voice and through what these specific people do with it
- The Pfeiffer household does NOT listen to the radio in German (established in the world bible); the BC-1206 delivers English-language news
