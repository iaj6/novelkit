# Chapter 29 — The Harbor Log

**One-line summary**: The proceeding finds in her favor; that evening she adds a single entry to the harbor log from October 1943: the two figures, the time, the weather. It goes nowhere. It is the record.

**POV**: Eira Bowman  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Setting**: County harbor authority meeting room → Coldwater Reach harbor → harbormaster's shed  
**Time**: March 1945  
**Target words**: ~3,000 (intensive chapter — Eira's arc completes)

**Chapter shape**: Two-scene with transition  
**Ending mode**: Resolved beat

---

## Goal
Eira wins the proceeding (the record is unanswerable) and, the same evening, makes the harbor log entry she has been withholding for seventeen months. These two acts — the official resolution of the authority challenge, and the private record she makes for no one but the log — are the completion of her arc. She has occupied the post; she has put the entry in the right column.

## Obstacle
The proceeding is the last formal obstacle to Eira's tenure. She has met every previous challenge with documentation; this is the final one. The proceeding scene is not the chapter's real challenge, though: the real challenge is what she does afterward, in the shed, alone with the harbor log. She could not have made that entry two years ago; now she can.

---

## Opening texture
Set-piece: the formal proceeding. Eira in front of the three-member county harbor authority panel. She has her records. She has done this before, in smaller ways. Now it is official.

---

## Action beats (Scene 1 — the proceeding)

1. **The panel**: Three members of the county harbor authority: a retired vessel inspector (who is not hostile), the county board member who initiated the review (who is), and a third person (neutral, local, who knows the harbor). They are in the county building in Rockland, not Coldwater — the proceeding is formal, not local.

2. **Eira's presentation**: She presents her record. The harbor logs. The mooring registry. The fuel allocation ledger. The safety inspections. Eight years of documentation. She does not argue; she shows. The numbers say what she wants said.

3. **The question about continuity**: The initiating board member asks about the "continuity of appointment" — whether her "acting" status was ever formally confirmed. She presents the 1935 county letter appointing her pending review, and the fact that the review was never conducted. She notes that the harbor has been safely operated under her management for eight years without any formal irregularity being identified. She says this without affect.

4. **The lobsterman's nod**: While waiting in the corridor, a lobsterman she has worked with for ten years passes through on some other business. He sees her and nods: the simple nod of professional acknowledgment. It is worth more than anything the panel could say.

5. **The vote**: The panel deliberates. She waits. The vote: two to one to retain her appointment and formalize it. The holdout is the initiating board member. The others: the record is the record.

6. **The drive back**: She drives back to Coldwater along the coast road. March in Maine: patches of snow, the sea visible between the headlands. She does not feel what she might be expected to feel. The appointment was always hers, in the sense that the work was always hers. The paper now matches the work. This is tidy, not triumphant.

---

## Transition

*That evening, back at the shed.* She opens the shed. The harbor is quiet — March, most of the fleet not yet out; the breakwater dark in the late afternoon. She sits at her desk.

---

## Action beats (Scene 2 — the harbor log)

7. **The drawer**: She opens the middle drawer. The Portland letter (W. Grau, 114 Franklin Street) and the unanswered CG form. She reads them both. She has had the name for six months. She has done nothing with it. She has not reported it; she has not made the connection public; she has simply held it, as she held the dock observation for seventeen months.

8. **What she decides**: She will not send the CG form. The case is closed — officially, months ago. Werner Grau, if that is who the body was, has no one left to claim him or pursue the question of his death. Reporting the name now would not serve Werner Grau. It would serve the machinery of record. She has served the machinery of record for eight years; she knows when it serves a purpose and when it doesn't.

9. **The harbor log, 1943**: She gets down the 1943 log from the shelf. She opens it to October. She finds the October 12 entry — the official entry she wrote that morning: body discovered, CG notified, CG responded, body removed. Precise and complete except for one thing.

10. **The entry she writes**: Below the official entry, in her precise hand, in the space available:

    *0055: two men observed south dock. One unknown. One familiar by gait, farm-type. Neither vessel registered in port. Weather deteriorating.*

    This is what she saw. It is true. It is minimal. It names nothing and no one. It is in the log.

11. **Closing the log**: She closes it. She replaces it on the shelf next to Owen's. The log is the record of the harbor. Everything that happens at this harbor goes into the log. She does not send this anywhere. It is not for anyone but the log. It is in the right column.

---

## Outcome / resolved beat
Eira has occupied the post. She has made the record she should have made. She did not tell anyone; she did not report anyone; she did not solve anything. She filed the entry correctly, seventeen months late, with no one to see it. This is what the post required of its holder.

---

## Ending (resolved beat)
She locks the shed and walks down the pier in the March air. The harbor is hers: the water, the boats, the granite breakwater, the tide table, the fuel dock, the mooring registry, the harbor log. Hers and no one else's, formally, finally. The entry is in the log. The tide is going out; she can tell by the sound. She walks back up the pier and goes home.

---

## New question raised
Nothing remains unasked in Eira's arc. One thread is deliberately unresolved: Albrecht's account of what happened between him and Werner that night. Eira has written "farm-type" in the log. She does not know it is Albrecht. She will not know. This is the shadow that stays.

## Continuity notes
- The harbor log entry: "0055: two men observed south dock. One unknown. One familiar by gait, farm-type. Neither vessel registered in port. Weather deteriorating." — these are the exact words; if the drafter changes them slightly for prose rhythm, the substance must be consistent
- The vote: two to one; the formal appointment is now confirmed; this closes the harbor board thread opened in ch. 07
- The Portland letter: Eira does NOT use the name W. Grau to connect to Marcus's inquiry about the Pfeiffer family (ch. 18); she does not have that connection; she simply holds the name and the knowledge that the dinghy was rented by someone named W. Grau; she decides not to report it because it serves no one
- "Farm-type" in the log: the second figure, whom Eira half-recognized by gait as someone with a farmer's build, is Albrecht Pfeiffer; Eira does not know this; the reader can infer it; this is precisely the shadow that stays
- Period note: formal county harbor authority proceedings in wartime Maine would be at the county seat or the nearest substantial county facility; Rockland is plausible for mid-coast Knox County
