# Chapter 01 — The Breakwater

**One-line summary**: A body comes in on the breakwater after the three-day blow; Eira handles it procedurally, and alone afterward retrieves the image of the dock she has not yet put in the right column.

**POV**: Eira Bowman  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Setting**: Coldwater Reach harbor — breakwater, pier head, harbormaster's shed  
**Time**: October 12, 1943 (morning after the storm clears)  
**Target words**: ~3,000 (intensive opening chapter)

**Chapter shape**: Single scene  
**Ending mode**: Turn

---

## Goal
Establish Eira's voice and competence; introduce the body as the novel's spine event; plant the dock image as the secret knowledge she is already holding.

## Obstacle
The body is a bureaucratic anomaly in a harbor she runs correctly. The CG takes it efficiently. There is no call for her to do anything except file it. The difficulty is internal: there is something she saw that does not belong in the standard column.

---

## Opening texture
Routine-procedure: Eira walks the breakwater in the post-storm morning, conducting her standard damage survey. The register establishes immediately — she thinks in load, draft, and tide. Weather, damage, what needs doing. The body is encountered in the course of correct procedure, not as drama.

---

## Action beats

1. **The survey**: Eira walks the breakwater at first light after the blow; she notes damage methodically (two iron mooring rings bent, granite face shifted at the elbow, kelp and cable draped over the top course). She has a clipboard. She uses it.

2. **The body**: At the elbow of the L — where the storm would deposit anything that came in from the southeast — a man is face-down in the wrack. Male, middle-aged or older, clothing not a fisherman's (town clothes, wool coat). No vessel, no dinghy, no gear. She does not touch him beyond checking the inside of the coat and the trouser pockets: no papers, no wallet, nothing.

3. **Procedure**: She goes back to the shed, calls the Coast Guard station (Sprucehead) on the telephone. She gives them the facts she has. She returns to the breakwater and waits.

4. **The CG arrival**: Lieutenant Mercer arrives with two enlisted men and a stretcher. He is young, efficient, and not especially curious. He asks her the standard questions; she answers with what she has. The body is loaded. Mercer thanks her. The exchange takes perhaps twelve minutes.

5. **Back to work**: She returns to the shed. She finishes the damage survey. She writes the harbor log entry: date, time, weather status, body discovered, CG notified, CG responded, body removed. Precise to the quarter-hour.

6. **The ledger and the image**: She sets down the clipboard. She stands at the window. The south dock — which she can see from here — is empty. And then, not dramatically, the way an incorrect entry surfaces when you run the numbers: the dock two nights ago. Before the blow peaked. Around midnight. Two figures on the south dock. One she did not recognize. The other she couldn't see clearly — but the build, the way the person stood, was not a sailor's. A farmer, maybe. Somebody from inland.

---

## Outcome / turn
The body is gone; the case is handed to the Coast Guard; the harbor is back under her management. But Eira has retrieved the image of the dock, and the image does not match the standard entry she just wrote. The body and the figure are, she suspects, the same man at different points in the same night. She has not written that down.

---

## New question raised
What did she actually see on the south dock, and who were those two figures?

---

## Continuity notes
- Establishes: October 1943 timeline; body has no papers; Coast Guard takes the body (Lt. Mercer, first appearance); Eira knows what she saw on the dock
- The figure she half-recognizes has a "farm-type" build — not yet identified; the drafter should not reveal it is Albrecht yet
- The harbor log entry is written precisely and omits the dock figures: this is established here so that chapter 29's addition to the log has full resonance
- Owen Bowman should be felt as an absence before he is mentioned — the harbor log from 1935 is on the shelf; Eira's eye passes it; no explanation needed
- Period texture: the breakwater, tidal range, October weather on the Maine coast; no 1942 U-boat speculation (that's behind them)
