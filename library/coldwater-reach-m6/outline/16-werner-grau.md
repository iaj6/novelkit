# Chapter 16 — Werner Grau

**One-line summary**: Marcus assembles the complete picture of who Werner Grau was; his parallel to Grau's illegal status surfaces; he folds the papers away and goes for a walk.

**POV**: Dr. Marcus Klein  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Setting**: Marcus's room in Henry and Lillian's house  
**Time**: May 1944  
**Target words**: ~2,500

**Chapter shape**: Single scene  
**Ending mode**: Elliptical

---

## Goal
Marcus reaches, in private, the limit of what he can know about Werner Grau from available evidence: a name, a legal status, a purpose (to reach Albrecht and Karl), a timing that puts him in Coldwater the night before the storm. He cannot prove the body was Grau; he will not report what he suspects; he has not decided if this constitutes something. The chapter is about what a man does when he reaches the end of what he can know.

## Obstacle
Marcus is a precise man who has assembled an imprecise account. He cannot close it — there are gaps — and he cannot report it without consequences he cannot control (Werner's illegal status; Albrecht's presence on the dock; Karl's situation at the farm). He does nothing, which is itself a choice, and he knows it.

---

## Opening texture
Interior: Marcus at his desk in his room, with the papers laid out. The chapter opens on the act of assembly — methodical, academic — before the emotional content of the assembly becomes apparent.

---

## Action beats

1. **The materials on the desk**: The two pieces in his possession: Werner Grau's letter to Albrecht (September 28, 1943); Sondheimer's reply confirming Grau's illegal status after 1940 (returned March 1944). He has laid them out. He has also written, on a sheet of school paper, what he knows:

   - Werner Grau, German national, born perhaps 1890–1900 (estimate from the letter's tone and Albrecht's age as approximate peer)
   - In America 1936–1943, legal until 1940, illegal after
   - Painter/laborer, last known address Portland
   - Knew Albrecht Pfeiffer before America; knew the Reinmar family in Ansbach
   - Planned to travel to Coldwater in October 1943 to find Albrecht and leave a message for Karl
   - The body on the breakwater: unidentified male, no papers, October 12, 1943

2. **The inference**: Marcus writes, below the last item: *Plausible account: W. Grau rented a vessel in Portland, traveled up the coast, arrived at Coldwater, met or attempted to meet A. Pfeiffer, was on the south waterfront the night of October 11–12, died — by drowning, by the storm, by some combination — before the storm's peak.* He circles "by some combination." He cannot close this.

3. **The parallel**: He has been aware of it since March; in May it is fully present. Werner Grau in 1940 was, legally, what Marcus Klein was in 1940: a German-category national without valid status in an Allied country. Marcus was interned at Sherbrooke; Werner was not found, or was found and tolerated, or was simply invisible in the marginal economy of Portland. The difference between them is not character or capacity. It is, partly, the country — Canada interned; America looked away. And partly luck.

4. **What he cannot do**: He cannot bring this account to the Coast Guard — Werner's illegal status would complicate everything; Albrecht would be implicated; Karl's situation at the farm would become vulnerable. He cannot bring it to anyone official. He is not, himself, fully official.

5. **What he will not do**: He will not suppress it deliberately. He will simply hold it. This is the action available to him: to be the person who knows the plausible account and holds it accurately, without acting on it and without pretending not to have it.

6. **The papers put away**: He folds the letter, the Sondheimer reply, and his own sheet of inferences and puts them in the bottom drawer of the desk, under the Latin dictionary where the letter began. He locks the drawer.

---

## Ending (elliptical)
He goes for a walk. The spring rain on the street outside Henry's house; the smell of the harbor, three streets over. He walks to the end of the street and back. He does not go to the harbor. He does not go to the school. He goes back inside. The chapter is the walk and the return: the gesture of a man who has reached the end of what he can do with what he knows, and continues to live in its company.

---

## New question raised
Is holding the plausible account without acting on it — without reporting it, without suppressing it — a form of justice, or a form of cowardice, or simply the only thing available?

## Continuity notes
- This chapter establishes Marcus's assembled account as a private document in his desk drawer; this document is what he will hold until Hannah gives him the last piece in ch. 28
- The inference on the sheet of paper: "by some combination" around the cause of death — Werner may have been unwell; the storm was the occasion, not necessarily the sole cause; Marcus cannot know this from available evidence
- Marcus's parallel to Werner (illegal status, German-category national) is rendered precisely and without self-pity; this is Marcus's register — he names it accurately and with some distance
- The lock on the desk drawer: a physical gesture; the thing locked away is not a secret he is hiding from investigators, but a knowledge he is managing; the distinction matters
- Do NOT have Marcus take any action based on this chapter's conclusions; the chapter is about the end of his capacity to act
