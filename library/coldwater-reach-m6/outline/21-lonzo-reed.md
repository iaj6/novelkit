# Chapter 21 — Lonzo Reed

**One-line summary**: Lonzo's exceptional chapter: a year of watching; the man he saw on the farm road the night of the storm; the decision he has not yet made.

**POV**: Lonzo Reed (exceptional chapter)  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Setting**: Pfeiffer farm — icehouse post, farm road  
**Time**: Late August / early September 1944  
**Target words**: 1,500–2,000 (short exceptional chapter)

**Chapter shape**: Interiority-heavy with movement  
**Ending mode**: Turn

---

## Structural justification
Lonzo's chapter is placed at the high point of the farm thread (after the haywork of ch. 20, before Karl's transfer in ch. 24) because it gives the reader the vantage Lonzo has built over a year of watching — and because Lonzo saw something on the night of October 11–12, 1943 that no primary POV can supply: he drove past a stranger on the farm road. This sighting, when the reader encounters it, completes the picture of Werner Grau's night in a way that corroborates Eira's dock observation from the other direction.

---

## Goal
Establish Lonzo as a full person; give him his piece of the mystery; and leave him at the edge of a decision — whether to tell anyone what he saw — which he has not made and may not make.

## Obstacle
Lonzo's obstacle is the one he has lived with for sixty-two years: knowing what he knows and deciding what to do with it, in a world that did not always ask his opinion. The particular obstacle here: he knows something about a dead man that nobody has thought to ask him about, and he is trying to decide whether that means he has an obligation.

---

## Opening texture
Routine-procedure: Lonzo at his post by the icehouse door. The late-summer afternoon. He has been here, at this post, for over a year. He knows how to sit with his attention engaged while appearing to do nothing.

---

## Action beats

1. **The post**: Lonzo in the chair by the icehouse door, the carbine across his knees. The late afternoon light. He has been doing this for more than a year, and the year has made him an expert in this particular piece of ground — the icehouse, the barn, the farmyard, the Pfeiffer family.

2. **What he has read**: He has read the four Germans correctly. Karl: the young one, not military in any meaningful sense; conscripted, trying to survive; the waxwing boy, as Lonzo thinks of him. The other three: older, harder, not dangerous but not relaxed either. He has managed them without incident by being exactly what he is — a sixty-two-year-old man with a carbine who is not going to do anything stupid and expects the same.

3. **The girl**: He has watched Hannah and Karl's exchange develop over the year with the attention of a man who has watched things develop his whole life and knows that watching is sometimes the whole of what's required. He is not alarmed; he is not approving; he is seeing it for what it is and making sure it stays in the range of what he can permit. What they have done — the words, the notebook, the bird names — he has decided is not his business to stop.

4. **His own war**: His nephew Desmond is in the Army, Pacific; Lonzo gets letters from Desmond's mother, not from Desmond. The letters are getting shorter. This fact is in Lonzo at all times; it is not the first thing he thinks about every minute, but it is never absent.

5. **October 1943, the night**: He has thought about this before. He was driving back from the post office in town — later than usual; a personal errand. The farm road. A man he didn't recognize on the road, about a quarter-mile from the farm's front gate — heavyset, not young, moving toward the farm. Lonzo slowed. The man had already turned off the road and into the field track that runs alongside the pasture. Lonzo did not stop. It was not his post; it was a public road; the man was not on the farm property. He drove on.

6. **The connection, now**: He made the connection months later, when the body story was still being talked about in town. A man came to the farm the night before the body. Did the man reach the farm? He doesn't know. Did he meet Albrecht? He doesn't know. Lonzo has enough to put in front of someone, but he is not sure who someone is or whether someone will do anything useful with it.

---

## Ending (turn)
Late afternoon, the shadows coming long across the farmyard. Karl comes out of the icehouse and sits on the step, as he does. He looks at the sky, which is clear, in the way he looks at the sky when he is naming birds he can see. Lonzo watches him. He thinks about the man on the farm road: heavyset, not young, moving toward the farm in the dark. He is deciding whether he will ever say this out loud. He has not decided.

---

## New question raised
Will Lonzo tell someone what he saw? He has the piece that Eira needs — the approach to the farm, the direction the man was coming from. But no primary POV character ever asks him directly, and he does not volunteer. The reader holds this piece; the principals do not.

---

## Continuity notes
- Lonzo's sighting: he saw Werner Grau on the farm road approximately 11:00–11:30 PM on October 11, 1943, heading toward the farm; this is consistent with Werner arriving at the Coldwater waterfront by dinghy (corroborating Soule's testimony from ch. 15) and then walking to the farm before Eira saw him on the dock around midnight (or he went to the dock first, then tried the farm)
- Ambiguity preserved: Lonzo doesn't know whether Werner reached the farm; he doesn't know what happened between Werner and Albrecht; the reader now knows Werner walked toward the farm that night but cannot confirm what happened next
- Lonzo does NOT report what he saw; the chapter ends in deliberation, not action; he is never directly asked by anyone; this piece stays with the reader, not with the investigators
- Lonzo's voice: unhurried, observational, dry without being performative; he has a Black Southerner's lifetime of calibrating what he says and to whom; this should be rendered through his attentiveness, not through dialect
- His nephew Desmond: mentioned once; does not need development; simply the war's presence in Lonzo's life
