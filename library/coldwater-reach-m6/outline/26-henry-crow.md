# Chapter 26 — Henry Crow

**One-line summary**: Henry's exceptional chapter: the boiler room, James's hard letter, what he sees in Marcus; and the pre-dawn figure he now connects to the breakwater.

**POV**: Henry Crow (exceptional chapter)  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Setting**: Coldwater Reach High School — boiler room; Henry and Lillian's house  
**Time**: November–December 1944  
**Target words**: 1,500–2,000 (short exceptional chapter)

**Chapter shape**: Single scene  
**Ending mode**: Turn

---

## Structural justification
Henry's chapter is placed here — between the proceeding's setup (ch. 25) and Marcus's commitment (ch. 27) — because it provides the reader with: (1) Henry's piece of the mystery (the pre-dawn figure he connects to Werner Grau), and (2) the view of Marcus and Lillian from the outside, which no primary POV can supply and which gives the reader full context for Marcus's commitment in ch. 27. Henry also knows about James, whose situation is worsening, and this is the chapter where the reader sees that.

---

## Goal
Establish Henry as a complete person — not Marcus's landlord or the school custodian, but a man with his own full account of the town; give him his piece of the mystery; show James's situation from Henry's angle; and show, through Henry's eyes, what Marcus has become in this household without Marcus knowing it.

## Obstacle
Henry's obstacle is the chronic obstacle of a man who has watched a place for twenty years and learned exactly how much of what he notices is useful to say. The decision about Werner Grau's connection to the pre-dawn figure is the same calculation he has made about many things: whether saying it changes anything, and for whom.

---

## Opening texture
Routine-procedure: Henry in the school boiler room, the November cold beginning to demand constant management. This is Henry's domain; he knows the boiler the way Eira knows the harbor.

---

## Action beats

1. **The boiler room**: Henry with the boiler — feeding it, checking the pressure gauges, cycling the radiators. This is precise, competent work; the school's warmth is entirely his doing from October through April. He has been doing this since 1928. He knows the building's heating rhythms better than its architecture.

2. **Seeing the town from here**: Henry's twenty-year view of Coldwater: who is useful and who isn't; where the weight rests; the small acts by which the town defines who belongs and who is counted. He is not bitter; he is accurate. He does not spend energy on what cannot be changed; he is precise about what can.

3. **What he sees in Marcus**: He has watched Marcus Klein for two and a half years from the particular vantage of the kitchen and the hallway and the early-morning house. What he sees: a man who is performing the temporary resident with such expertise that he has worn through the performance to the thing underneath, which is that he is simply here. Henry understood this before Marcus did. He has not said so.

4. **James's letter**: A letter from James arrived last week. Henry and Lillian have read it several times. The letter is short — shorter than James's letters used to be — and careful in its way of being careful. The Philippines campaign. James is well; the work is hard. But Henry has been reading James's letters for two years and he can read what "the work is hard" means. He does not say this to Lillian, who also knows. They do not discuss it at the table with Marcus present.

5. **The pre-dawn figure, October 1943**: Henry takes the early bus, always — he is at the school at 5:30 AM to start the boilers before anyone else arrives. On October 11, 1943, in the pre-dawn dark, he passed a man he didn't recognize walking toward the harbor. He took the bus; the man was on foot, going the other way. Heavyset, not young, a laborer's build. Henry noted him as unusual and thought nothing more of it.

6. **The connection**: When Marcus mentioned, once, in the kitchen — not pressing, just a question — whether Henry had ever heard of a man named Werner Grau, Henry said no. It was true; the name meant nothing to him. But now, over a year later, with the name in his memory and the body having been the talk of the town in October 1943, Henry has assembled it: the pre-dawn figure was walking toward the harbor. The body came in from the harbor. Werner Grau was a man Marcus was inquiring about quietly.

---

## Ending (turn)
Henry closes the boiler room. He will go home; Lillian will have supper. James's letter is in his coat pocket. Marcus will be at the table. Henry has assembled his piece of the account — the pre-dawn figure, the harbor, the name — and he has not yet decided what to do with it. He does not decide now. The turn is the assembly: he has it; he holds it. Another man who knows something he has not said.

---

## New question raised
Will Henry tell Marcus what he saw? The reader now has Lonzo's piece and Henry's piece: two sightings of Werner Grau on the night he died. Neither man tells the principals directly. The picture is assembled in the reader's mind, not in any character's.

---

## Continuity notes
- Henry's pre-dawn sighting: he saw Werner on the street walking toward the harbor, approximately 5:15–5:30 AM on October 12, 1943 — meaning Werner was still alive and on foot after the dock encounter Eira witnessed (approx. midnight) and the farm road encounter Lonzo witnessed (approx. 11 PM); the timeline is: Werner arrived at the waterfront by dinghy late evening; walked to the farm (Lonzo's sighting); returned or stayed in the harbor area through the night; was seen by Henry in the pre-dawn heading toward the harbor again; was found on the breakwater the following day after the storm peaked
- Henry does NOT tell Marcus in this chapter, or anywhere in the novel; this is a deliberate unresolved thread — one piece the reader has that no primary POV character assembles
- James Crow: the Philippines campaign (late 1944 — real historical context); his letters are getting shorter and more careful; this is the closest the novel comes to a combat casualty in the narrative; it is not resolved in the text (his survival or death remains unannounced in the novel's timeframe)
- Henry's view of Marcus: he has understood Marcus's situation with accuracy and warmth for two and a half years; his chapter makes this visible to the reader before Marcus's commitment (ch. 27)
