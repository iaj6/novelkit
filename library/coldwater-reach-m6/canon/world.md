# World

## Setting

**Coldwater Reach, Maine** — a fictional working port on the mid-coast, small enough that the harbormaster knows every hull that ties up, large enough to have a high school, a post office, a diner, a feed store, and a Catholic church that also serves the Swedish Lutherans when the weather closes the inland roads. Population: perhaps 800 in town, another 400 on outlying farms and woodlots. Not a tourist place. Not a retirement place. A place that has always been slightly behind on its repairs.

**Time span**: October 1943 to May 1945. The war's last eighteen months as experienced by people who are not fighting it.

## Geography and landscape

The harbor is tidal, with a thirty-foot range at neaps. The breakwater is granite rubble construction, thrown out in an L from the south headland; in storm it takes green water over the top. The working waterfront: a fish pier, a cold-storage house, a boatyard with a railway-haul-out, the harbormaster's shed at the pier head. The inner harbor holds lobster boats, draggers, and two sardine carriers that run to Eastport. The outer anchorage, which is exposed, is empty most winters.

Three miles inland the land flattens into drumlins and hay fields. The Pfeiffer farm sits at the end of a dirt road off Route 1, with a barn that pre-dates the Revolution, a renovated icehouse now serving as bunkhouse, and forty dairy cows. The pasture smells in August of warm manure and sweetgrass. In November it smells of frozen mud. The Germans work the farm under the rules of the Geneva Convention and the supervision of Lonzo Reed, a 62-year-old Coastal Patrol private who sits by the icehouse door with a carbine that may or may not be loaded.

The school where Marcus teaches is a three-story brick building on the main road, built in 1912, with a gymnasium that doubles as a polling place and a basement boiler room where Henry Crow is in charge of all heat from October through April.

## Technology and material culture

- **Radio**: war news comes over the radio. BC-1206 sets in households that can afford them. The diner has one on all day. Hannah's family does not listen to war news in German, and her father does not discuss this rule.
- **Mail**: V-Mail (photographed letter forms for overseas), ordinary letters for domestic, casualty telegrams delivered by the Western Union boy on a bicycle nobody has replaced. The telegram is the shape of dread.
- **Rationing**: OPA ration books govern gasoline, tires, sugar, coffee, meat, canned goods, and shoes. The harbormaster has a supplemental fuel allocation. Farm families barter informally. Marcus owns no car and walks everywhere; this was unremarkable in Vienna and is remarked upon in Coldwater.
- **The U-boat question**: in 1942, U-boats sank ships off the Maine coast. By 1943, the threat has receded — convoys, air patrols, the longer Atlantic battle going the other way. But the memory is present; fishermen remember the oil slicks and the life rings. The harbormaster's log still has 1942 entries with phrases like "unusual surface wake" and "detonation, bearing approx. SE."
- **POW labor**: governed by the 1929 Geneva Convention. German prisoners at satellite camps (under the umbrella of Camp Houlton) work civilian farms. They are paid in canteen credits, not cash. They wear POW-stenciled clothing. They may not fraternize with civilians — this rule is loosely kept where there is no officer to keep it.

## Mood and atmosphere

Autumn fog. The smell of salt and diesel and fish offal. The particular quality of light in coastal Maine in November: flat, gray, uncompromising. The war is in the kitchen through the radio, in the post office through the telegrams, in the feed store through the ration books and the missing young men's names on the window placard. It is not dramatic. It is long. It is the texture of ordinary life deformed by a very large event far away.

The town is mostly white, mostly Protestant or Catholic, economically precarious in ways the Depression made worse and the war has partially remedied (factory orders, Navy contracts). Henry and Lillian Crow are one of perhaps three Black households in town; they are not newcomers, but they are counted. Marcus Klein is the only European refugee, and before he arrived he was described in the town meeting minutes as "a Continental educator." Both are absorbed into town life with the particular pragmatism of small places that need useful people — but neither is invisible.

## What the war has taken

The harbormaster's assistant, drafted in January 1942. Three Coldwater boys dead as of October 1943 (two in the Pacific, one in North Africa). A fourth missing. The teacher Marcus replaced. The sardine carrier *Helen F.* lost to collision in a dark-running convoy. The easy certainty about what Germans are like.
