# Style

## POV and Tense

Third-person limited past tense throughout. The narrative binds tightly to whatever POV the chapter belongs to — no cross-chapter omniscience, no free-floating observations that require knowledge the POV character does not have. The three POVs are differentiated enough that the reader should be able to identify the POV within the first paragraph.

## Overall Register

Literary adult fiction, restrained register, period-exact texture. The closest comparables by feel: *The Remains of the Day* (Ishiguro) for the quality of repression and what is not said; *Gilead* (Robinson) for the moral weight that accumulates in quiet sentences; *The Hours* (Cunningham) for the braided three-POV structure and the way each strand is complete. Not any of these in style — but the emotional pitch and the trust placed in the reader are comparable.

Prose is action and observation. Interior monologue is sparse and earns its space. The story trusts the reader to infer affect from event and detail, not from labeled emotion. "She felt sad" is not a sentence this novel writes.

## Eira's Register

Spare and procedural. Shortest sentences of the three POVs. The working vocabulary of the waterfront: tide, draft, load, bearings, the names of vessels and weather patterns. Even her emotional life translates into this vocabulary before it surfaces as narration. She does not explain herself. Her chapters create their effect through accumulation of precise physical observation; the emotional content is the gap between what is observed and what is withheld. Dialogue in her chapters is clipped — short exchanges, no social lubricant.

Average sentence: 10–16 words. Most interior passages max out at three sentences before Eira's attention returns to the physical world.

## Hannah's Register

The most tonally flexible of the three. Associative, capable of metaphor, registers sensation with more directness. German phrases are embedded without translation — they are there when her father's voice is in her head, or when Karl Reinmar has said something she is still processing. These are set in italics and contextually comprehensible. Her chapters open up to more dialogue, more scene-building, more of the texture of the farm (smells, sounds, the weight of animals and equipment). She is also the most likely to observe something accurately and not yet know what she has observed.

Average sentence: 12–20 words, with wider variance. Her registers of sensation allow occasional longer lyric passages, but these should not tip into poeticizing — Hannah is smart and feeling, not precious.

## Marcus's Register

Precise, slightly formal, architectured. Marcus writes long subordinate clauses, the way a man writes who learned English on the page and learned it in the academic tradition. His diction is more Latinate than the other two. He qualifies his statements — he knows words can be evidence and he handles them accordingly. His chapters have a dry, careful irony that emerges from the precision, not from jokes. He does not perform suffering; he names things accurately and with some distance, and this restraint is itself a form of feeling.

Average sentence: 15–22 words. His chapters have more internal structural complexity (if/then thinking, historical analogy, measured qualification) but never tip into academic opacity.

## Dialogue

- **Eira**: Short, functional, declarative. She doesn't explain herself in dialogue any more than in narration.
- **Hannah**: More willing to ask questions, more willing to leave sentences unfinished. Her conversations with Karl Reinmar are conducted in a broken pidgin of German and English; her conversations with Marcus are the most complete.
- **Marcus**: Careful, occasionally formal, precise. His English is deliberate — he chooses the right word, not the idiomatic word. This is not parody; it is texture.
- **Albrecht Pfeiffer**: When he appears in dialogue (which is rare), his English is spare and slightly stilted — not phonetic stage-German, but the English of a man who has decided to stop speaking it easily.
- **German speakers in general**: No phonetic burlesquing. German phrases where appropriate; the reader can hear the texture without a performance of accent.

## Pacing

Chapters run 2,000–3,000 words each, with a target of about 2,600 for a 30-chapter manuscript of ~80,000 words. The pacing within chapters follows the POV character's attention: Eira's chapters are the most compressed (she doesn't dwell), Hannah's the most expansive in sensory register, Marcus's the most deliberate in structure. No chapter should feel like it is waiting for another chapter's event.

## Period Texture

History is not deployed as set decoration. Ration books and V-Mail and the BC-1206 radio appear because these are the objects through which these characters live — not because the author wants the reader to think "authentic." The same logic governs all period detail: if it is in the scene, it is in the scene because a person in 1943 would encounter it and it matters to what they are doing.

Anachronism in diction should be avoided. The vocabulary of mid-century New England maritime is available through period sources. No modern management-speak, no contemporary psychological vocabulary (characters do not "process" things or have "trauma responses" as labeled events).

## Failure modes

The AI-author patterns most tempting in this story's register. The drafter should actively avoid these; the compression pass should cut them.

- **Post-scene subtext naming** — after a charged scene between, say, Hannah and Karl, a paragraph naming what the scene was "really about" or what Hannah "now understood." The novel does not do this. It leaves the scene. The reader understands.
- **Thesis restatement** — an abstract sentence at the end of a chapter restating the POV character's arc or the novel's theme. "She was learning, perhaps, that authority meant this." Cut it.
- **Emotional structural analysis** — dramatizing a feeling, then explaining its architecture. Eira's grief is especially vulnerable to this. The architecture should be the image, not the explanation.
- **Redundant transition / re-summary** — opening a chapter by recapping what the previous chapter left off. Each chapter begins in medias res of the POV character's day. No "It had been three days since she found the body."
- **Dialogue glosses** — "as if to say," "which meant." The three POVs are already distinguished by how they hear silence. Do not tag dialogue with interpretation; let it sit.
- **Meta-commentary on cognition** — "She noticed," "He registered," "Marcus found himself thinking." If the prose is doing its job, the noticing is the sentence, not a label for it.

## Lean-into patterns

Patterns the drafter and editors should NOT treat as failures, because this register depends on them.

- **Delayed interiority** — Eira especially: the emotional meaning of a scene arrives a paragraph later, after she has returned her attention to the physical world. This gap is the technique, not a failure to show feeling.
- **The untranslated German phrase** — in Hannah's chapters. Not explained, not italicized for foreignness (though set in italics as convention). Contextually graspable. The reader who does not know German should still feel it.
- **Measured qualification in Marcus's narration** — his subordinate clauses and qualifications are voice, not verbosity. Do not compress them out.
- **Scene endings that go one beat past resolution** — the last sentence of a scene is often something physical, something observed, rather than a statement of where we stand. This is not evasion; it is the texture of the register.
