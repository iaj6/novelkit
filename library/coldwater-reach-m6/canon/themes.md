# Themes

## 1. What authority costs

Eira holds a post built for someone else's body. She holds it correctly — better, in some senses, than Owen held it — and this does not make it hers in the eyes of the men who tie up at her dock. The novel asks what it means to hold a position entirely on the merits while the legitimacy question goes on and on. The answer the novel offers is not triumphant: authority at this cost is a kind of solitude. By the end, Eira has done something that the post required and that no one asked her to do, and in doing it she has stopped waiting for the post to become hers and simply occupied it. The novel does not call this heroism. It calls it work.

## 2. Whether displacement ends

Marcus has been displaced for five years by October 1943, across four countries, through an internment he does not discuss. He has become expert at the stance of the temporary resident — at not putting down things that will have to be picked up again. The novel asks whether this expertise, once acquired, can be given up. The answer is not a revelation; it is a small, specific, irrevocable act that commits him to this place, these people, this address. Displacement ends, the novel suggests, not when the external situation resolves but when the person displaced stops holding in reserve the option of leaving — when he stops performing rootlessness and simply lives where he is.

## 3. The accumulation of small choices

Hannah's arc is structured around the premise that adolescence is a series of small decisions that look, in the moment, like they don't count, and which turn out in aggregate to be a person. What she chooses to tell and what she withholds, who she defends and who she lets the town think what it thinks about — these are not dramatic acts. They have the weight of ordinary life. By the close, she has made one or two choices that cannot be undone and that are, without her having named them so, the shape of her character. The novel does not celebrate these choices or condemn them; it observes them with the same directness it brings to everything.

## 4. The partial knowledge the living hold

The body on the breakwater is the structural manifestation of this theme: nobody has the whole picture. The reader assembles what happened across thirty chapters and three perspectives and still may not have all of it. But the theme extends beyond the mystery: Eira does not know the full truth of Henry Crow's experience of the town; Marcus does not know what Hannah understands about Karl Reinmar; Hannah does not know what her father knows about the dead man. The novel's moral texture comes from the recognition that partial knowledge is the condition, not the exception — that acting responsibly from inside it is what character means.
