# Pitch

Coldwater Reach, Maine, October 1943. A man's body washes in on the breakwater after a three-day storm — no papers, no name, nobody's business but the sea's. The Coast Guard takes the body and most of the questions with it. What it leaves behind is shared, in fragments and separately, by three people who will never all sit in the same room: Eira Bowman, the fifty-two-year-old harbormaster who knows what she saw on the dock the night before the body came in; Hannah Pfeiffer, seventeen, whose family's dairy farm now houses four German POWs under an aging Coastal Patrol private, one of whom is from a town near where her father was born; and Dr. Marcus Klein, forty-seven, the Viennese émigré teaching German at the high school, a man who spent a year in a Canadian internment camp and now boards with the only household in Coldwater that doesn't treat him as a specimen.

Across roughly eighteen months of the war's second half, the three of them carry the breakwater question — each from their own angle, none with the full picture. What the novel is actually about is not the body: it is what authority costs a woman who holds a post built for a different body; what it means to make a series of small choices that accumulate into a life, when you are seventeen; and whether displacement ever ends, and what the displaced person becomes when it stops.
