Eira did the breakwater first.

The storm had run three days; the blow peaked sometime around midnight on the eleventh, fifty-three miles per hour at the outer head, which was honest for October but not a record. Owen's worst had been sixty-one, the November of thirty-five. She was not thinking about this. The damage was what it was. She would know it when she walked it.

She came through the pier gate at first light with the clipboard and walked into a wind that had dropped to twelve, backing northwest. The harbor surface still carried a chop. Two of the boats at the inner float had banged hard enough to leave marks on the dock face; she wrote down the berth numbers and moved on. The breakwater ran two hundred yards from the pier head before it turned at the elbow, closing the harbor to the southeast. She walked it from the base.

Mooring rings at the fourth course: two bent, one sheared at the eye. Wrote it down. The granite face at the elbow had shifted — she could see it in the pointing, a crack now wide enough to hold a thumb. A matter for next spring, if the appropriation came through. Cable and kelp lay draped over the top course for the first twenty feet past the turn.

She stopped.

At the end of the kelp line, in the wrack the storm had piled in the angle of the elbow, a man was lying face-down.

She crossed to him. He was not moving. She put two fingers to the side of his throat and held them for a count. Nothing. The coat was dark wool, town-cut, not oilskin. The shoes were oxford-type, leather-soled. No gear, no line, nothing on the stonework around him to suggest a vessel or a bag. She turned out the inside coat pocket: empty. Right trouser pocket: empty. Left: a folded handkerchief, no initial.

She stood. The harbor was quiet. A dragger was making its way out the channel, running lights still burning, the man at the helm not looking toward the breakwater.

She wrote down the position — body, elbow of the L, wrack-line, face-down, male, middle-aged or older, no papers — and the time: 0627. Then she went up the pier to the shed and called Sprucehead.

The call took four minutes. The station would send someone within the hour. She hung up and stood at the window. The harbor log for 1935 was on the shelf above the radio, the spine gone gray. She looked out at the south dock.

She put on water for coffee and went back to the breakwater to wait.
