# Portland

She took the window seat. The bus left Coldwater Reach at six-fifteen in the morning — twice a week in the winter, fuel rationing, since October of the previous year. The seat beside her was empty to Rockland. The ledger was on her knee.

The coast road ran south through Northport and then Lincolnville. Inland side: flat fields, grey and bare with December. Harbor side: water showing through birch and spruce when the road bent near the shore. The tide was out somewhere around Rockland — dark mud flats, the channel running grey between them. She noted the swell direction from the southwest. Weather system had come through the Gulf clean, not tracking up the coast. The next few days would be clear for the fleet.

She had been over the figures twice before leaving the office. Correct both times. The basis was the October and November catch records. The fleet was running at roughly forty percent of pre-war capacity — itself already reduced from before the fuel rationing. The winter request was a twelve percent increase over the prior year's winter figure, supportable by December-through-March catch tonnage going back to 1939. Conservative. What the fleet needed to run winter schedules and maintain moorage. She had written the proposal in the standard format because the standard format was what the meeting used.

She was the only woman on the bus going to this meeting. She would be the only woman in the meeting room. She had been the only woman at each of the three previous quarterly coordination meetings — Bangor, Portland, Portland. At the first one, in Bangor in January of the previous year, the harbormasters from the other towns had looked at her twice when she came in and said nothing. By the third meeting the double look had largely stopped.

She had learned to arrive early. It gave her time to be visibly at work before the room filled, which handled the entry.

The bus stopped at Rockland. Two passengers off, four on. Then Camden — the Penobscot Bay opening wide and grey to the west, the islands dark shapes against the pale sky. Working water. She turned back to the ledger.

Mooring allocations: eight vessels laid up for the winter, four on reduced schedules, eleven running regular through March. The north slip at the commercial wharf occupied by the Navy maintenance vessel since September, which had tightened dock space. She had written to the District office in September and received a deferral in November. She noted the item in the margin. She would raise it if a suitable moment arose at the meeting, though she was not counting on one.

South of Camden the road bent close to shore for a long straight stretch, and through the window she could see the bay with white water on the outer ledges — four feet of swell from the southwest, maybe a little more. The last of the daylight was thinning in the west, the sky a flat, committed grey. It would be dark before they reached Portland. Four hours from Coldwater Reach; she had made this trip twice before and knew the way the ride settled into itself after Camden, the coast receding, the road running more directly south.

She turned back to the figures and reviewed them once more. They were still correct.

---

The Portland harbor coordination office was on the third floor of a brick building a block from the waterfront. Four long tables arranged in a square, a chalkboard at the far end. Someone had written the day's agenda in large block letters before the first harbormaster arrived. Coal smell from the office stove, the dry warmth of a room that had been heated since early morning.

She was the fourth person to arrive. She arranged her ledger and papers on the table before her and was engaged with them before the next man came in.

Twelve harbormasters from Maine coastal towns by nine o'clock. She knew some of them by sight. Varnum from Pemaquid — a large man going somewhat to heaviness, with the particular unhurried quality of someone who has worked a stretch of coast long enough that its recurring things have arranged themselves into a known sequence — had spoken with her in the spring about maintenance costs for the outer-coast granite breakwaters. She had found him useful and direct. The harbormaster from Thomaston sat back from the table and spoke too loud to compensate for the distance. The others she knew by sight and had not retained by name.

The Coast Guard liaison was not Parsons. This one was younger, clean-mannered, with the specific manner of someone who conducts meetings rather than investigates incidents. Hartley was the name on the folder before him. One of the two men from the fuel allocation office placed a briefcase on the table and did not open it for the first forty minutes.

---

Fuel allocation first. Fitch, the Portland allocation officer, ran through the regional figures — town by town, current allocation, prior year comparison, variance. When he reached Coldwater Reach she recognized her own numbers coming back through his reading of them. He moved on.

The Rockland allocation generated twenty minutes of discussion. She listened without contributing.

When Fitch asked for additional documentation and requests, she stood and went to the chalkboard. She set the ledger open on the chalk rail and went through the figures by month. October. November. The catch records and fuel consumption alongside. The basis for the twelve percent increase. She had gone through the sequence that morning in the harbormaster's office before the bus left — not from nerves, she was not nervous — but to confirm it would stay clean under questions. Her ledger, her handwriting, her organizing system, started in January of the year before. She knew every figure in it.

Fitch said: "The supporting documentation is available for review?"

"Yes," she said.

He said her request would be included in the regional report. She returned to her seat and opened the ledger to the mooring allocation page. The harbormaster from Thomaston said something to the man beside him at a volume she could not entirely make out. She did not look at them.

---

At ten-thirty they broke for fifteen minutes. She went to the window.

Portland's working harbor below — commercial slips, vessels moored in their winter configurations, fewer than the pre-war count. A tug working toward the fuel dock. The channel running grey between the wharves toward the outer harbor. Five times the capacity of Coldwater Reach, three times the commercial traffic, but the same grammar underneath. Rope and brine and diesel from the door when someone opened it downstairs.

She drank coffee from the urn and returned to her seat.

The second half of the morning: coastal communications network. BC-1206 relay stations, the winter maintenance schedule, three stations on the outer coast with intermittent signal logged in October. Hartley ran through the specifications efficiently. She noted the items relevant to Coldwater Reach and let the rest pass.

Then the quarterly incident summary.

Hartley read from his notes.

A vessel fire in Camden Harbor on November fourteenth. Stove malfunction, no casualties. The *Mary Agnes* in dry dock for forward section repair. A collision in the Sheepscot River on November twenty-seventh between a lobster smack and a coastal trader. Minor damage to both, no injuries, referred to District claims.

Then: "Unidentified remains, Coldwater Reach breakwater, late October. Classified accidental drowning, case closed."

He moved to the next item.

Varnum, from across the table, said: "Yours?"

"Yes," she said.

He set down his pen. He had a specific quality of stillness — the stillness of a man who has seen enough of a recurring thing that each instance settles into the larger category almost before he has finished looking at it.

"I had one like that in '42," he said. "Came in after the February blow. The way he was dressed, and that particular year, we all had submarines on our minds. Turned out to be a fisherman from New Brunswick. Missing three weeks, his family knew to come." He paused. "Your man was different. The clothing, they said."

"European cut," she said. "Not military."

Two other conversations were running at the far end of the table. Hartley was making a note. The room was not waiting on this.

Varnum nodded. It was the nod of someone who has already arrived at a place and is simply confirming the route. "Likely a passenger off something that didn't file a manifest," he said. "There's more of that along this coast than people think. The refugee crossings."

She said: "Yes."

The meeting continued.

---

At twelve-fifteen Fitch read the regional recommendation. Allocations as submitted, with modifications to the Rockland and Damariscotta requests pending additional documentation from the respective harbormasters. Coldwater Reach: approved as submitted.

She wrote the date in the ledger margin and the abbreviation for approved. She capped the pen.

They broke at one. She found a lunch counter two blocks from the building — fish chowder, good, the kind they made near the working docks with the right weight of cream. The woman behind the counter refilled her coffee without being asked. Two men at the far end were talking about the midcoast heating oil allocation and whether the extension would come through by January. She ate without contributing to it and was back at the table when the afternoon session began.

Winter security protocols. Blackout compliance schedule, civilian vessel restrictions after dark, Coastal Patrol coordination with the District office for February and March. She noted two items for Coldwater Reach. The north slip did not come up in a suitable moment. She wrote in the margin: *January — north slip letter, Fitch attention*.

The room thinned by four. She collected her papers, closed the ledger, and went downstairs.

---

The bus north left Portland at five. Dark already. The harbor lights shimmered in the water at the foot of the hill. She took the window seat.

Two follow-up items. The north slip letter, January, Fitch. The blackout protocol clarification for the mooring lights, which she could handle by telephone to Hartley. She noted both and closed the ledger.

The bus was cold at the back. The heating ran off the engine and reached the forward rows well enough; by the third row from the rear the air held its temperature. She kept her coat on. The seat was damp from the cold that had gotten in at Camden. She had noted this and settled in.

The bus moved through the south end of Portland and turned onto the coast road heading north. Headlights threw the road ahead in a moving oval, the spruce close on both sides, the road dark beyond the beam. Occasional openings where the shore showed as a dark mass. The swells broke white at the rocks where the road bent near the water.

She thought about what Varnum had said.

*The refugee crossings.* He had said it the way you name a known feature of the coast — the way you name any recurring thing that the work has made familiar over enough seasons. *There's more of that than people think.* Consistent in the specific case with what Parsons's file did not contain. No papers, no identification, European civilian clothes. A man traveling by means that did not generate documentation.

She had been holding the man on the breakwater as an isolated fact. Inexplicable within the available explanations and therefore stored without a heading, in the place where an explanation would go once one arrived. The official ruling — accidental drowning, unidentified — was accurate within its scope and she had not disputed it. The town's submarine theory had dissolved by November without requiring anything from her. Her own version she had held privately and had not developed further than it had developed on its own.

Varnum's word was *crossings*. Not crossing.

She did not know whether this was true of the specific man on the specific breakwater. The man still had no name. The official record would not change.

But she had not had a word for it before. She filed it alongside what she already held and did not reach further than that.

The bus stopped at Rockland. Three passengers off, one on. She looked at the dock lights through the glass. The hulls were riding right against their moorings — tide well in, another hour to the top. She checked this without particularly deciding to.

The haddock catch from the second half of November had been strong. Hallett's boat especially — good numbers off the midbank, the best week of the month. The fuel approval would carry the fleet through February on reasonable discipline. She would post the winter allocation schedule Monday morning, before the hard cold came down.

Rockland to Camden. Camden to Northport. She put the ledger in her bag when the night figures were settled and watched the dark outside the window — the spruce, the occasional cleared field pale with frost, the water showing once at the coast for a long straight stretch before the road turned inland again.

Coldwater Reach at nine-ten.

She was the only one getting off. The street was empty when she came down the steps. She could hear the harbor before she could see it — the creak of mooring lines, the slap of chop against the pilings, the sound of the tide at the top of the flood and beginning to turn. Temperature had dropped since morning. Good. The front had come through clean.

She walked to the office. Alderman had locked up after the evening check. She unlocked the door, lifted the handle as she turned — the stiff latch, unchanged — and went in.

The night log was on the chart table where it should be. Alderman's hand in the eight-thirty space: *fleet secure — nothing to report.* She initialed the bottom of the page and wrote the time of her return.

She locked the office behind her and walked home.
