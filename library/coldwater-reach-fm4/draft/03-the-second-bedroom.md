# The Second Bedroom

Clara Walsh had been looking at the board long enough that Marcus had started to wonder whether she was still working on the grammar or had already made it through to something on the other side of it.

He had explained the ablative absolute twice — once in its architecture and once in what the architecture was meant to convey. The grammatical explanation had been correct, and the class had received it in the way that students received correct explanations of things they could not yet feel: as information to be held until the feeling arrived, if it arrived. The second explanation had been an attempt at the feeling itself: *his rebus gestis*, he had written on the board, translated it, then tried to show what it was doing — how it took an action, its agent and its verb, and pushed the whole of it into the background, completed, settled into the past before the main clause began. Caesar built his bridge, and the grammar had already performed the accomplishment, had arranged the earlier action as the prior condition of everything that followed.

Clara Walsh looked up from the board.

"So it is like circumstances," she said.

"Yes," Marcus said. "Exactly like circumstances." He waited a moment. "The subordinate action is finished before the main clause begins. It becomes the background against which the main action is possible at all."

She wrote something in the margin of her notebook with the focused economy of a student who had understood and was now recording it for herself, not for Marcus. The class around her was working through it more slowly, which was what classes did with new constructions, and Marcus had the patience for it: he had explained the ablative absolute for the first time approximately twenty-five times and had watched students arrive at the same understanding by different routes and at different intervals. The patience was not indifference. It was its own form of attention.

He moved them through the Livy passage he had assigned for the following week — the Macedonian campaigns, which he had chosen in part because the ablative absolutes came at close intervals and the students would encounter the construction in context before they were asked to produce it themselves. Then the declension review.

Raymond Foss, in the back left seat, committed the same error in the third declension dative plural that he had been committing since the end of September: put the accusative ending where the dative belonged. Marcus corrected him. Raymond Foss corrected himself and committed it again. Marcus corrected him again and had him read the ending aloud before writing it, the body's memory being on occasion more reliable than the pencil's. Raymond Foss read it aloud and wrote it down and had it wrong a third time.

Marcus corrected it a third time, with the patience that came from knowing the ending would one day feel inevitable — that a point existed in Raymond Foss's future at which this confusion would be as incomprehensible to him as the grammar was natural now, and that reaching that point required only the correction given and given and given again until it accumulated into knowledge. He believed this about grammar because the evidence consistently supported it.

He assigned the Livy passage and dismissed the class.

---

The teachers' room had a coffee tin and a teapot on the radiator shelf, a table with two chairs against the window, and a view of the north side of the building where the maples had finished dropping their leaves the previous week. He made his tea — he brought his own, in a paper bag in his coat pocket, the situation at Beal's having been irregular since early 1942 — and sat at the end of the table.

Edna Price and Ralph Garland were at the other end. Marcus had been there perhaps two minutes when he understood that they were talking about the body on the breakwater, which had been the town's conversation for the better part of a week and had arrived at this table in what he recognized as its hardened form: the original cluster of possibilities had collapsed into a single confident explanation.

"A U-boat agent," Garland said. "It is the only explanation that makes any sense. A man washes up here with no papers, in foreign civilian clothing — of course he came off a submarine. Of course."

"Mildred Foster's husband says the Coast Guard is calling it a regular drowning," Edna Price said.

"Well. They would say that."

Marcus drank his tea. He looked at the window. The maples were bare, their branches precise against the gray sky.

He was aware of several explanations that were not the U-boat theory. He was aware of them because he had lived through the period of European history that produced them. A man in civilian clothes, no papers, washed up on a foreign coast: the correct first hypothesis was displacement, not espionage. A man moved through the unofficial channels that wartime Europe had developed for people who could not move through official ones — through the arrangements of borrowed boats and borrowed names and borrowed trust that he knew, from his own passage through something like them, to be more extensive and less organized than the people outside them supposed. Such a man could arrive on a coastline and not survive the arriving. This required no submarine. It required only the war, and a coast, and bad luck.

He also knew that this was not an explanation he could introduce into a conversation in the teachers' room at Coldwater High School.

He drank his tea.

He was thinking about the calluses he had heard about — not at the base of the fingers, not the callus pattern of a fishing hand or a farm hand or a cannery hand, but in the centers of the palms. He had heard this detail the way that details from a death moved through a small town: passed between people, softened by transit, arriving with less precision than it had started with. He filed it. The calluses were specific. The specificity was useful.

Garland was describing the U-boat in some detail, with a confidence that bore no evident relation to any knowledge of submarines. Marcus thought of the third time he had corrected Raymond Foss's declension — the patience that had been required, the giving of the right ending until it accumulated — and then he thought that there was a version of patience like that, in situations like this one, that was not patience at all. It was only silence, because the correction would cost more than the correction would ever be worth.

He rinsed his cup and went to the next class.

---

He left the building at half past three. The cold had arrived in its late-October depth in the past week — not the fresh cold of early October but the cold that had weight and intention to it, that was beginning to mean November. He turned up his collar and walked.

Three blocks to Elm Street. He had walked this route every school day since the middle of March, 1942, and knew it now with the thorough knowledge of daily repetition: which boards in the Maple Street sidewalk were soft, that the elm at the corner of Birch had a root that had forced a ridge in the pavement, that the Garson house had a broken gutter that dripped onto the path in a particular way. He passed the harbor road without turning onto it, as he did every afternoon. The smell of brine and diesel reached him briefly from the south and then was gone.

He was thinking about the Livy passage. He had been thinking about it since the class — this was what happened sometimes when a grammatical construction moved into the foreground of his attention, it stayed there for a day or two, reorganizing the way he saw things, until it receded. *Dum haec geruntur* — while these things were being done. Not technically the ablative absolute, but in the same territory: the action happening in the background while the main clause went forward in the foreground. One event held in suspension while another was accomplished. The thing that was also happening.

He turned onto Elm Street.

---

The smell reached him when he was still on the front step: fish chowder, which meant Lillian had found what she had been looking for all week. He hung his coat and hat on the peg inside the door and went to the kitchen.

She was at the stove, her back to him. The pot was on the back burner, the lid trembling slightly with the heat.

"Fish chowder," she said, without turning. "Real milk. Not the powder."

He sat at the kitchen table. "Where did you find it?"

She set the wooden spoon on its rest and turned around. "Pfeiffer farm sends milk on Tuesdays. Twice a week if the week is good." She said it with the satisfaction of a practical problem solved — the quieter kind, belonging to a person who had paid attention and been proved right. "It was a good week."

He knew the Pfeiffer farm because he knew the Pfeiffer daughter, who was in his Latin class and worked harder than the work required of her. He did not say this. He said: "It smells very good."

"It is very good," she said, and turned back to the pot.

She asked about Vienna while she worked — she asked about Vienna most evenings, at intervals close enough that he had come to understand it as genuine curiosity rather than courtesy, which were different kinds of asking. He had learned to answer a piece of it each time, which was the only honest way to handle something that large: a piece that could be given without requiring everything that came after it.

Tonight he told her about the Prater in October. The park, the chestnut trees along the main avenue, which had shed their nuts in September and dropped their leaves by mid-October, leaving the avenue yellow-brown and the ground beneath it scattered with split casings. The smell of roasted chestnuts from the cart near the eastern entrance. The Sunday-morning habit he had maintained for several years — the walk before noon, the light in the leaves at this time of year. He described it. He gave her the years before the years he did not give her.

He did not get to 1938. He had learned at this table that stopping before 1938 was the right stopping place — not because of what came after so much as because the before was what answered what she was actually asking, which was: what was the place, before everything that happened to the place. He could answer that honestly. He had found it was better to have something he could answer honestly than to preface every answer with where it could not go.

Henry came in at ten past six.

He brought the smell of coal on his jacket — not the faint background smell Marcus had associated with him for a year but the immediate working version. He pulled the jacket off and hung it on the peg by the kitchen door.

"East furnace flue needed clearing," he said. He sat down. He looked at the bowl Lillian had set at his place and then at his wife. "Real milk," he said.

"Pfeiffer farm," she said.

He picked up his spoon.

They ate. The kitchen was warm in the way the kitchen was always warm in the evenings, which was a warmth Marcus had come to understand, over a year, as belonging specifically to this room and these people. Outside, the dark had settled fully, the October dark arriving earlier each week on its way to becoming the winter dark. He ate his chowder. He tasted the milk in it, which Lillian had been right about. He drank his tea.

Henry had brought home the copy from the teachers' room. He set it on the table and folded it in half and read the front page. Then he said, without looking up: "Someone in town was asking about you. Earlier in the week."

Marcus looked at him.

"The German teacher," Henry said.

A moment. Marcus thought of Ralph Garland and the U-boat and the particular logic by which a week of speculation hardened into a theory that required a local figure to be suspicious of.

"What did you tell them?" Marcus said.

Henry turned the page. "That you teach Latin too. Figured that was the useful part."

Marcus said: "That is accurate."

Lillian came to the table with the bread she had been keeping at the oven's edge and they ate, and Henry read the rest of the paper, and Lillian did the dishes. Marcus thanked her for the chowder. She said it was nothing. He had known for a year at least that it was not.

---

He went upstairs at half past eight.

The second bedroom was at the end of the hall, window facing the street. The room was tidy with the tidiness he had maintained long enough that it required no maintenance — the desk clear, the books on the shelf ordered by subject and then by size, the narrow bed made tightly in the European habit of order as a form of dignity. He took his coat off and hung it. He stood a moment in the center of the room. The kitchen sounds came up through the floor: water in the pipes, Lillian's voice briefly, Henry's reply.

He went to the shelf and took down a book.

He had had it since Vienna: *Einführung in die deutsche Grammatik*, published in Leipzig in 1929, which he had carried through several countries and arrived with in Maine with a water-damaged cover and a spine repaired with tape that had since yellowed and dried. He did not use it for teaching; he had better materials for teaching. He used it sometimes at the end of days for reasons he had not felt the need to articulate.

He sat at the desk and opened the book to the middle, where it fell open naturally from having been opened there enough times. The pages in front of him carried his own marginal notes in pencil — a paragraph on the genitive of possession, several observations on adjectival constructions, some marginal dialogue with the text. His handwriting from 1937, which he could identify by the particular formation of the *d*, the angle of the lettering, the specific way the lowercase *g* closed: the handwriting of a man in Paris, in a room on the Rue des Ecoles, writing notes in a book he had brought from Vienna in preparation for a teaching life that had not yet arrived, in a year that had not yet become what followed it.

He read one paragraph. The observation his 1937 self had set down was still accurate.

He closed the book.

He sat at the desk for a while without moving. The window faced Elm Street and through it he could hear the elm moving in the wind, its bare branches audible now that the leaves were gone. A car passed on the main road a block over. The house made its sounds around him — the furnace, the pipes, the small adjustments of a wooden building against the cold.

The room was cold. He was not, not entirely, not yet: the kitchen's warmth was still in him in the way that warmth stayed in the chest for a while after a person left a warm room, diminishing slowly.

He put the book back on the shelf.
