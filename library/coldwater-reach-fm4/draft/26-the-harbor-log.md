# The Harbor Log

The last of the seasonal boats were gone. The Nancy Marie had cleared for Spruce Head the final week of September; the Ida May had followed on the third of October, Kell running her out on the morning tide in a light northwest wind. The harbor was back to its winter numbers — twelve working moorings, the lobster fleet centered again, the summer berths empty and their cleats washed clean by the equinox blow. The sardine shed had closed its processing run the week prior. This was what October looked like, and it had looked this way for nine years under her management and for a good many years before that.

She was at the chart table by seven. Tide log open, the morning reading copied in at 0715. She set it aside and pulled the annual binder from the lower cabinet.

Log review. Every first full working week of October: twelve months of entries confirmed and closed, mooring registrations checked against the current register, incident notations followed to their official resolution, fuel allocation log balanced against the Portland figures. The accountancy of a year's harbor work — not audit, exactly, more the verification that the record said what had actually happened, entry by entry, so that when the district office asked she could answer for any month. She had done it every October for nine years. The previous harbormaster had done it before her, and she had learned the practice from his version of it and kept the structure without revision.

This year's review covered October 1943 through the present.

She had known, since January, what month it would eventually reach.

She opened to January and started.

---

January: equipment inventory, logged on the third. Chain counts, grapple hooks, the spare bollard hardware ordered in November and received on the 29th. Maintenance checks done. No incidents. She had done the January counts the same morning she found Owen's 1934 ledger in the back of the lower cabinet while tallying the chain. The old ledger was not part of the annual review; she had set it aside. She had come back to it later in the morning. The January log entry was complete.

February: a Coast Guard district letter on the ninth, standard quarterly operations acknowledgment, no action required. Nothing further on the October matter. She had not expected anything. Routine harbor operations. No incidents.

March: the spring opening. The Bethia on the fourth, Henry Cates bringing her down from the Camden haul-out on the morning tide. Mooring assigned to dock seven, as it had been for as long as Eira had been keeping this log and for some years before. Fleet registrations coming in. She checked each against the register. All current.

April: the main registration push. Six boats in, three renewals, two new mooring assignments from boats relocating out of Jonesport. She had processed the paperwork in a single afternoon at the chart table. The Portland fuel allocation confirmed on the 18th. All matched.

May: maintenance week. A corroded ring bolt on dock four's approach fitting, which she had replaced on the 14th with Declan Walsh's help. Materials and hours logged. No incidents. She checked the entry against the maintenance log. It matched.

June sixth: the D-Day notation. She had logged the radio call at 0302 — *Allied landings, Normandy coast; per radio, WLBZ 0300. Harbor routine* — and below it, same date, the dock-three cleat replacement she had done that morning in the window between the call and the fleet departure at 0530. She had also noted Eisenhower's address on WLBZ at 1820 — not harbor business, but she had put it in as context, as she had been in the habit of noting major radio events when the harbor was immediately involved. Fleet out on schedule. No incidents.

She held the June entry for a moment.

Then she turned the page.

July: the Nancy Marie arrived on the twelfth, temporary berth at dock eight, mooring fee paid in advance. The Ida May arrived the nineteenth at dock nine, Kell's double-ended hull needing the longer slip. Both registrations current. Sardine processing shed at capacity through July; harbor boat count and fuel draw elevated accordingly. She confirmed the allocation against the Portland records. They matched.

August: fuel margin held at three hundred gallons above forecast, confirmed on the 15th when the Portland figures came in. Ruth Fuller's dragger registered at dock six with Ruth as sole operator — she had updated the entry in July, Ruth having been running the boat herself on fuel-allocation days since the spring. Three mooring adjustments in August for the high-summer sandbar drift on the north channel approach. All corrected and logged.

September: no incidents. The sardine shed winding toward close. The Nancy Marie's departure logged September 29th, cleared for Spruce Head as anticipated. The Ida May logged October 3rd, Kell out on the morning tide.

She confirmed the departure entries against the register. They matched.

October 1944: current entries. Two and a half weeks of routine operations, tide readings, mooring notations, the weekly fuel draws. An approach-marker adjustment at dock seven on the 9th when Henry Cates came in on the strong northwest chop with the Bethia riding heavy. She had moved the marker eighteen inches north and logged it. The entry was current.

She confirmed the present entries against the register.

Everything matched.

She closed the front section of the binder.

She turned to October 1943.

---

The entries were present.

October 11th, 1943: barometric drop, storm advisory from the Coast Guard district office. Fleet pulled back from the outer moorings per standard protocol. She had confirmed the outer mooring lines herself on the afternoon of the 12th, walking the full dock in the blow. Entry complete.

October 14th, 1943: body found on the seaward face of the breakwater by Hector Brann, crew of the Alicia B., at approximately 0700. She had logged the notification at 0750, the Coast Guard arrival at 0820, the removal of the body from harbor jurisdiction at 0930. Unknown male individual, civilian appearance, no papers found on the person. Entry complete.

The entries following through the month: Lt. Parsons's visit on the 17th, her formal statement, the official ruling received in November. Accidental drowning, unknown individual, case closed to harbor records. Notation dated and signed. Entry complete.

She turned back one day.

October 13th, 1943.

Her entry for the thirteenth: *Mooring check completed, commercial dock, 2300. Fleet secure. No incidents. Tide reading 11.2 ft.*

She read it twice.

The entry was accurate in what it contained. The mooring check had been completed. The fleet had been secure. The tide had read 11.2 feet at 2300 hours. Nothing on the dock that night had required notification to the Coast Guard, or action of any official kind she had been empowered to take.

She had not entered what she had seen at the far bollard.

---

Owen's J.T. entry: January 8th, 1934, with the later addition below the original notation in his handwriting, the slope different from the dated entries above it. *This not entered at time of incident; entering here as complete account.* Then the account. His initials at the bottom.

He had been in Portland when the incident with J.T. happened. His deputy had logged the surface facts. Owen had read the entry when he returned and had made it complete.

She had been thinking about this since January. He had deferred completeness and returned to it; there was a word for what he had done. She had written the tide reading and the mooring check and the words *no incidents* and turned off the lamp and gone home and done it again the next morning and the morning after that, for twelve months.

He had come back to it.

She set her pencil down on the chart table.

---

At 2300 hours on October 13th, 1943, she had completed the mooring check and was walking back along the commercial dock toward the office. The storm had been clearing for the previous two hours, the harbor settling, the boats riding more easily at their lines as the blow moved northeast. She had made the outward pass down the full length of the commercial dock checking each bollard and cleat, and on the return she had seen the man at the far end.

Civilian coat, not oilskins or harbor work wear. Standing at the far bollard with his weight shifted forward, not at his sides in the way of someone working a line. The posture of a person waiting at a specific location rather than passing through. She had not recognized him. Not a registered vessel operator, not a face from the fleet or the dock work or anywhere she could place him.

He had been facing the water when she went past. He had not turned.

She had caught the second figure at the edge of the dock light — a shape in motion, already turning away, already in the first steps of departure. She had heard something: two or three words, spoken low, an exchange between the two of them. Not English. A sound she could not place at the time, consonants she had no frame for. The second figure walked inland before she reached the far bollard end. An ordinary pace, not running.

She had stood at the bollard end for a moment, long enough to see that the first man was still there, that his coat was wet at the shoulders and that he had not turned toward her. She had looked at him for the length of three or four seconds.

She had not approached. She had not called out. She had turned and gone back to the office and logged what the log said now: mooring check complete, fleet secure, no incidents, tide 11.2 feet.

When she turned off the lamp she had told herself there was nothing to enter: a man on a public dock after hours, a second person who had already departed, a few words in a language she did not recognize. No law broken that fell within harbor jurisdiction.

She had known, as she locked the office door behind her, that the log was not complete.

---

She knew things now that she had not known that October night.

Jan Václav Horak: Czech national, displaced person, last known address Halifax, Nova Scotia, 1942. Thirty-eight years old or thereabouts. No papers. The Coast Guard district office had sent her the name in a form letter five months after they closed the case, in April 1944. She had read it twice, noted it in her own log margin, and filed the letter.

She knew the language. In the spring she had gone to Marcus Klein — she had brought him what she could reconstruct of the sounds, the consonants she had been carrying for seven months — and he had given her the answer with the careful precision he gave to answers: Czech. Not German. Czech.

A Czech displaced person, last known Halifax, moving south along the coast through the channels that a man without papers moved through in 1943. Making for Coldwater Reach, for whatever he had been making for here. The second figure at the dock had walked inland. She had not identified that person. She had assembled what she could, from what she had seen and what Marcus Klein had been willing to tell her, and it stopped there.

None of this fell within harbor jurisdiction. None of it changed the official ruling.

What it had changed, slowly, across twelve months: the weight of the blank in the October 13th entry. She had known the blank was different from an ordinary omission since the morning Hector Brann found the body. She had carried that knowledge through the official inquiry, through the case closing, through the winter, through reading Owen's J.T. entry in January, through the spring and summer and this October. She had watched the blank and she had known what it was.

She picked up the pen.

---

She wrote below the original entry, in the space remaining on the dated page. The late notation format was the same regardless of what it corrected; she had used it twice before in nine years, both times for routine administrative matters. She wrote steadily, in the same hand she used for all harbor log entries:

*Late entry, dated October 1944: 2300 hours, 13 October 1943: completed mooring check, commercial dock. One unidentified individual present at the far bollard, civilian appearance, not a known registered vessel operator. Observed in the company of a second party who departed walking inland before I could make identification. No approach made. Not entered at original time. Entering now in accordance with complete record.*

Below that:

*— E. Bowman, Harbormaster, October 1944.*

She set the pen down.

---

She read it.

The entry said what had happened. An unidentified individual at the far bollard. A second party, direction inland, departed before identification. No approach made. Not entered at original time.

It did not say that she had known the next morning, looking at the body in the gray light on the breakwater face, that the man at the bollard and the body were the same. It did not say that she had spent twelve months aware that the Coast Guard's official record rested on the incomplete version. The entry said what she had seen, at the distance and in the light she had seen it, on the night of October 13th, 1943.

The entry did not name Horak. She had not known the man's name on October 13th, 1943. The entry was the night's evidence, and the night's evidence was an unidentified individual. She had not identified him then and could not identify him in this entry now.

The second party was also unidentified. She had not been able to identify them that night and had not been able to identify them since.

She read it a second time.

The record was complete.

---

She closed the binder. The stove was ticking on its steady burn, the morning cold fully pushed back from the desk. Outside the office window the harbor was at its October work: the lobster smacks at their moorings, the northwest chop making the small easy motion the boats were rigged for at this time of year, the channel mouth open and grey under the overcast. The Bethia in dock seven. Ruth Fuller's dragger in dock six, tied up for the morning. The two empty berths where the Nancy Marie and the Ida May had been.

She sat at the chart table for a few minutes with her hands flat on the closed binder.

The entry she had written was in a log that the Coast Guard district office read only in the event of an inquiry. There would be no inquiry.

What the entry changed was not any of this. What it changed was the record. By her hand, in her handwriting, in the log she had kept for nine years. This log, not Owen's — hers. The record now said what had been on the dock on October 13th, 1943. An unidentified individual and a second party and no approach made and not entered at the time.

Whatever she had assembled or suspected or held without saying in the months between then and now — that was not the log's business. The log's business was what she had seen.

---

She opened the current log.

She wrote the noon tide reading. Eleven feet, four inches. Clear. Wind north-northwest, seven to ten. Fleet at moorings.

She closed the log.

She took her coat from the hook by the door and went down the dock to walk the breakwater.

The October tides exposed the lower granite courses on the seaward face; she checked them as she went, working from the shore end out. The seam between the first and second course on the north face had been taking some water since spring; she crouched and ran a hand along the joint. Dry today. The iron pin on the third section was holding, which she had been watching since August. She went all the way out to the end, which was the habit, and stood at the seaward edge looking back at the harbor.

The office at the foot of the wharf. The cedar shingles. The fleet at the inside moorings. The empty summer berths. The harbor at its winter numbers, which were its honest numbers, what it was when the seasonal work cleared off and left the actual work.

She stood there for a moment. Not long.

Then she turned and walked back. There were mooring receipts to sort. There was the fuel report from the Portland office she had not yet opened, which would need to be answered by Thursday. The review was done.

She went inside and latched the door.
