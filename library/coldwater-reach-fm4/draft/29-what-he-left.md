# What He Left

The mud came up through March the way it always did — not melting exactly but surrendering, the frozen ground releasing one layer at a time, first along the south wall of the barn where the sun hit it earliest, then the path between the house and the milk house, then by the second week the full yard. The Holsteins came in from the morning pasture with it halfway up their legs. Hannah scraped the stalls twice a day now and still the mud came back. It was the season's particular tax.

Her father told her at supper. In German; it was still German at the supper table.

"Lonzo came by this afternoon," he said. He set his bread down and looked at his hands for a moment. "The men are being sent back to the camp. Thursday."

"All four of them?"

"All four." He picked up his bread again. "From Houlton they will go back eventually. To Germany, when the arrangements are made. It takes time, with the war not finished."

She ate the rest of her soup. She heard something in how he said *all four* — a flatness that was not nothing. But she did not ask about it.

After supper she went out to do the late check — water lines, the heat lamp over the new calf, a walk through the stalls with the lantern. The icehouse window was lit at the far edge of the yard. She stopped in the barn doorway and stood looking at it the way she had stood looking at it all winter, from November to now — watching the low amber square of it, the shape of Karl reading at his small table by lamplight.

Four days.

She went inside.

---

She spent those four days in the rhythm of the farm. The 5 a.m. milking, school, the afternoon chores, the 6 p.m. milking, the late check. The prisoners were on their winter schedule: Brunner and Krebs on the wood lot, Fischer running repairs on the east fence, Karl working the strip below the lower field where the dead growth from winter had come down. She could see them from the kitchen window in the mornings and from the barn doorway in the evenings. She kept her distance the way she had been keeping it since October, the choice made once and then remade every day, which was how she knew it was a choice.

She told herself she would not go to the south fence. On the second day she didn't. On the third day she went out with the post bar and the tamping rod, because the south fence needed work — the same corner post on the south section that had heaved in November of 1943 had shifted again over the winter, and she had been putting off the repair since February when the ground was still frozen. The frost heave was real. She had not invented it.

She carried the tools around the barn and set her footing in the mud.

---

He was working the strip between the fence and the lower wood lot, clearing the winter deadfall. When she first came around the corner of the barn, he had his back to her.

She put the bar in and worked the post. Drive, pull, reset — the mud closing back in around the bar each time she pulled out, the cold still in the ground below the first thawed layer. She was thinking about the heel of the post and whether she needed gravel beneath it when she heard him stop.

She looked up. He was at the fence on his side, his hands in his coat pockets, looking at her.

She straightened.

She said: "Guten Morgen."

He said: "Good morning."

She put the bar down.

She had not been this close to him since October. He looked the same and did not look the same — the sun-chapped skin still there, the gray-blue eyes, but something in his bearing that was different. The first year's watchfulness was gone; so was the second year's settled tiredness. He looked like a man who had been somewhere long enough to know it and was now preparing to leave.

She reached into her coat pocket and took out the carved bird. Three inches of birch, the wings folded against the body, the head angled slightly down. The corrected ridge on the breast where the blade had slipped. She had been carrying it for more than a year.

She held it out over the fence toward him.

"You should take this back," she said. In English, because that was what felt right.

He looked at the bird. He looked at her hand. He said: "No."

Just that word.

She kept her hand out a moment longer, then let it drop. She did not put the bird away.

"Will you have somewhere to go," she said. "When you get back. After the war."

He thought about this. She could see him working through it — not making a show of it but actually moving through it, word to word, the way he had all last summer at the fence.

"Back is not a place," he said. He placed each word carefully. "Not anymore."

The fence between them. Three feet of air, the top wire, the mud on both sides the same mud. Behind him the lower wood lot, spruce going dark where the sun had not yet reached. Behind her the barn.

"What will you do," she said.

"I don't know." He shifted his weight slightly. "Start." A pause. "The same thing as everyone."

She looked at the bird in her hand. She turned it once. The grain of the birch, the corrected ridge on the breast, the tail cut to a clean notch.

She had been carrying it for more than a year. In October when staying at a distance was its own kind of heaviness. In December when she stood in the barn doorway watching his lamp. This morning when she loaded the tools and came around the corner.

"I don't know what to do with this," she said.

"Keep it."

She put the bird back in her coat pocket.

Then she became aware of her father at the barn door. He had the pitchfork at his side — he had come out of something in the middle of it, or he had taken up the pitchfork to have something to hold. He was not coming toward them. He was watching. He had warned her to be careful, not stop; he had made his judgment about what was happening at this fence, and the judgment was held in whatever place he kept such things, and he was watching the end of it now without a word.

She had been at the fence perhaps five minutes.

"Good luck," she said.

Karl nodded once. He put his hands back in his coat pockets and turned back to his work.

She picked up the post bar and the tamping rod and walked back across the yard. Her father said nothing when she passed him at the barn door. She went inside and finished what needed to be done.

---

Thursday came in with rain, the fine kind that was mostly mist.

She woke at five and did the morning milking and came back to the house for breakfast. Her father was at the table with his coffee made. Magda was at the stove. Nobody said anything about the truck.

It came at eight. She was in the kitchen and she did not go out.

She watched from the window over the sink.

The four of them came out of the icehouse in order: Brunner first, then Krebs, then Fischer. Karl last. Each carried what he had. Brunner had a bag over his shoulder; the others had bundles or coat pockets that were full. Karl's hands were empty.

The Coastal Patrol truck had a canvas back. The driver stayed in the cab. Lonzo was at the tailgate with the Springfield over his shoulder.

Brunner climbed in first, then Krebs, then Fischer. Lonzo said something to the driver. Karl stood in the mud of the yard for a moment.

He did not look at the house.

He looked at the barn — the long, even look of a man registering what he is leaving. Then he put one foot on the tailgate and climbed up.

Lonzo folded his carrying sling around the Springfield and put it behind the seat in the truck cab. Seventeen months on this farm with that rifle, and now he was putting it away. He turned to where Albrecht had come out of the barn and was standing at the yard's edge.

They had been on this farm together for seventeen months. In that time they had exchanged the morning's schedule and whether the ground was passable and whether the rain was likely to hold, and nothing that needed more than that. Lonzo nodded to him. Albrecht nodded back. Neither said anything.

The truck turned out of the yard and went down Route 9.

She watched it until it passed the willow at the bend and was gone. The willow's branches had gone yellow at the tips, the early yellow that came before the leaves.

---

Her father padlocked the icehouse door himself, not long after. She watched through the window: he crossed the yard without hurrying, fitted the shackle through the hasp, pressed it closed. He stood with his hand on the lock for a moment, not doing anything with it. Then he went back to the barn.

She did the morning work. There was always work to do in a dairy barn in late March — the stalls, the milk lines, the new calf that needed checking twice before noon. The farm had its own schedule and did not care what else had happened in the yard.

Around midday her father came in from the barn, knocked the mud from his boots at the door, and hung his coat. Magda had made the white bean soup — Monday's soup, and this was a Thursday, but the soup was there regardless, on the stove where it always was. The bread was on its board. The lamp was on over the table.

Hannah came in and hung her coat on the hook by the door. She could feel the weight of the bird in the pocket — three inches of birch, lighter than she always expected when she closed her hand around it.

She sat down. Magda set a bowl in front of her and went back to the stove.

Outside, Route 9 ran east toward town and west toward the rest of Maine, and the truck was somewhere on it now, or had long since turned north toward Houlton. The icehouse sat at the edge of the yard with its door locked. Through the window the willow at the bend was yellow-tipped, the tips a little more yellow than they had been in the morning.

The bird was in her coat pocket and she would feel it there in the morning when she went out, and it would still be hers.

Her father reached for his bread.

Magda sat.

The farm went on.
