# Owen's Ledger

The inventory form had twelve columns and ran to four pages when she did it thoroughly, which she did every year in the first week of January. Eira set it on the chart table on the morning of January seventh and began at the left.

Dock hardware, itemized by category, quantity, condition. The hardware section always took longer than it should. Items had accumulated across years — some from Owen's inventory, some from hers, the category names drifting so that she couldn't always determine whether a "dock bollard, standard" and a "mooring bollard, harbor-grade" were two things or one thing entered twice by two different hands. She had been standardizing the categories since 1941 and was not yet done. She worked through the list: cleats by type and quantity, shackles, dock line coiled in the dock locker, winch components, the spare hardware in the deep bin under the north-facing shelf. The south mooring shackle showed rust at both joints — she had seen it in November, and it was worse now. She noted it and recommended replacement before the summer season.

The kerosene stove had been burning since seven and the office was as warm as it was going to get. With the coat on, that was warm enough. Outside the window the harbor showed its January shape. Five lobster boats at the inside moorings — Brann's Irene M., Hollis's boat, three others she recognized by the silhouettes of their cabins in the flat southern light. The commercial wharf was empty. The fleet came down to the lobstermen in January, and even they ran shorter days; they were out before light and back by early afternoon, and between their comings and goings the harbor was as quiet as it got.

Ice in the shallows. The tidal range in this harbor was eleven feet, which meant the shallows froze every night and thawed with each flood and froze again with each ebb. She had watched this for eight winters. The ice never broke apart quite the same way twice — sometimes a continuous plate from the north shore down to the mooring field, sometimes panels, sometimes absent entirely for two or three days before the overnight cold reasserted itself. Right now the flood was running, the panels separating, dark water widening between them. By four o'clock the water would recede and the process would reverse.

She worked through the hardware columns, column by column. This was reliable work. It had the quality of work that could be done in any weather, in any frame of mind, because it required only accuracy and time. She had learned the inventory in her first year from Owen's prior-year form, the same twelve columns, and had been using it since. The form knew what it needed. All she had to do was tell it true.

She finished hardware and moved to the mooring chain assessment.

The north mooring blocks had been on her list since October. She had noted wear on the anchor pins before the blow — written it in the September notes as something to watch, then again in October as something to address. She needed the block replacement date to know how much runway remained.

Nineteen thirty-two, she thought. But Owen's records would say.

The cabinet on the far wall held the annual logs going back thirty years — Owen's years, and before Owen a man named Ames whose record-keeping had been approximate enough that she'd checked several of his entries against the District office copies before she would trust them. Owen had taken the post in the late twenties. His logs ran through 1935. Then hers: 1936 in her own handwriting on page thirty-eight of the requisition book, where his stopped at page thirty-seven.

She had been using his logs as a format guide from her first week. Not deliberately, not as a studied practice — she had simply opened the volumes to see how the job had been done, and his handwriting was there, and she'd read it, and taken from it what the recording of the harbor required. What he documented. How he handled the line between a note and a notation.

She went to the cabinet and found 1932 on the lower shelves. The mooring block entry was in August: north blocks replaced, August 18, work completed by Alderman and crew, new anchor hardware, both sets. She noted it on the inventory form. Then she checked 1933 for any repair — nothing on the north blocks. Then she pulled 1934, for the inspection record.

Owen's handwriting in the 1934 log was the same as it was in all the volumes she had read over the years. She knew it by now the way you came to know any script you had read long and often enough — not letter by letter but as a whole, a characteristic rhythm of the line. It was a neat hand. Careful in its letterforms, smaller than she would have expected if she hadn't known Owen. His hands had been large, thick through the knuckle, the hands of a man who had handled lines since he was old enough to haul. The writing was another thing entirely — small and precise, as deliberate as a surveyor's marks.

She found the 1934 maintenance records. The north blocks had been inspected in June: condition satisfactory, no action needed. She noted it: last inspection June 1934, satisfactory. That put the blocks at twelve years since replacement. She filled in the form: present condition serviceable; wear on anchor pins within operational tolerance; recommend replacement within two to three years.

She was closing the 1934 volume when the October section opened under her hands.

It was the binding. Some years fell open at their most-used section — the spine working loose at the point of most handling. This one had been used in October, or had been consulted and reconsulted until the October pages separated from the rest. She hadn't used this volume enough to have worn it open herself. She steadied it on the chart table.

The entry was dated October 14, 1934.

*Body of male individual, identified as Elias Foss, fisherman, Vinalhaven. Reported missing from vessel 9/29/34. Recovered at end of commercial dock by night crew, E. Hollis, at approximately 0600 hrs October 14. Medical examiner J.H. Pell notified 0700; examination completed 0900; death attributed to drowning, circumstances consistent with extended exposure; estimated time of death 10/6 or 10/7. Body in water eleven to twelve days. Coast Guard District Portland notified, formal report filed. Family contacted per standard procedure. Body released to family October 16.*

The official documentation. Notification chain, examination, classification, resolution. It was complete — the structure she had learned from reading Owen's logs and had built her own entries on. A man missing twelve days, found at the end of the dock. Owen had written it fully and in the correct order.

Below the entry, set off by a line break and in a slightly smaller hand — the same hand, but the letters compressed, the pen moving faster or at a different hour:

*J.T. came in on the 12th. I met him at the dock. He was in a bad way and needed some time before he was in any state for the official record. This not entered at time; entering here as complete account.*

She read it again.

She read it a third time.

The stove ticked. Outside the window the harbor was gray and still. Brann's boat showed its green hull through the frost on the glass.

She stood at the chart table.

J.T. — not further identified. Someone who had come to the dock in the middle of the search, or after the finding, in a state Owen had judged unsuitable for the official record in that hour. Owen had met him there. He had waited, or stayed nearby, for whatever time the man needed. And then he had not written it when it happened. He had written it afterward, with the small notation that explained the gap: *not entered at time; entering here as complete account.*

The record was complete. The 1934 log had an entry that accounted for what had happened, and the entry was dated to the correct year, and anyone reading the 1934 log would find what had occurred. Owen had decided when to write it. But he had written it.

She looked at the window for a while. The flood tide was still running. The ice panels were wider apart than they'd been at seven, the dark water between them the color of the overcast above.

She thought about when Owen might have gone back to write the J.T. note — whether it had been a week later or a month. Whether J.T. was someone he'd known from before or someone he'd only met that morning at the dock. Whether writing it had felt like completing the record or like something else. The log didn't say, and he hadn't annotated the annotation.

She set the 1934 volume on the chart table and went to the shelf where the current logs were kept.

*Tuesday, October 26, 1943. Wind SSW at 14, backing. Barometric pressure 29.61, falling. Swell 4 feet at harbor mouth, increasing.*

*2100: fuel allocation check, Hollis and three lobster smacks. 2300: mooring check completed, fleet secure.*

She read it straight through. The 2300 entry was accurate in everything it contained. She had done the mooring check. The fleet had been secure. The barometric reading was correct for that night.

She stood with both volumes open on the chart table — Owen's 1934, her 1943 — the October pages of each showing side by side.

Owen's entry had a gap and said so. *Not entered at time; entering here as complete account.* The record was complete.

Hers had no such notation.

She closed the 1943 log.

She put the 1943 book back on the shelf and returned the 1934 volume to the cabinet. As she did she noticed the 1935 volume — thin, half the width of the others. Owen had started it in January and his entries ran through October. The last entry: a weather note, a routine mooring check, the approach of the November storm. She knew what it said.

She pushed the 1935 volume back into alignment with its neighbors and closed the cabinet.

Back at the chart table she picked up the inventory form and checked it through. Hardware: complete. Mooring chains: assessed and documented. Three staging planks at the far end needing replacement — she'd been watching them since September, and there was no point deferring the notation further. The dock ladder's second-from-bottom rung: stress cracking at the left joint, the same as it had been in September and unchanged in three months. She would need to write up the spring budget request in the next week — south mooring shackle, staging planks, dock ladder rung. These were the priorities.

The north mooring blocks she wrote on the final line: last replaced August 1932, twelve years, present condition serviceable, wear on anchor pins within tolerance, recommend replacement within two to three years.

She set the form aside and made tea from the tin on the stove shelf. The water had been keeping warm on the back of the stove since she'd boiled it that morning; it was still hot enough. She poured a cup and stood at the window.

The flood tide had done what she'd expected. The ice panels were well-separated now, open water showing the full width between them, the near shallows mostly clear. The light was the flat undifferentiated gray of a January midday — it would stay like this until it began to fail at three, the darkness coming in from the east without any transition from the gray.

She finished the tea and put the cup in the rack.

She dated the bottom of the inventory form, signed it, and put it in the current file.

The office was quiet. The stove ticked and settled.

She put on her coat.

The south mooring shackle needed a closer look before the tide turned. She had noted the rust in November and again today on the form, but she wanted to see what it looked like up close before she put a specific dollar figure on the replacement request. Surface rust at the joint was one thing; rust into the threads was another; the form she sent to Portland needed to know the difference.

She lifted the door handle while she turned it — the way you had to in cold weather, the way she had learned from Owen, the latch she had never had the carpenter fix — and went out onto the dock.
