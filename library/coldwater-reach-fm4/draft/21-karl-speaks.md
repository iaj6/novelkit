# Karl Speaks

The radio had gone on until past eleven the night before. She had heard it through the floorboards — her father's voice first, calling her mother, *Magda, komm* — and then the radio sound rising a little above its usual murmur, the evening news expanded to something that required more time than evenings usually gave it. She had lain in the dark at the top of the stairs and listened to the voice of it without the words, and when the voice she could not make out stopped she heard her father say something low and then say nothing more, and the radio went off, and the house was the house in the June dark with the insects going and the cows settled for the night in the barn.

In the morning he said nothing.

Not in German, not in English. He drank his coffee with both hands around the mug and she ate her bread and Magda was at the stove and the kitchen was the kitchen at six in the morning in June with the flies already starting where the cream pitcher had sat overnight. When she looked at him he was looking at the glass in the window, or at something on the other side of the glass that was not the yard.

*Er weiß es schon*, she thought. Whatever they said on the radio last night, her father was holding it now in the way he held things since the war started — somewhere interior, somewhere she could see the outside of but not the inside.

The bread needed finishing. The cows needed doing.

June cows were restless. The flies were back — the black biting kind that made the Holsteins shuffle and switch their tails and ran the milking time up to close to an hour instead of the forty minutes of March. She worked the far half while her father worked the near, and they went through it in the silence that was the usual silence of morning milking, except that this morning the silence had something in it. The quality of held air, a room where something had just been said or was about to be said, and neither had happened. By six-thirty the cans were set and the light was fully up and she went to the north field.

The bean stakes she and Karl had started setting two days ago needed the second check — three had gone in crooked and she could see it from the end of the row, and June meant the plants were already reaching for the first stakes, already committed to whatever structure she gave them. She walked the rows with the mallet and reset the crooked ones, tamping each level before she moved on. The field smelled the way it smelled in the good part of June: damp-warm, the particular richness of soil that had taken last week's rain and was working with it now. The beans were up, curled at their tips, pale green, tender and already sure of themselves.

Lonzo came at seven. She heard the truck on Route 9 before it turned onto the drive and then saw the dust and then heard him park and come around with his stool and his rifle and the Bangor Daily News folded under his arm. He set up at the barn door in his usual configuration — stool angled to see the icehouse and the near field at once, rifle across his knees, newspaper folded in his lap. The ordinary shape the day took when it began.

The prisoners came out from the icehouse at ten past seven. Fischer first, pulling on his work gloves as he walked. Brunner and Krebs behind him. Then Karl, a half-step after the others, blinking once in the morning light the way he always did — she had noticed this about him in October and it had not changed, his eyes adjusting from the dimness of the icehouse more slowly than the rest, like a man who had spent a good deal of time recently with his eyes half-closed.

They came across the yard. Lonzo watched them come.

He opened the paper. He had been reading the Pacific section first since at least February — she knew this from something he'd mentioned in passing back in the winter — but today he opened it straight to the front and looked at it for a moment and then looked up at the four men.

"ALLIED ARMIES LAND IN FRANCE," he said. The voice he used when he needed to give information to the whole yard without making an occasion of it: level, factual, no performance in it. "Yesterday, June sixth. Normandy. Multiple Allied divisions. Troops reported ashore."

He folded the newspaper back along its crease.

Fischer and Brunner had stopped walking. They looked at each other — the quick sideways exchange she had spent eight months learning to read and still could not read all the way through. Brunner made a sound that was not quite a word, low in his chest, and she could not tell from the sound whether it was the end of something or the beginning of something; it might have been both. Fischer looked at the field for a long moment. Then he picked up his tools and went to work.

Krebs said nothing. He had been the quiet one all along.

Karl was already at the north fence. She had watched him change course for the north field as soon as he came through the yard — his mind already at the next task, the way it usually was. When Lonzo read the headline Karl had a stake in one hand and the string line in the other and he did not look up. He checked the string line. He went to the next stake.

He heard every word, she thought. And set the stake anyway.

Her father came out of the barn at eight with a milk can he had missed in the first pass. He carried it to the stand and set it and stood there with his hands open at his sides looking out at the field in the direction of the north fence without quite looking at it. He stood there for the length of time it took her to walk from the east end of the row to the third stake in. Then he went back into the barn.

He had heard the news last night. The landing had been in the house since six o'clock the evening before and he had said nothing at breakfast in any language — not the usual *Guten Morgen*, not *Noch Kaffee*, not even the silence that was companionable rather than the other kind. He had held it all morning alone, whatever he held with it, the calculation she could not quite see him making. She thought of the fence in May: his hands leaving the fence rail, going into his coat pockets, the ten seconds of stillness before the work resumed.

She went back to her stakes.

---

The afternoon came in flat and held there. By two o'clock the heat was at the part of itself where it stayed for three hours and the north field was full of the June smell of plants working — bean leaves, the soil drying on top while it stayed wet three inches down, the occasional warm smell of the cow barn when the south wind came through.

She was back at the north fence with the second-row stakes. Karl was on the other side. He had been working his own second row from the west end, east, and she had been working from the east end, west, and they had been moving toward the same section of fence all afternoon. She had been aware of this for the last hour and had not mentioned it to herself.

When they reached the middle section — six feet of fence between them and the same three stakes needing setting on either side — she said: "Did you hear the news?"

More words at once than she had said to him since February, maybe since before the bird.

He looked up. The gray-blue eyes, the wind-chapped skin that the warm months had eased somewhat but not entirely, the settled quality she had first read as tiredness in October and had come to understand as something more considered than tiredness. He looked at her and nodded.

She waited. She tamped her stake and straightened and let the silence sit there.

He said: "I did not think I would live to see it."

The English was deliberate. Each word placed before the next one came, the way he set stakes — level first, then tamp, no shortcuts. He had spent the winter reading Lonzo's newspapers in the icehouse by lamplight; she had seen him through the window sometimes in January, the paper held at an angle toward the lamp, his lips moving in the slight way of someone reading carefully in a second language. It sounded like something he had composed first and then read aloud, and the reading was true to the composition.

She sat with the sentence for a moment. She did not try to open it further.

"Are you glad?" she said.

He didn't answer right away. His hands were still on the stake. He looked toward the far end of the field — Brunner and Fischer and Krebs at the south trelliswork, Lonzo visible beyond them at the barn with his paper, the sky above the field the even blue of a June afternoon with nothing happening in it. He looked back at her.

"I think I am tired," he said.

She said: "Yes."

Nothing else. He went to his next stake. She went to hers. Lonzo sat at his end with the paper on his knee and the rifle propped at its angle beside him, not aimed at anything and not forgotten. The afternoon continued to be what it was: warm, green-smelling, the farm doing what June farms did.

---

She carried the two sentences through the rest of the day. She just had them, the way she had the weight of the mallet, the smell of the soil, the specific tired that arrived at four o'clock in the afternoon of a full farm day.

*I did not think I would live to see it.*

*I think I am tired.*

She had expected something else — she hadn't known this until he spoke and the something else didn't arrive. What she had instead was Karl in his second language, carefully, saying what was actually true.

He was nineteen. He had been a soldier since he was seventeen, two years of war as the whole of his adult life. What he had at the end of it — when the news came that the end was beginning — was being tired.

He would be repatriated when the war ended. Back to Franconia, to somewhere fifty kilometers from where her father had been born, in a place she had never seen and could not picture except as a word her father had made German-shaped by the way he never quite said it. She would stay here. The arrangement was the same whether she was glad about it or not.

The evening milking ran to six. She and her father worked the cows in the usual silence, which by now was just the silence of two people who knew the work well enough to do it without talking about it. Magda had the bean soup on when Hannah came through the kitchen door — white beans, Thursday's soup, right on its day. Magda turned once when Hannah came in, a small nod, and turned back to the stove.

Her father said grace the way he always said it, in German, the words she had known since before she understood them: *Herr, segne diese Gaben, die wir von Deiner Güte empfangen.* Lord bless these gifts which we receive from Your goodness. His voice was even. Magda sat and folded her hands and unfolded them and picked up her spoon. Hannah ate her bread and the soup and the last of the spring radishes from the cold cellar, and no one said anything in any language, and the kitchen was the kitchen on a Thursday evening in June with the long light going in the west window.

Her father's hand on the bread plate. The farmer's hand — the calluses on the palms and on the first fingers, the right little finger slightly bent from the break that had healed wrong when she was five, the hands she had been knowing since before she had hands of her own. Whatever he held with it was still in him, held in the old way, the way a man held things for twenty years and knew by now not to put them down in a room where other people might trip on them.

*Sei nicht so neugierig*, Magda would have said, in the time before she stopped saying things. She was still curious.

She helped with the dishes. Her father turned the radio to WLBZ for the nine o'clock broadcast and then went out to the barn for the last check, and she could hear the announcer's voice under the sound of the water, the same voice that had been running through every kitchen in the country all day. France. The landing. What was known. What was not.

She went upstairs at eight.

---

Through the open window came the farm sounds: the cows settling, a loose board against the icehouse wall where the warm afternoons expanded the wood and the cool evenings brought it back, the June insects constant in the yard. The radio voice came up through the floor until her father came in from the barn and switched it off and the house went quiet.

She had heard him speak in English, carefully, and what he said was true.

She thought: *das ist es*.

She turned over and closed her eyes.
