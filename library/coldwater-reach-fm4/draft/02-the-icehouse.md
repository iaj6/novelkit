# The Icehouse

The barn door was stiff with October damp. She pushed it open with her shoulder, the way she always did now, and stood a moment in the opening before she went inside — her father had taught her this without teaching it, by doing it every morning alongside her until she did it herself: hold still, let the animals hear you first. The dark inside was warmer than the yard. She could feel it on her face. Behind her the east field ran black to the tree line, Route 9 invisible through the maples beyond.

She went in.

The smell came at her the way it always did: the warm hide smell of twenty-two animals in an enclosed space, the manure smell she'd stopped noticing by September, and over everything the sweet rot of silage from the silo on the north end. She'd grown up smelling this. She didn't know what her room smelled like to someone who didn't live there. She went to the peg, hung her coat, rolled her sleeves.

Her father was already in the third bay, his lantern on the hook above the grain box. His back was to her, his shoulders moving in the slow careful rhythm of the early work. He kept a low murmur going with the animals — not words she could make out, just the rhythm of a voice — and the nearest Holstein turned toward it. He didn't look up when she came in.

She waited a moment.

*Fang an*, he said, without turning.

She started.

Twenty-two of them, and she knew them by now the way you knew anything you spent two hours with every morning in the dark: not by thinking but by the accumulated weight of attention that had stopped being attention and become something else — the kind of knowledge that was in the hands and not the head. She moved stall to stall. Her father worked from the other end, not speaking, and the lantern hung between them and swung slightly when the nearest animal shifted. The barn had its own sound at this hour, the sound that was not any single thing but the sum of all of it: breath, movement, the rhythmic work, the chains.

Through the window above the third stall she could see the icehouse.

She hadn't meant to look. She had looked. There was a lamp burning inside — the light leaked out around the covered window in a narrow seam along the bottom of the curtain — and they were already awake in there, or some of them were. She didn't know at what hour they woke, or what the whitewashed partitioned room was like at five in the morning, whether the two cots and two pallets the Houlton men had installed were adequate or whether the four of them lay awake long before the work started. She had wondered about this in the first week, had constructed and discarded several versions of what it was like to be inside that room, and had eventually let the wondering go.

Three weeks since they'd come. The farm had arranged itself around their presence the way it arranged itself around any new constraint — not happily or unhappily, just as a fact of the season. They worked the east field in the mornings and the north end of the corn in the afternoons. They ate their meals in the icehouse. In the evenings they went back to the icehouse and the door closed and the light showed at the curtain's edge until it didn't.

She'd watched their arrival from this same window: the Coastal Patrol truck pulling up on the road, the four of them climbing down with their packs, the men from Camp Houlton doing the handover on the gravel while her father stood in the yard and didn't speak. Her mother in the doorway behind the screen. Hannah here, at this window, uncertain what she'd expected but watching.

She went back to work.

---

Lonzo came at seven the way he always came — the truck on the road, the cab door, his boots on the gravel — and settled onto his stool at the barn door. He was sixty-three or close to it, with a face that had been weathered into something that communicated very little. He pulled the Bangor Daily from his coat pocket and opened it. He had a son in the Pacific who didn't write often enough, Hannah had heard her father say to her mother once; she didn't know if this was why he sat the way he sat, with the patience of a man who had decided a long time ago what he was going to do with an afternoon and was going to do it regardless. He laid the Springfield across his knees.

He looked up when she looked at him.

She nodded. He nodded.

That was all.

At quarter past seven the icehouse door opened and all four of them came out into the gray cold and walked in a loose cluster toward the near field where the last rows of winter corn waited. She watched from the barn doorway — the way she watched most mornings now, a habit that had started without her deciding to start it and that she hadn't been able to stop. Three of them she knew well enough by now to have put shapes to, though not names: the heavyset one whose step she could hear from inside the barn, who walked with his shoulders rolling, always first; the thin one who carried a slight lean to the left, a habit of the body she recognized from men who had done hard work on one side for too long; the older one with the bald patch, who moved deliberately, as if he had been told where to go so many times that he'd stopped having opinions about the direction. The fourth walked quickly at the back of the group, keeping pace with the others by moving faster, the way you moved when you were the shortest.

She'd heard Lonzo call his name three days after they arrived — *Reinmar*, sharp, pointing at the east fence — and had not forgotten it.

Karl Reinmar. The youngest of the four.

---

She was carrying feed buckets to the far end of the barn when she heard the clatter. Small sound, something dropped. She set the buckets down and looked: a short-handled cultivating tool — the kind her father used for the kitchen garden bed — had fallen into the gap at the base of the east fence post. The fence ran close to the barn wall on that side, close enough that something dropped from the near field could slip into the gap without being reachable from outside without going all the way around to the gate.

She picked it up.

He was on the other side of the fence.

She hadn't heard him come to the fence. He was two feet away through the boards and wire, closer than she had been to any of them, and there was nothing to do about it — no time to decide how to hold her face or what to think about first. She'd had some half-formed expectation of something, she realized now, standing here with the tool in her hand: some quality to the face that would explain what he was or what it meant that he was here. There was no such quality. He was young, that was the first thing — younger than she'd thought from the distance of the barn window, which had reduced all four of them to shapes. His face was wind-chapped, the skin across his cheekbones roughened by weeks of October cold. His hair had been cut short and not carefully. He had a tired quality that was not the tiredness of a single bad night but the kind that accumulated over time until it stopped showing as tiredness and showed only as the particular stillness of a face that had stopped expecting things to change. His eyes were gray-blue, the color of the harbor on a cold morning when the sky was low.

He was looking at the tool, not at her.

*Danke*, he said.

She pushed the tool through the gap at the bottom of the fence and he took it and stepped back and went back to work.

From his stool at the barn door, Lonzo turned a page in the Bangor Daily. The paper made its flat, dry sound. She had picked up a tool that had fallen and passed it through a fence and a person had said thank you.

She picked up the feed buckets. Her hands did not feel like they had done anything remarkable. She carried the feed to the far stall and finished the work.

---

She walked the three miles the long way, under the maples along the edge of the Hallet property, where the leaves were still coming down in the cold morning. She counted things when she didn't want to think — fence posts, the crows on the wire above the Osborn field — and got to twenty-three fence posts before she stopped counting and let herself think instead.

*Danke.* She had known what that meant since she was four years old, since before she'd known what anything in English meant. Her father's voice in the kitchen before it had been her father's house, before the rules had settled: *Danke, Liebling.* He hadn't called her that in a long time. The word was such a small one that using it barely counted as language, barely counted as deciding to speak.

She thought: Karl Reinmar is nineteen. He has been in Tunisia and in Houlton and now he is on a farm in Maine in October. He said *Danke* for a cultivating tool through a fence. She had handed the tool through because there was nothing else to do with it.

She thought: he is a person. She arranged the facts in a row — the tool, the fence, the word, the gray-blue eyes not looking at her — and they were still just facts. She walked the rest of the three miles and arrived at school before the first bell.

---

The lesson was on indirect discourse. *Oratio obliqua*, Mr. Klein said — the grammar of reported speech, how a statement became secondary when it was embedded in another clause, the verb shifted, the meaning changed depending on what tense the main clause had been given. She wrote the examples in her notebook and worked through the practice sentences twice. She got three wrong the first time and corrected them. She thought about the fence and told herself to stop thinking about the fence.

At lunch Ada Wheeler sat across from her with her tray.

"Raymond Foss says his father says the Coast Guard thinks it was a German agent," Ada said. "The body on the breakwater. That he came off a submarine. A spy."

Hannah ate a bite of her sandwich. The body on the breakwater had been town news for a week. She'd heard three versions: the spy, a merchant sailor from a vessel wrecked on the outer ledges in the blow, and something involving a man from Ellsworth and a debt she hadn't quite followed. Raymond Foss's father worked at the cannery and was known to have opinions on many things.

"Maybe," Hannah said.

"Doesn't it worry you? Right here in Coldwater?"

She thought about saying: Raymond Foss's father doesn't know anything about submarines. She thought about saying: the body is in Rockland now with the Coast Guard. She thought about saying: a lot of things wash up after a blow. She didn't say any of these.

"I didn't know him," she said.

Ada looked at her as if this had missed the point. Two other girls sat down and the conversation moved on and Hannah ate the rest of her sandwich.

---

The evening milking was done by six and the dark had come in solid before she got back to the house. She stood a moment in the yard and looked at the kitchen window — the lamp lit inside, the yellow square of it in the dark, her mother's shape moving past — and then went in.

Her father was at the far end of the table with the German-language Bangor Daily, the edition he received by post on Thursdays. He read it folded open to the inside pages, elbows on the table, in the way he'd always read. Her mother was at the stove, her back to the room, stirring something that smelled of potato.

Hannah sat down with her Latin book.

The kitchen had its own kind of silence in the evenings, a silence she'd grown up inside, which meant she knew its textures the way she knew the animals' sounds: what it weighed when nothing was about to happen and what it weighed when something was. The clock above the door ran a minute fast. The stove made its sounds. Her mother's spoon made its sounds. Her father's newspaper turned once every several minutes. Outside the window the dark was complete.

She worked through the indirect discourse drill and corrected the errors she found. Her father reached the end of the page and let the paper fall slightly in his hands and read the next page without turning it, the way he did when he'd found something he was going to stay on for a while.

He said something under his breath. A single word, low, not directed at her or at anyone. The sound a person made when reading something that had confirmed what they'd known it was going to say. She glanced up. His expression was unchanged, his eyes on the page.

She went back to the drill.

Then her mother said: *Wir hätten früher gehen sollen.*

She said it to the stove, or to the window above the stove, her voice flat and without inflection — not quiet, exactly, not a whisper.

Her father looked up.

They looked at each other across the kitchen — he at his end with the newspaper still folded in his hands, she at the stove with the spoon held out to one side where it wouldn't drip. The clock made its sound. The stove made its sound. Something passed between them that Hannah could not have named, and she did not try. Four seconds. Five.

Her father looked back at the paper.

Her mother looked back at the pot.

Hannah looked at the indirect discourse drill. In the margin beside the third set of sentences she had written, before she had decided to write anything: *Wir hätten früher gehen sollen.* She read it. She pressed the pen down and crossed it out, once and again and again, until the paper was dark there and nothing showed through.

---

She took the lamp upstairs after supper and finished the last of the homework and then sat on the edge of her bed and didn't move for a while. From the window, when she lifted the curtain, she could see the shape of the icehouse against the sky — just the roofline, the dark mass of it — and below the roofline the narrow strip of light at the curtain's edge, still burning. Whatever they did in the evenings in there she didn't know. Whether they talked or read or sat with their own language, with their own thinking, in the whitewashed room where the cold must come up through the boards. Whether they thought about the places they'd come from or had decided that direction didn't help.

The strip of light at the icehouse window went out.

She sat with that a moment — the dark where the light had been, the shape of the roof still present, the sky above it that was slightly lighter than the roofline. Then she blew out her own lamp and lay down in the dark and listened.

The barn sounds came through the wall: an animal shifting in its stall, the knock of a board, the wind at the eaves. The silage smell moved through on the air and then didn't and then did again, the way smells moved at night when the temperature dropped and the air settled.

*Wir hätten früher gehen sollen.*

She knew what the words meant. What they were about — what her mother had meant by *earlier*, what *leaving* was and what had been left and what had been meant to be left behind — she didn't know yet. She might not know for a long time. She was probably going to have to find out.
