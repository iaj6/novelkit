# The Spring Opening

April, and the harbor moving.

The ice had been out of the main channel since the third week of March — long gray pans of it riding the ebb tide south past the breakwater mouth, dissolving in the chop before they reached open water. She had watched from the office window without going out. There was nothing useful to do about ice going out; it went when it went, same as it always had.

The first week of April she had walked all the mooring lines. Three on the east row had ice-chafe bad enough to need ricing; she found one on the main float that had chafed through to the splice entirely, which meant it had been weakening since before the freeze and she had missed it in the fall inspection. She replaced the whole length. She had riced the east row lines herself, kneeling on the dock planking in the early cold with the caulking iron, and entered both repairs in the mooring log. Standard notation. The usual spring assessment of what winter had taken.

Now it was the standard spring inventory, and she worked through it the way she always did: depth soundings off the north pier at low tide, the mooring condition check, the fleet registration review. Ten years of spring openings, and the sequence was her own by now — not Owen's sequence handed down but the order she had settled on in her second year, when she understood what the harbor needed first. Depth soundings before slip assignments; mooring check before registration.

Depth soundings: two days running, the tide gauge and the weighted line, working along the north pier face from the bollard to the end. The year before's sounding log had two shallow readings at the channel's edge, same location both times, which meant winter silting from the January storm surge. She marked the position on the sounding chart and flagged it for the summer dredge schedule; the channel was still inside clearance for the second float's boats, but narrowly. She would watch it through the month.

Fleet registration review: nine boats on the main float, three on the east pier, one at the commercial wharf. She pulled the registration files and went through them against the winter registry.

The fleet was smaller than it had been in 1940. She had taken twenty-three boats through the spring opening of that year; this spring it was thirteen. The difference was in the ledger if you wanted to count it — boats sold, men gone, the war and the fuel allocation together. She did not count it this morning. She went through the files against the registry and noted what had changed.

Two new registrations. The first was a lobster dragger that had come down from Owls Head when the operation there folded — the owner, a man named Severance, had done the Portland transfer and come to her for the slip assignment. She had assigned him the outside berth on the east pier, right for the dragger's draft, and entered him in the Coldwater fleet ledger: vessel name, tonnage, operator's certification, assigned slip. Standard form. The second was Danny Jessup's first boat, a twenty-two-foot trap boat he had worked two seasons to buy. The engine was sound; she had checked the documentation and the certifications and assigned him berth seven on the main float, the small-boat row. She entered both and noted the dates.

Two boats out since last fall.

Haskell's sloop, sold in November to a Rockland man. She had sent the release notice to Knox County in November and heard nothing since, which was how Knox County handled these things; the transfer was complete in the record.

Galen Fuller's dragger. Ruth Fuller had renamed it and reregistered it in her own name, coming in on a Monday morning in February with the completed forms and a check for the registration fee. She had sat across the chart table from Eira and signed where the form required. She said she was thinking about running the lobster route herself come May, if she could find a mate who knew the ground. Eira had said there were a few men who knew it; they had talked through who was available and what the pound was paying. Then Ruth Fuller had stood and shaken Eira's hand and gone out. Eira had entered the registration in the fleet ledger under the new name, Ruth Fuller as registered operator, and filed the original in the back cabinet. Fifteen minutes, start to finish. She had not stopped over it; the paperwork was the same paperwork.

She had processed Robert Cates's spring mooring renewal the day before. Berth 14 — his grandfather's berth and his father's berth before him, the third-generation berth that had sat empty for fourteen months. He had come home from France in February, thirty pounds lighter. She had heard this from his father in March, running into him outside the post office: the old man red-eyed and saying Robert was standing, that he was home. By April, Cates had been out in his boat three times — twice coming in with a decent haul, once with nothing. A normal spring. She wrote the mooring renewal in the ledger: berth 14, April 18, 1945, confirmed and paid.

By mid-morning she had the registration review done and the depth soundings entered. She checked the mooring assignments against the harbor chart. Everything in its place. She set the chart on the drying rack and went to make coffee.

---

At noon she walked to Beal's for flour and two kerosene wicks.

The radio was on. It was always on at Beal's now, had been since November — the Ardennes and then the push east and then the Rhine crossings, Manny Beal keeping WLBZ on the shelf behind the counter where he could reach the dial without putting down what he was doing. She had grown used to the sound of it coming through the store door as she came up the steps.

Today the voice was different. Not the tone; the tone was always measured. What the voice was saying.

Three men at the counter: Harris Vane, Elliot Pease, old Cullen from the lobster pound. Manny had his coffee cup set down and both hands on the counter's edge.

She went to the flour bin and measured what she needed.

Germany. Berlin. The German state radio and then the Allied command's confirmation: April 30th. She listened and tied off the flour bag.

At the counter she waited while Manny tallied her items. Harris Vane and Elliot Pease were talking, low — not the kind of talking that calculated what the news meant for the next push, the next month, the winter holding or breaking. This was the other kind. Old Cullen said nothing and looked at the counter.

Manny made change. She thanked him.

Six blocks to the office. The harbor was opening. The war in Europe was ending. She walked the six blocks in the April air, the breakwater visible at the channel's edge, the water flat and gray-green in the midday light.

---

In the afternoon she went to the breakwater.

Not for any reason the log would record. She went the way she went sometimes — down the wharf, left at the bollard, out along the granite face. Two hundred feet. The blocks cut and laid in the 1890s, rough-faced and gray where they were above the waterline, dark and algae-striped below it, the stripe marking the tidal range exactly: eleven feet, the same it had always been.

Harbor on one side. Open water on the other. The April chop, a foot and a half, the surface running gray-green where the channel went deep. She stood at the end.

She had been standing at this breakwater since 1935. October of that year she had worn Owen's work gloves because her own were still packed and the post was a month old; the gloves were too large for her hands and the tools were Owen's tools, and she had been learning what they were for by using them. She had stood here in January 1944 when the ice came in early and locked the east pier's second float, working through the dredge sequence and what would have to wait. She had stood here on the morning of June 7th, 1944, the harbor quiet before the boats came in, not yet knowing whether Robert Cates or Galen Fuller had been on a Normandy beach the morning before.

Nine winters. The breakwater kept no account of any of it. Granite and seawater and the tidal stripe.

She thought about Horak.

Jan Václav Horak — Czech, displaced, last known Halifax in October 1942. The name had come in a letter from the Coast Guard regional office in Portland in April 1944, six months after the body came in off the blow. It was in the cabinet with the 1944 binders. She had seen him on the commercial dock on the night of October 26th, 1943, moving south from the herring shed toward the lobster landing. She had heard the language, carried it seven months, and Marcus Klein had placed it for her: Czech, the consonant cluster, the first-syllable stress. She had written it all into the late entry in October 1944 — the standard format, her own hand, the record of what she had seen and had not logged at the time. The record was complete.

The second person on the dock. The one who had been there when she came around the corner of the lobster landing and had walked away before she reached them — not running, just walking, back toward the head of the wharf and out of the dock light. Nineteen months since the night of the blow and she had nothing more than she'd had that first morning, when she opened the tide log and read the gap her silence had left.

She didn't know if she needed to.

The harbor log was not a record of everything. It was a record of what a harbormaster could enter in good faith. Owen had known this. His 1934 entry about J.T. — she had found it in January the year before, the deferred notation eventually made whole: what he had seen, in the standard format, when he had what he needed to put it down. He had not entered what he couldn't confirm. Neither had she.

She stood at the breakwater's end another minute. The spray at the south corner where the chop hit the face. The lobster pound at the far side of the harbor, Cullen's truck already back in the lot. The open water south, gray under the April overcast, the horizon gone into the sky.

She turned and walked back.

The wharf, the cedar-shingled office at the foot of it, the window with the tide board visible from ten feet: May figures, her handwriting. The numbers the numbers regardless of what the radio at Beal's was saying.

---

Owen's ledger was in the cabinet. She had not opened it since October's log review, and she would not open it today.

She sat at the chart table.

Her log was open to Wednesday, May 2nd. The day's entry was started but not finished — the six-forty-five tide, the weather notation, the morning fleet status. The spaces below were blank.

She picked up her pen.

The noon notation: wind shift from northwest to west-northwest, gusting ten to twelve. Fleet status unchanged. The Jessup boy had gone out at ten-thirty and come back in at noon; she had noted it in the transit log and she transferred the notation now: departure time, return, no significant events.

The two-o'clock mooring check. All lines good. East pier, second slip — slight fraying at the nip, the same she had noted Tuesday. She entered it formally: slip number, location, description, assessment. *Monitor. No action yet.* Next week she would pull the line and measure what was there.

Hanson brothers: due Thursday to open their season. Three slips reserved on the main float, the same positions every year. She wrote the pending reservation, the dates, the standard mooring-fee condition.

Robert Cates's renewal confirmation. She had entered it yesterday but she added the season notation now in the space reserved for it — *Spring 1945* — and underlined the year the way she always underlined the first confirmed renewal of a new season.

Outside the window the harbor held what it held. The boats on the main float riding the small chop of the afternoon, the light on the water thin and flat in the overcast. A gull on the bollard at the dock's end. The breakwater at the channel's edge, gray in the afternoon, the algae line invisible at this hour of tide but there — she knew where it was.

She turned to the next page.

*May 1945.* She wrote the heading in the top right corner the way she always did, the month and year and nothing else. Below it, the entry for Wednesday was still unfinished — the afternoon's remaining notations waiting, the six o'clock tide still to come, the evening fleet check, whatever else the harbor brought before the day was done.

She kept writing.
