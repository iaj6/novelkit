# What Is and Isn't Dangerous

The third period exercises were still on his desk when Hannah Pfeiffer knocked and came in before he had answered.

He had said it at the start of term, as he said it every year: the door open after four o'clock meant questions were permitted. It was not an invitation, merely a statement of availability; occasionally a student appeared. She was not here about Latin.

She did not sit in the third desk on the window side, where she had occupied the same position for three terms. She sat in the desk directly adjacent to his, pulled it slightly forward, and folded her hands on the desktop.

"I need to ask you something," she said.

"Then ask," he said.

---

She was prepared. He could see the structure of it — the way she moved through the categories she had decided on, nothing extraneous, nothing omitted. He had taught her for a year and a half; he knew how she thought when she was ready to think. She was not given to the kind of preparation that performs itself. When she had something to say, she said it.

Since approximately June, she told him, she had been having conversations with Karl Reinmar at the south fence section of the Pfeiffer property. Not every day. The work brought them to the same section two or three times each week, sometimes less, and on those occasions, when Private Reed was occupied with another part of the perimeter, they spoke. The conversations were brief — five minutes, sometimes ten, sometimes less.

What they talked about: the farm. The drainage of the south field, which backed up in wet years and required particular management. The question of what to put in after the corn stubble turned under — both of them favored winter rye, for reasons that had the same agricultural sense behind them and arrived from different directions. His English. In German, when German came up, he spoke a dialect she recognized from her father.

She had not given him anything outside the established program terms. No food beyond the regular mealtimes. No documents. Nothing of military use, in either direction.

She paused.

She said: she had not received anything.

The pause was less than a full breath, and he set his pencil down. He did not comment on it. He asked his first question.

---

"Has Private Reed filed any incident report with the Coastal Patrol that you're aware of? Or has any official — the patrol, the county, anyone — come to your family about the fence section specifically?"

"No," she said.

"Have you passed anything to Karl Reinmar, or received anything from him — food, documents, written communication, any object?"

She held his eyes for a moment. Then she reached into the pocket of her coat and set something on the desk between them.

It was a wooden bird, roughly three inches in length, carved from a single piece of softwood. The grain ran along the body; the tail had been shaped carefully, if not with any particular refinement. Pine, probably, or white cedar — the wood was light. It had been carried: the surface was smooth at the breast and at the base in the way of something handled many times over a period of months.

He picked it up. He turned it once, examining it with the attention he gave to any object he needed to assess correctly — weight, material, what it was and what it was not — and set it back.

"When," he said.

"He left it at the south fence post. In July."

He considered it for a moment longer. Then he pushed it back across the desk toward her.

"That is not contraband," he said.

---

She picked it up without looking at it.

He thought about how to give her the accurate account — not simplified, not reshaped for reassurance, but actually accurate, so that she would know precisely where she stood. He had found, over the past two years, that she received precision better than most people did. She had not come here for comfort.

"The regulations governing prisoner of war labor placements derive from two sources," he said. "The terms of the Geneva Convention, which govern the custody and treatment of prisoners under international law, and the domestic regulations under the Emergency Farm Labor program, which govern the specifics of farm placements and what is required of civilian host communities. The categories of prohibited civilian conduct that carry legal weight are four."

He set them out.

"Assisting an escape attempt — providing any assistance to a prisoner attempting to leave the custody of his escort. Passing military intelligence, or any information that could assist enemy military operations. Providing weapons, tools, or any material with military application." He paused. "The fourth category, which the domestic regulations treat with particular severity, is romantic or sexual relations between civilian women and prisoners of war. That category exists, it is enforced, and the consequences for both parties are not small. I mention it specifically because it is treated differently from the others, and you should know that."

She had not moved.

"None of what you have described falls into any of these categories," he said. "What you have described is a technical violation of the escort arrangement's terms, which specify that civilian interaction with prisoners is supervised and limited to work-related instruction. That is a violation of the arrangement's spirit. It is not a violation of any statute, and it is not a Convention violation. The distinction is meaningful, and the Coastal Patrol knows that it is meaningful."

He had come to know these distinctions carefully. The internment frameworks governing enemy alien status and prisoner status had provisions that overlapped in some areas and diverged sharply in others; he had read the relevant sections when the farm placement program began, and he had read them with the specific attention of someone who had lived under a related but distinct set of provisions for two years, at the Sherbrooke facility and in the administrative proceedings that followed it. The distinctions were not academic to him. They had been practical.

"In practice," he continued, "every farm placement that has been operating for more than a season has some version of what you are describing. The Coastal Patrol is not unaware of this. They manage it by leaving the specific judgment to the escort."

"Lonzo," she said.

"Private Reed has been at your property since September of last year. He knows what happens at that fence section. If he has not reported these conversations, he has made a decision not to. That decision is within his authority." He looked at her. "I think his judgment is probably correct."

---

She was still holding the bird. Not looking at it — looking at the desk surface in front of her, at some middle distance — but she had it in her hand.

"Did I do something wrong," she said.

He had seen it from the moment she sat down, underneath the careful categories of her account.

He thought about it for a moment — not because he was uncertain of the answer but because the answer had two parts that needed to be kept distinct from each other, and collapsing them would not serve her.

"You did something human," he said. "Those are sometimes the same thing. In this case I do not think they are."

She looked at him.

He did not elaborate on this. He said: "But I would stop."

"Because of the risk," she said.

"Because of the risk. Not because the conversations were wrong — I want that distinction to hold. The war is moving toward its end. He will be repatriated when it does, probably within the next year. Between now and that point, neither of you benefits from becoming more visible than you already are." He paused. "What you have described suggests you have been largely invisible. Private Reed has exercised his judgment; your father knows and has not acted on it. I would not test either of those conditions further."

She absorbed this.

"He told me to be careful," she said. "Not to stop."

"I know." And because it was accurate: "I am saying the same thing. The difference between *be careful* and *stop* is the difference between a person who understands what is at stake and a person who does not. Your father understands."

---

She was quiet for a moment. The afternoon light through the east windows had moved to its low October angle, the shadows crossing the floor at length, the room holding its warmth against the cold beginning to come in at the sills. Outside, the school yard was empty.

"I think my father knows more about why I should be careful than he's told me," she said. She said it without emphasis, not quite looking at him, looking instead at the desk between them — the stack of exercises, the pencil lying across the top sheet.

He considered what he knew and what he was going to do with what he knew. He did this the way he did most things that required precision: without rushing to the conclusion before he had located it correctly.

"I think," he said, "that your father is a man who has made some decisions about what he knows and what he does not need to say." He paused. "Like most people."

She looked at him. The light was low and slanted and her face in it had the quality of someone reading something carefully, trying to determine what the text was actually saying. He held her eyes. He did not add anything.

He had said it about Albrecht Pfeiffer, who was, as far as Marcus could read the available evidence, a man carrying a particular knowledge across twenty years — knowledge of a specific kind, the kind that accumulated around certain decisions made in certain years in certain parts of Europe, and that did not dissolve when you crossed an ocean.

After a moment she looked away. She put the bird in her coat pocket. She lifted her bag from the floor and stood.

"Thank you," she said.

"You're welcome," he said.

He listened to her go.

---

The room was quiet after she left. The exercises were in front of him, the pencil on the stack. He sat for a moment without touching either.

He had not told her about Horak. He had not told her the inference that followed from a Franconian dialect and a Czech surname and the known patterns of the displacement networks operating in the North Atlantic corridor in 1942 and 1943. He had not told her about the probable route south from Halifax — the chain of small contacts, the handoffs, the way a person without papers moved through a country that was not permitting him to move. He had not told her that the most probable contact node in this part of Maine for someone coming through those networks was a German-American farm on Route 9, with Bavarian origins in the same Franconian geography where those networks had concentrated in the late 1930s, where a man might have known, twenty years before it mattered, who the reliable people were and how to find them. He had not told her what any of this meant about what her father might have been to Jan Václav Horak, or what Horak had been trying to reach in the hours before the breakwater.

She had not come to ask. She had come to ask whether she had done something wrong at the fence, and he had answered that question as precisely as he knew how.

He thought about the sentence: *a man who has made some decisions about what he knows and what he does not need to say.* He had said it about Albrecht Pfeiffer and he had meant it about Albrecht Pfeiffer. He had also meant it about himself — not as confession, just as fact. He had been making those decisions since Paris, since the particular autumn that had required them, and they had continued to be required. The letter in the right-hand drawer of his desk at the Crows' house was the most recent instance of this. There would be others, there were always others; you arrived somewhere, you assembled what you could, and you made decisions about what you were going to do with the assembling. *Like most people.*

He picked up his pencil. On the first exercise: *dum haec geruntur, Cicero ad senatum scrīpsit.* While these things were being done, Cicero wrote to the senate. The ablative absolute — the construction for two actions occurring simultaneously, neither one subordinate to the other. He had been teaching it for three years. It remained a good construction for what it described: the persistent simultaneity of things, the way the main clause and the absolute clause ran alongside each other without either one subsuming the other.

He marked the page. He worked through the rest of the stack until the light was gone, and then three more by the lamp, and when he was finished he put on his coat and closed the classroom and went down the stairs and out into the October dark, which was the dark of this specific street, this specific town, these three blocks between the school and the Crows' house where the kitchen window would be lit and Lillian would have left something on the stove. His to walk through. His to go home to, which was not the same as home and was also not nothing, and he had made a decision about that too, some time ago, though he had not yet named what the decision had been.
