# Private Reed

The truck needed a minute. Lonzo sat in the cab while the light came up — barely gray at six-fifty, the spruce line solidifying first, then the barn roof, then the near fence posts finding their shapes against the pale sky. The icehouse light showed through the barn gap: already on. He wouldn't need to knock anyone up.

He got the Springfield from behind the seat. Bolt, magazine, safety — the check was the same as every morning. He had not fired it since an April exercise on the range, unrelated to this posting, and was not going to fire it today. But you checked it and so you checked it.

The ground was iron-hard under his boots. The cold had a dry character today, the kind that carried sound; he could hear the Holsteins shifting in the barn before he reached the barn door. He hung his lantern on the door nail, set his stool beside the entrance, positioned his thermos on the wooden crate. Six fifty-four.

At seven the icehouse door came open.

Fischer first, the way Fischer was always first — the heavyset one, deliberate in his walk, the man who set the pace for the others whether he intended to or not. Brunner behind him, leaning left the way he always leaned, a habit of the shoulder that twelve weeks of watching hadn't explained. Then Krebs — the oldest of the four, bald on the crown, missing the upper third of his right ear from something that had happened before Tunisia. Then the young one, Reinmar.

Four. Lonzo said "guten Morgen" the way he said it every morning, which was the only German he'd managed to retain, and Fischer nodded, and the others went to the field.

He poured half a cup from his thermos and settled in.

He knew this field now. Twelve weeks of watching gave you that — the patterns of the work, the patterns of the men in it. Fischer organized whatever needed organizing; the other three read from him without being told. Brunner and Krebs worked the heavy things together, reliably, which left Reinmar to the far end and whatever tasks didn't require a second man. Lonzo had wondered at first whether the young one was being pushed to the margins or whether he chose them. He'd watched long enough to decide it was neither: Reinmar simply found the work that was waiting at the far end and went at it, and at the far end there was always work waiting. Character or nineteen years old. Couldn't always tell those apart.

The December field was maintenance — hauling the last equipment to the barn, attending to what the season had broken. Pale light coming in flat from the south; by ten it would be as good as it got. Albrecht Pfeiffer came out at nine and gestured toward the stone-wall line, and the four of them went. Pfeiffer did not speak to them and they did not speak to him.

—

At ten, Pfeiffer went to the south fence.

Lonzo had seen this before. The posts along that line had heaved over the wet autumn, and Hannah and her father had been working them in November before the ground went hard. One post in particular was still wrong somehow; Pfeiffer stopped there on his way through most mornings. He walked the line now with his hand on the top wire, testing tension, bending to check each post in turn.

Reinmar's work had moved him to the same fence by then — the south edge of the near field, loading out the last of the broken corn stalks into the cart. He was within talking distance of Pfeiffer when he said something. Low — Lonzo couldn't hear it at forty yards — but he could see the shape of it, the mouth moving, something said under the breath while the young man was working a heavy load and not quite landing it the way he wanted.

Pfeiffer stopped.

Not dramatically. Not the stop of a man startled. The stop of a man who has heard something that requires a moment to sit.

Two seconds. Three. Pfeiffer's hand was still on the wire.

Then he moved his hand and kept walking the fence line. Reinmar got the cart loaded and wheeled it toward the barn.

Lonzo made a note. In his head, not on the clipboard.

The young prisoner had some way of running his words — vowels that sat in a particular place, a shape to the sounds that Pfeiffer had recognized from a long way back and a long way away. Lonzo had grown up on lobster boats on the bay and could tell a Jonesport hull from a Friendship sloop by the width of the stern; he figured a man from Bavaria could hear where another man was from by how his vowels moved.

Not an incident. He would not write it.

He finished his cup and refilled it.

—

He ate at noon. Bread, cheese, mustard, wax paper going soft against the crate. He ate without tasting it and read the Bangor Daily News he'd folded into his coat pocket that morning before five-thirty.

Front page for the general situation, then he turned to the Pacific.

He always read the Pacific first, then went back to the front. This habit had organized itself around Jimmy in February when the orders came through. Jimmy was on a Buckley-class destroyer escort — that was what Lonzo knew, plus the theater, and from there he was reduced to inference. The censors had taken the specifics out of every letter, which meant he tried to locate his son by reasoning about what the newspapers reported and where a Buckley-class was likely not to be, and the zone that remained was several thousand miles of open water with Jimmy somewhere in it.

The Pacific news today covered the operations in the Gilberts, the mopping-up after Tarawa. He read every line. He had no way to know if the Gilberts was where Jimmy was. He read it anyway.

Jimmy wrote every six weeks or so when things were going well. He had written seven weeks ago. The letter was in Lonzo's jacket pocket, where it had been sitting for two weeks now. He had been meaning to answer it. The letter said the ship was a good one and the men on it were good, said the food was adequate, said don't worry, Dad in the second paragraph. Lonzo had put that last phrase in the category of things he was not going to look at too carefully.

—

The afternoon light was going by three o'clock. The field was nearly done. Fischer directed the last of the winterizing from the barn entrance and the other three moved around him in the established pattern, and Lonzo watched it all from his stool the way he'd been watching it for twelve weeks.

Hannah Pfeiffer came out of the barn at close to three-twenty. She was carrying something small — hardware or a tool, he couldn't tell from his position. She went to the near fence where Reinmar was working. He turned toward her. She passed something to him, or he handed something back — the low afternoon light made it hard to read direction. They said something. A few exchanges. Maybe two or three minutes.

Lonzo watched without moving.

The particular quality of attention that didn't come from anything you could put in a report.

He knew what dangerous looked like. This wasn't it.

He'd watch.

At three-forty the prisoners came in from the field.

—

He walked them to the icehouse. Fischer, Brunner, Krebs, Reinmar — counted them through the door and locked it behind them. Checked the lock.

He filled out the Coastal Patrol report form at the tailgate of the truck. The form had the usual blanks: date, prisoner count, hours worked, incidents, violations. His handwriting was large and slightly unsteady in the cold; he had lost sensation in the first two joints of his left hand after a winch accident in 1931, and it showed in December. He wrote carefully anyway.

December 9, 1943. Prisoner count: four. Hours worked: seven.

He got to incidents and wrote: None.

He got to violations and wrote: None.

He read it back.

It was accurate.

He signed it.

—

The road went through three miles of spruce before the harbor lights showed. Full dark by ten past four. He drove with the heater running and his left arm against the door.

He went over the day the way he always did on the drive home — ran through it, filed it, let it go. Pfeiffer at the south fence. Hannah at the near fence. Reinmar at the far end of the field all morning, working ahead of the work.

Then he stopped thinking about the farm.

He was thinking about Reinmar being nineteen years old and what the war from his direction had probably looked like before they took him in Tunisia. He was thinking about Jimmy.

He had learned this line of thinking didn't go anywhere useful. He let it go.

The road came over the last rise and the harbor was below and to his left — the wharf lights, the shapes of the lobster smacks at the inside dock, the long dark of the breakwater reaching out into the black water. He downshifted and followed the road down.

Jimmy's last letter was in his jacket pocket. He was going to write back tonight. He would tell Jimmy the weather had been cold and clear. He would ask about the ship. He would write that he was well.

The truck leveled out at the harbor road and Lonzo drove the last quarter-mile home, the harbor sitting quiet off his left window.
