# Mr. Klein's Room

He left at a quarter to seven. The walk was three blocks — Elm Street north to the school — and he had been making it for eighteen years.

The May morning was the best morning of the year in Maine. Cold still in the shadow of the houses, the sun coming over the ridge to the east, the light flat and clean in a way it had not been since October. He knew every house on his route — the Peabody porch board that had needed painting since 1939, the forsythia at the corner that came back the same width every spring regardless of how far it was cut in April, the fence at the white house that had lost two boards in the January ice and still needed replacing. He knew which yards the low-tide smell reached on a southeast wind and which ones the lilacs got to first.

Going the other way, as he turned onto the main road, the fishermen were heading down to the harbor — two of them that he could see, in their oilskins, carrying their gear. He nodded. They nodded. They had been passing each other at this corner, going opposite directions, for as long as he had been in Coldwater.

At number 14, the curtain moved.

The woman at that window was always there when he passed. He had considered this, years ago — whether she was always at her window or whether something about the sound of his step on the road brought her to it — and then let the question go. He nodded toward the curtain. The shape behind it nodded back. They had been exchanging this nod since the spring of 1936, when he had first noticed her there and decided to acknowledge rather than ignore.

He walked on.

The town had known him for twenty years. His father had brought the family north from Norfolk in 1924 — a sound decision, given what his father knew about Norfolk and what he had been told about Maine. He had found work at the school within the first two years, when the previous custodian left for Bangor, and he had been there since. He knew the town's face now: not the face it showed to strangers but the one it wore in the moment before deciding whether to grant you the ordinary courtesies without having to think about them first. Coldwater Reach was not unkind, as a rule. Not unkind in the way a person is not unkind when they have been polite long enough that the politeness runs on its own, without decision. You did not mistake that for something else. You did not have to, after twenty years.

He turned the last corner before the school.

---

He opened the building at seven. The key was on the ring with eleven others; he never confused it because the shaft was bent at the top from the time he'd used it to pry open a stuck gutter coupling in 1937 and had never gotten it straightened. He let himself in through the east door and stood for a moment in the hallway. The air inside was cooler than outside — furnaces banked overnight — and the smell was chalk dust, the sourness of the mop water that never quite left the floor, and the first warmth working in through the south-facing windows.

He did the east furnace first.

He had been managing the east wing's flue since November. A baffle plate sitting wrong in the air-change fitting had restricted the draw on cold mornings; he had shimmed it in December and adjusted the damper morning by morning since then, reading the stack draft by the sound of it and the gauge reading together. In May he could bank it down to almost nothing — the radiators in the east wing were warm but not needed — and this itself was a relief, the easing of a piece of equipment he had been watching all winter. He bled the pressure valve and recorded the gauge in the log: thirty-eight pounds, which was right. He checked the firebox, cleared the grate, made the entry in the maintenance book. Same for the west furnace, which had been easier all winter; the boiler there was newer, installed in 1938, and the system ran clean.

He did the hallways. He had mopped them the afternoon before and they were dry; he walked each corridor checking for what had come in overnight, because the east door seal let in leaves in any wind, and found nothing. He checked the lavatory fixtures on both sides of the ground floor. He oiled the gymnasium door hinge that had been dry since January and that he had been getting to for two months. He was working his way down the second-floor corridor when he came to the classroom at the end.

He had left the door standing open six inches the previous afternoon, because the window latch on the south side had been sticking since March and he had meant to come back to it. He pushed the door open now and went to the window.

The latch had freed itself. The warmth of the last few weeks had broken the paint seal and the swivel pin was seated properly; when he closed the window and opened it again it moved without resistance. He checked it twice. He opened the window four inches, because the morning warranted it, and looked out at the yard below.

Then he turned and looked at the room.

It was Marcus's room — he never thought of it as the Latin room or the German room, though it was both. Twenty student desks in four rows, eleven of them with names carved into the wood because this school had been here long enough for that. The blackboard with tomorrow's date in the upper right corner, where Marcus put it at the end of every class so the students could see it when they came in. The shelf behind the desk: the Latin dictionary, the mark book in its green cover, the spare chalk box.

The German grammar was not on the shelf.

All winter it had been there — spine out, between the dictionary and the mark book, and he had dusted around it every week since October. It was on the desk now. Cover up, in the working space, next to a composition book with student names written across the front in various hands, a different student's name in the corner of each page where a page corner was turned. And a pencil, short now — four inches at most — the tip broken off and not yet resharpened.

He stood at the door for a moment.

Then he went out and pulled the door back to six inches and continued his rounds.

---

He had been watching Marcus change since the school board meeting in February.

Precision was the instrument he used for pipe fittings and furnace draws and joist tolerances, not for people. People he observed and understood by the slow accumulation of what he saw, and named what he saw when a name was useful, which was not always.

What he had been seeing: since February, Marcus had stopped holding himself quite so far from the door.

He'd had a quality, when he arrived in '42 and for the first year and more after that, of a man whose coat was not fully off. Not literally — Marcus was particular about his coat, hung it on the hook when he came into the house, kept it brushed. Something else. The quality of a man still working out whether this was the place where the coat came off and nothing happened. Of a man for whom *I am here* was still a provisional sentence, held out like a question rather than set down like a fact. He had recognized this quality when he first saw it because he knew it from the inside — from his own first years in Coldwater, before he had gotten to the provisional sentence himself.

After February, it was different. Not that the man was careless — Marcus was never careless, and the particular quality of attention he gave to everything had not changed. The grammar was on the desk. The window was open in the mornings when the weather allowed. The chalk went onto the blackboard in longer strokes, the kind a person made when they were comfortable with the room they were working in.

He had sat in the Legion hall in February, along the left wall in the extra chairs the Legion kept for when the membership room was being used for other business, while three men he had known for sixteen years decided something about a man he lived with. He had sat in rooms along the wall before, for different reasons, with different people at the table, and he knew how those rooms felt from that position — what it cost to be in the chair in front, what it cost to be at the wall, and that these costs were not the same cost. He had watched Marcus sit in the chair before the table and use the voice a man used when he had used it before, in other rooms, and had learned exactly how to use it. That was a specific kind of knowing: how you held your shoulders, how you waited for the next question, what you did not answer when it had not been asked. He did not know whether Marcus had it from the same place or a different one; that was not his to assume.

He had said *I have too*, walking out. Not because Marcus needed to hear it — or maybe he did; he couldn't say — but because it was true, and he had been careful, for some years, about leaving true things entirely unsaid.

What he had not said, and did not say now, and was not inclined to say: the two things were not the same. His twenty years here and Marcus's two were not the same condition with different names attached. They rhymed — he had seen the rhyme since the first months, seen it clearly and without needing anyone to point it out to him — but a rhyme was not a claim of equivalence, and the specific nature of what each of them had put down in this town, what it had cost each of them, what each of them had left in order to be putting anything down here at all, was not the same. He had been careful about this. He intended to remain so.

He was also glad Marcus was here. He was glad the grammar was on the desk.

He said none of this, because no one had asked him. His observations had a way of becoming invisible to the people who benefited from them, and over time he had learned to hold them. It was a skill — the skill of a man who had been watching long enough that the watching was its own kind of work, precise and complete and done for its own sake, not for the record.

---

The last item on the morning rounds was the south radiator in the main corridor.

It had been slow all winter — a fitting on the feed line that he had suspected was admitting air into the circuit, starving the pressure and keeping the radiator from reaching full heat on cold mornings. He had worked around it all season, balancing the other radiators on the circuit, and it had never been serious enough to pull the fitting during the heating season. Now it was May. He got his wrench from the furnace room, bled the fitting, and ran it until it was clear: eight seconds of air before the water came. He tightened the fitting and tagged it with a strip of red tape — his notation for the summer work order. He made the entry in the maintenance book: *South corridor, main feed, air in fitting. Bled and tagged, summer priority*.

He put the wrench away. He washed his hands at the utility sink. He could hear, out in the corridor, the east door opening — the first teacher arriving, footsteps on the floor he had mopped the afternoon before, the ordinary sound of the building coming on.

He went and got his coffee from the thermos on the shelf in the furnace room, the same shelf where Lillian packed it every morning and where it had been every morning for eighteen years.

His rounds were done.
