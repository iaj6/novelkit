# Official Report

She was finishing the seven o'clock entry when she heard a vehicle on the harbor road.

The tide gauge reading, the fleet status: three smacks out since before light — the *Ruth Ann*, the *Mabel*, the *Cornelia Best* — and the *Dorothy Mead* still on her mooring with the engine trouble Alderman had promised to see to by Wednesday. Today was Thursday. She blotted the entry and set the pen in the groove of Owen's chart table.

The Coast Guard vehicle parked at the foot of the wharf at three minutes past nine. Dark green, the Sector designation stenciled on its door. She recognized it from October.

Parsons came up the dock with his clipboard under his arm and his cap on against the cold. He came through the door — the latch caught on the first try — and looked at the office with the brief professional scan of a man checking a space against his memory of it: chart table, tide log, field telephone, kerosene stove. He looked at the window and the harbor, and back at her. She gestured to the chair across the table. He sat and uncapped his pen.

"Harbormaster."

"Lieutenant."

He was in his late thirties — the right age for the Coast Guard in 1943: too young for the last war, a few years past the eligible window of this one. She had seen the type before. He had the manner of a man who had given this interview many times — professional, careful, not warm and not unkind.

"We'll go through the morning of the twenty-eighth," he said.

"Ready when you are," she said.

---

He started with the timeline. She gave it to him the way she kept the log: in order, each item complete before moving to the next. Time of notification: seven-fourteen, Hector Brann at the harbor road telephone. Response time: she had been in the office when the call came in and reached the breakwater by seven thirty-five. He asked if she could have moved faster. She said she had stopped for the tide gauge reading she would need for the official account; the data was there when he asked for it. He wrote that down.

The body: male, face up, arms at his sides, in the granite seam on the ocean side of the breakwater approximately forty feet from the harbor-side opening of the seam. Eastern face. No sign of prior injury. She had confirmed the absence of a pulse and sent Hector Brann for the constable's telephone and stayed until the Coast Guard arrived.

"You have the tide data," he said.

She brought out the charts — the gauge readings she had taken during the blow, the fleet status records, the weather notations for the preceding forty-eight hours, each logged in the standard format. She had organized them before he arrived: chronological order, the relevant entries tabbed at their edges. He did not remark on this. He went through them with the focus of a man reading instruments he understood.

He asked about the seam — whether it would hold something deposited by water, or whether the tide would carry material back out. She explained: the blocks had settled unevenly since the 1890s, leaving a joint where the ebb current dropped what the flood had brought. The angle of the eastern face. The specific dynamic at that location. He listened without interrupting and wrote.

"Your estimate for when the body arrived," he said.

"Twelve to two a.m. on the twenty-eighth," she said. "Given the seam and the position. That's the window I'd put it in."

"Based on."

"The tidal data, the deposit pattern, the orientation of the body. All consistent." A pause. "I've thought about it."

He wrote.

He asked about the clothing: what she had observed on scene, what was in the log. The heavy civilian coat, unfamiliar in its cut, double-breasted, buttons she had not recognized. He asked if she had searched the pockets. She had not — once she called it in, the body was under Coast Guard authority and she had not touched it. Correct procedure, he said.

She answered each question in the scope it was asked. He had a good method.

---

"The preceding night," he said. He turned to a new page on his clipboard. "The blow was at its worst the preceding forty-eight hours?"

"Mostly. It had eased some by Tuesday evening."

"Did anything unusual occur in the harbor area during the blow? Any unauthorized vessels in the inner anchorage? Anyone on the dock who shouldn't have been there?"

The stove ticked once. Outside the north window the harbor lay in its November gray: flat water, flat light, the smell of brine that came through the window frame even when the glass was shut. The smacks were out or in. The season was what the season was. Everything visible from this window was where it was supposed to be.

"The blow kept most activity down," she said. "The fleet was in." One pause, one breath. "I did a late mooring check at approximately twenty-three hundred hours."

He looked up from the clipboard.

"I didn't enter anything unusual."

He held the pen for a moment — not suspicion; only the habitual check of a careful man making sure the answer was complete before he committed it to paper. Then he wrote.

"Any vessels you can't account for? Unregistered moorings, traffic you couldn't identify?"

"Nothing in the log," she said.

He looked at the tide log open on the table beside the charts. The October entries were visible in her handwriting — the standard format, the black ink, entry after entry. He did not ask to see Tuesday night's page specifically. If he had asked she would have shown him: the entry was there, it had been there since she wrote it. *2300: mooring check completed, fleet secure.* He did not ask.

He wrote, turned the page on his clipboard, and moved on.

---

He asked to walk where the body had been found.

November on the breakwater was the cold that came off open water — not the dry cold of the interior but the cold with moisture in it, the kind that worked through wool over time. The algae on the lower courses were slick. She wore her heavy coat and her work hat and went first; she knew every place on that granite where a foot would hold and where it would not. She had walked this breakwater since 1935 in every weather and every hour. She knew its two hundred feet the way she knew the distance from the chart table to the tide log shelf.

She brought him to the seam. The joint between two blocks on the eastern face, forty feet from the harbor-side opening.

Parsons looked at it with the same methodical eye he had given the charts. The approach angles from the open water side. The tidal marks on the lower granite, the algae growing in horizontal bands that marked the tidal range. The distance from the harbor mouth. He looked long enough that she understood he was done looking and was now confirming what he had already concluded.

"Two previous bodies on this breakwater," he said. The note she had given him in the office.

"Neither in this seam," she said.

He made a note. They turned back.

---

On the return along the granite, the harbor road ahead and the diesel smell from the smacks in the inner anchorage reaching them on the wind, he told her what the investigation had found.

Cause of death: hypothermia and drowning consistent with storm exposure. No injury prior to exposure. The body's condition was consistent with twelve to thirty-six hours in the water before discovery — a broad window, unavoidable given the storm and the water temperature. No military markings of any kind. No insignia, no service items.

The clothing: civilian, European in manufacture. The cut of the coat, the style of the buttons, the weight and weave of the lining fabric — all consistent with continental production. Not British. Not American. Wartime manufacture, based on the fabric quality and the composition of the buttons. The working estimate was 1941 to 1942.

No papers. No wallet, no letters, no documents of any kind. The pockets had been empty.

She asked: "Any theory on how he got there?"

"Storm exposure is the most probable account," Parsons said. "A man overboard from a vessel that didn't report it — that happens in a blow on a small boat. Or he was already in the water when the storm reached this coast." The wind was at their backs, off the Atlantic. "There's been talk in town. The U-boat line. We've looked at it. We're not pursuing it. The acute period on this coast was last year — the tactical situation has changed considerably. A man in civilian clothes, European manufacture, no papers — there are other explanations for how a man ends up in the water."

She said she understood.

They went the rest of the way in without talking.

---

At the office door he gave her the formal close. The case would be classified as accidental drowning, unidentified. Her statement would be on file at the District office in Portland. He'd keep her number in case an identification came in — a missing-persons report from another jurisdiction, something that matched the description. He said this without particular expectation.

She said she would be here.

He shook her hand — dry, brief — and went to his vehicle. She stood in the doorway and watched him back off the dock and turn up the harbor road, the vehicle rounding the curve toward town and out of sight. When the sound of his engine was gone there was only the harbor — the line of the water, the cold, the smell of it.

She pulled the door closed. The latch required a slight lift on the handle as she turned it; she gave it the lift and the latch seated. The trick Owen had shown her. The carpenter had offered to fix it twice and she had told him twice it didn't need fixing.

---

The stove had been going since seven and the room was warm. The cold she had brought in from the breakwater eased.

She stood at Owen's chart table without sitting. The November light came through both windows and made nothing particular of anything — everything the same flat distance. Through the north window the breakwater ran out to the harbor mouth, two hundred feet, empty and salt-whitened, the water behind it going gray to the horizon. She had looked at that breakwater from this window every morning for eight years. It looked the way it looked.

She sat.

The tide log was open to this morning — the seven o'clock entry she had written before Parsons arrived, two lines: gauge reading, fleet status, Alderman's engine not yet resolved. Her handwriting, black ink, in the format she had used for eight years. Owen's format, because it was correct.

She turned the pages back through October.

Her handwriting through all of it: tides, fleet conditions, mooring inspections, fuel inquiries, the cannery pier. The record of the harbor continuing — it had to continue; it did continue — in the format that made the record useful and the harbor manageable. She had kept it this way since the first morning she had taken the post and understood what she had been given.

She found the week of the twenty-eighth.

The Thursday entry — the morning Hector Brann had called — was written more carefully than the entries around it. Not dramatically. She would not have noticed it without looking. But she was looking, and she could see it: the letter formation tighter, the spacing a little wider than her habit.

She turned back one more day.

Tuesday.

Date, tides, fleet conditions. The weather notation for the blow, logged at each of her checks. Alderman's engine noted. The mooring check times for the early morning and the noon watch and the evening. At the bottom of the right column, added when she returned from the dock at half past eleven:

*2300: mooring check completed, fleet secure.*

She read it.

She read it a second time.

She knew everything that was in that line and she knew everything that was not in it. Both were facts, as durable as the paper and the ink and the cabinet on the far wall where the decade's records sat in chronological order. The record said what it said. Everything in it was accurate.

She closed the log.

The cabinet held Owen's volumes in the lower shelves, their spines worn pale with handling. The 1934 volume was on the third shelf from the bottom. She had taken it down once, in the first November she had the post, to understand what keeping the record meant and what it looked like to keep it honestly. She had put it back. She did not take it down now.

She set her hand flat on the closed log for a moment.

Then she opened it to this morning, looked at the office clock, and wrote the nine o'clock entry: tides, fleet status, the harbor doing what the harbor did.

Nothing unusual to note.
