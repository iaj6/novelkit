# The Names on the Board

Henry was at the table when Marcus came downstairs, the coffee already made and the bread on its board. He set his cup down when Marcus came in.

"Two men from Coldwater," he said. "In the German counteroffensive. I heard it this morning — a parent came to the building before seven." He paused. "The other name is Calvin Wilkes."

Marcus stood at the stove and poured his coffee. He did not say anything yet.

"He was in your Latin class," Henry said. "First year you were here."

"Yes," Marcus said. "I remember him."

He sat down. The kitchen was the same as it was every morning: the lamp over the table, the stove working, the window on the south side fogged where the warmth inside met the cold outside. Henry's cup made a small sound against the wood when he set it down. Neither of them spoke.

The boy's face came without effort. Calvin Wilkes — not a strong student in the grammatical sense, which was to say that his ablative constructions had been uncertain throughout the fall of 1942 and had never quite settled in the spring. But he had had a real facility for the rhythm of the sentences, which was not something Marcus could teach and not something he had expected from a student whose case endings required the kind of repeated correction that tended, in his experience, to indicate that the language was simply not going to come. Most students learned the structure and kept it at a distance, handling it as a formal object rather than working through it from the inside. Calvin had been different. He could recite the Virgil passages with a quality of feeling that Marcus had noticed and not remarked upon — remarking tended to kill it. He had kept marking the ablative errors down all year and had kept hoping the recitation would eventually close the gap.

It had not.

"The other man," Henry said. "Ned Harker's boy. He was on Galen Fuller's dragger before he enlisted. His mother's on Mast Street."

"I do not think I knew him," Marcus said.

Henry stood and took his cup to the stove.

---

The servicemen's board was in the hallway to the right of the main entrance — a framed corkboard, the white cards in rows going back to the spring of 1942. Marcus had walked past it every school day for two years and ten months. He could account for the position of each name.

Calvin Wilkes's card was in the third row. It had gone up in June of 1943, when he enlisted at seventeen. It had been a white card like the others since June of 1943.

It was not white like the others now. There was a black border around it — a thin frame, not theatrical, the standard thing that Bertrand Hatch placed on the card when the telegram arrived. Marcus stopped in the hallway and looked at it.

Fall of 1942, spring of 1943. One complete school year: the ablative absolute in November, the passive periphrastic in February, the Virgil in April. Calvin Wilkes in the second row, right side, the seat he had claimed by the second week of September and held for the year. The way he had set his pen down when Marcus called for the recitation, which was not something most of them did.

Marcus went to his classroom.

---

Third period was his third-year section — eleven students working through Caesar's *De Bello Gallico*, the standard text for the year and the text that came next in order. He wrote the construction on the board: *quo cum consilio convenerant*, explained the relative clause of purpose, and worked through the passage: translate, identify the case, identify the construction, explain the choice.

The passage was not the wrong text only because it was about war. Caesar's *De Bello Gallico* was always about war; that was its nature and its curriculum reason for being there. It was the wrong text because it was about a Roman army moving through a country with the calm procedural vocabulary of completed action — a vocabulary that assumed the events were finished and could now be held at the distance from which patterns became legible. He had taught it for two years in this school and had never entirely resolved what it meant to do so: students with brothers in the Pacific, fathers at the six o'clock radio. He had decided, sometime in the spring of 1943, that the resolution was not his to make. What he could offer was the grammar, and the attention, and the occasional passage that a student might hold for reasons Marcus would not always know.

He did not mention the Ardennes. He did not mention Calvin Wilkes. He taught the lesson, assigned the written exercise for the following morning, and dismissed the class at the bell.

After the last period — the first-year fundamentals section, which was where Calvin Wilkes had been in the fall of 1942, the same room, the same window light in the same mid-afternoon position — he stayed. He had papers to grade and he graded them. The school emptied around him, the hallway voices going, the main door at the far end of the corridor closing and closing again, and then the building going quiet in the way it went quiet at the end of a school day in winter, with the furnace working inside the silence and nothing else.

He graded the papers. He did not hurry.

Calvin Wilkes had sat in the third seat from the window. He had written his exercises in a slightly cramped hand that had improved through the year. By April he had been writing the ablatives correctly most of the time.

By April he had also been able to recite the opening lines of Book Two of the *Aeneid* with a quality of feeling Marcus had not anticipated — in part because Calvin Wilkes was not, technically, supposed to have encountered the passage at all; Marcus had used it as an example of a syntactic construction in February, and Calvin had held it. *Conticuere omnes, intentique ora tenebant.* All fell silent and held their faces intent. The sentence that opens Aeneas's own account of the fall of Troy. Calvin had not known what it was the opening of; they had not gotten there. But something in his reading of it had been right, in the way that some students arrived at a language before they could explain how, and Marcus had marked the ablative down and said nothing about the rest.

He finished the papers. He put on his coat and hat and went out.

---

The walk home was three blocks. He had made it in every weather since March of 1942 and knew it the way he had come to know a small number of things in this town — the exact position of the January ice on the school's side-yard steps, the way the harbor road received the wind off the inlet, the particular sound the Elm Street corner made at night when the cold had been extended for some days. He had not intended to know it so well. He had not intended most of what came from having been in one place for nearly three years, which was longer than he had been anywhere since Vienna.

January in Coldwater Reach was a particular kind of dark — the sun not fully up until after eight, gone before five, the sky between those hours a flat pewter that gave no warmth but gave light enough to work by, which was all the light a working town required.

He had his brother in Vienna, from whom he had had no word since the autumn of 1940. That was more than four years of silence, which had become its own kind of knowledge: not the absence of information but a form of information, indicating that the channels through which word might have come had closed, or that his brother had reasons of his own for not reaching them, or both. He had worked through it from many angles in the early years and had eventually stopped working through it from angles and simply carried it.

He had his colleague from Paris — Kessler, a classicist who had been at the Sorbonne since 1933 and whom Marcus had known for three years of occasional conversation before leaving Paris in 1938. In September of 1942 a second-hand source with the Emergency Committee in Cambridge had sent word that Kessler had been arrested in August. He had received nothing since.

He had the men from Sherbrooke: a handful he had remained in contact with for the first year after his own release, and had then stopped, not because they had ceased to matter but because the contact had become indistinguishable from the carrying, and he had enough to carry.

He had Horak — the dead man who had come to rest on a Coldwater Reach breakwater in October of 1943, whose name and probable history Marcus had assembled and placed in the drawer where he kept the things he held without further examination.

And now he had Calvin Wilkes, who had been alive eight months ago in this town and who was dead now in eastern Belgium in what the radio called a counteroffensive. He had known this boy. He had graded his papers. He had taught him for a year in the classroom at the end of this three-block walk, and Calvin Wilkes was dead in Belgium, and Marcus was walking home in the January dark past the same houses he passed every evening.

This was not the same as the other losses.

He was not certain the distinction held.

He turned the corner onto Elm Street. The Crow house with its kitchen light on — the lamp over the table visible through the window, the yellow square of it in the dark. He had been looking for it when he turned the corner for longer than he had recognized. He had become aware of the looking at some point in the fall and had not done anything about the awareness.

---

Henry was there when Marcus came in, and Lillian was at the stove. She had made soup — the bean soup she made through the winter months, with bread and what remained of the week's butter. They sat down and ate. Henry had the fuller account from Beal's: the two names confirmed, the approximate circumstances, the Battle of the Bulge establishing itself now as the phrase replacing German counteroffensive in the way that phrases settled as an event accumulated its name. Lillian had heard some of it from the church women's group. They covered it between them and then moved on, the way the kitchen moved on: the woodpile question, the Congregational women's event Saturday, whether the coffee at Beal's had improved or whether the November blend was now simply what coffee was going to be.

The kitchen was warm. The stove had been going since afternoon.

After supper Lillian went to her sister's on Birch Street, as she did most Wednesdays. Henry cleared the table and began the dishes. Marcus sat.

"Did you know the Harker boy?" Henry said. He was at the sink, back to the table, running the water.

"Not from the school," Marcus said. "He would have finished before I arrived, or nearly."

Henry washed a bowl and set it in the rack. "He was good with boats. That's what Fuller always said." He paused. "Ned's going to take it hard."

Marcus said: "You knew Calvin Wilkes."

"Since he started at the school. He was a talker in the halls — always had something to say about something. I used to let him keep going while I was doing the floors in the morning." Henry set another bowl in the rack. "That's what you remember. Not the quiet ones."

"He could recite Virgil," Marcus said.

Henry turned slightly from the sink. "Is that something they do in the first year?"

"I used passages as examples — syntactic constructions. He held one. More than most students do." Marcus paused. "He could not explain the grammar behind it. He could do the thing itself."

Henry turned back to the sink.

After a moment Marcus said: "I find I think about my students long after."

"I suppose that's what teaching is," Henry said.

He finished the dishes and dried his hands and put the plates away, the bowls, the cups, each thing where it went. Then he sat down across from Marcus with what remained in his cup.

---

Outside, the cold was at the clapboard — the small sounds a house makes in January when the temperature drops after dark, the contraction of the sills, the occasional shudder in the window glass when the wind came at it. The lamp over the table. Henry with his cup and his own particular quiet; he had his losses too, twenty years' worth of them in this town, and Marcus knew some of them and not others, and they did not, as a practice, place their losses alongside each other's.

Marcus had his hands on the table and nothing in them.

He was thinking about the board in the hallway — not Calvin Wilkes's card specifically but the whole of it: the rows of white cards going back to 1942, the names he had walked past every school day for two years and ten months. He knew those names. He knew which ones had come from the fishing families and which from the inland farms, which had brothers still here, which had been in his classroom and which had not. He had been here long enough to know them.

Nearly three years. The arithmetic of his displacements had recently stopped adding up in the way it once had: Vienna, Paris, London, Sherbrooke, Cambridge, and now here for nearly three years — longer than Paris and London and Cambridge combined. He had arrived in March of 1942 with his document file and his tea and his understanding that this was provisional, and the provisional had extended long enough that he was no longer certain what it was provisionally in the way of.

He could not say exactly when the arithmetic had changed.

He had this kitchen. He had this table, this man across it with his cup, the lamp over them both making the window a dark mirror. He had a classroom at the end of a three-block walk and a board in the hallway with names he had come to know. One of those names had a black border around it now, and he had graded that boy's papers for a year, and the boy was dead in Belgium, and Marcus was sitting at this table in January of 1945 and he had not moved.

He could not yet say what this was.

He was sitting in it.
