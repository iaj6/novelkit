# The Breakwater

He had written to the Emergency Committee address in Cambridge on the twenty-second of November — the contact he used to have at the Divinity School, a man named Whitmore who was managing the displaced scholars file — and had not heard back, which was not, he had been telling himself, unreasonable. The overseas mail slowed in December by some mechanism he had not fully understood in three years of receiving it; the Committee's work was extensive and Whitmore's desk, by all evidence, was a place where things accumulated before being addressed rather than a surface cleared at intervals; and twenty-one days, counted from the date of actual posting rather than the date of composition, was not long for wartime correspondence, even domestic wartime correspondence, even between two towns in the same country that shared a coast.

He had turned left at the Legion hall corner, which was not the shortest route from the school to Main Street. The most direct route to the post office was right on Birch, straight down, three blocks — the same three blocks he walked every school day without deviation, the same root-ridge at the elm corner that he had stopped catching with his shoe by April of 1942. He had turned left instead and gone down toward the harbor, and he had been on the harbor road for perhaps forty steps before he had registered that he was on it.

He was not going to construct an explanation for the turn that would satisfy him, so he did not try.

The harbor road ran parallel to the commercial wharf for its last hundred yards before it met the foot of the dock. To his left: the water, black-grey in the late-afternoon, the short chop of a harbor that had decided on its winter shape. To his right: building-backs and fish-house sheds and the rear of Beal's with its sign facing the other direction. He had walked this road perhaps four times since March 1942 — twice in his first week for orientation, when he had made himself walk the perimeter of the town until he understood its grid, and twice since without particular cause. His regular route passed the harbor road's intersection without turning; he had noted the intersection every school day and continued straight, which had been, when he examined it, a deliberate choice that had looked like an unremarkable one. He had no strong feeling about harbors, which was the polite way to say that he had crossed an ocean in uncomfortable circumstances and had found the experience sufficient.

He passed the commercial wharf.

The December fleet was on the inside moorings. In winter cover: canvas pulled taut over the fittings and winches, the boats' shapes reduced to their simplest outlines, a grammar of ovals and flat horizontals where the summer forms had been more particular. There was diesel-smell and rope and the wet cold off the water. The breakwater ran from the north shore two hundred feet toward the harbor mouth, granite blocks grey-black in the month's light, the algae-striping going from the waterline up to approximately the height of a man's knee, and the bare salt-pale granite above that. He could see, from where he was walking, the joint on the ocean side — the seam where two blocks had settled into each other at a slight angle, leaving a dark line against the stone, slightly lower than the others.

He stopped at the base of the breakwater.

He stood for two minutes, perhaps less, perhaps slightly more. There was nothing to see that was not simply there: stone and water and the particular shade the winter algae went when the temperature dropped below a certain point. The seam on the ocean side, the depth of the joint. The body had been found in the seam — face-up, which he had heard from the general circulation of the event through town in October. He had not been to the breakwater since. He was not looking for anything.

He had been thinking about it for two months, in the manner of thinking that consists primarily of declining to think directly about something.

The probable shape had assembled without his invitation. He had not tried to assemble it; he had, in fact, been careful for six weeks after the discovery to maintain a specific incuriosity about anything that would require him to know what he knew about how these things worked. This had functioned adequately until the conversation with Hannah Pfeiffer about the word *Landsmann*, at which point the incuriosity had become too expensive to maintain. The premises were what they were: a man in European civilian clothes — the wartime civilian of 1941 or '42, not military, not an oilskin, not anything that belonged on this coast — with palm-center calluses and no papers, deposited by a three-day blow onto the ocean side of the Coldwater breakwater. A Franconian prisoner from Camp Houlton on a farm owned by a family from Feuchtwangen, which was Franconian — the same vowels, the same particular geography, the kind of coincidence that was not, in his experience of how these chains of connection worked, coincidence. The structure they implied had assembled in the available space of his reasoning and was not going away.

He was not constructing this from reading about it. He knew the logic of wartime displacement from a specific interior angle — the angle of having been, at a specific earlier point in his life, the person being moved by it. He knew what it looked like when the official channels had stopped working for a particular kind of person: the contact in a city not named, the boat that didn't advertise its route, the room not registered under his name, the two days of waiting to understand which piece of the world he had arrived in. He knew the people who built these arrangements had not done it for their own advantage and had not expected to be discussed at dinner tables afterward, which was among the reasons he had not discussed them. What he was assembling in front of the breakwater was a probable shape only — not certainty, not anything he could state as fact — but it was assembled from knowledge that did not come from newspapers.

He turned and walked back along the pier-side toward the commercial wharf.

---

The harbormaster's office was the cedar-shingled building at the foot of the wharf steps — he had identified it in orientation and not entered since. The door was open by perhaps a foot, and a woman had come out and was doing something that involved the near mooring piling and a small gauge on a lanyard.

He had seen her at Beal's and at the Legion hall parking lot. He knew her by sight in the way of a small town: from a distance, from context. The harbormaster. He had formed no particular view of her beyond the professional fact of her, which was that she held a position in a town this size that was notable enough to mention, and the town had mentioned it to him in a register that was not, he had observed, entirely without complication.

Their paths converged at the foot of the wharf steps.

"Marcus Klein," he said. "I teach at the school." The introduction was not information she lacked; the town was not large enough for that. It was form — the acknowledgment that they had occupied the same place for over a year without occupying the same conversation, and that beginning a conversation required the form that beginning a conversation required.

"Eira Bowman." She held the gauge at her side. "Harbormaster."

"Yes," he said. "I know."

He mentioned that the wind had changed direction in the last half-hour, which it had.

"East-southeast," she said. "It started working around about three o'clock."

He said that the cold had a directness to it that he associated with a due-east wind rather than an angled one, and that he found the directness, in some personal sense, preferable to the angled variety. She agreed about the direction. Her voice was even, professional.

He was standing fifty feet from the seam in the granite.

"I heard about the remains found here last fall," he said. "I wondered whether any identification had been made."

She looked at him. It was a brief look, professional in quality.

"The Coast Guard handled it," she said. "Accidental drowning."

Her voice was level throughout the statement. Accurate, complete within its frame.

"Of course," he said. He was not pressing.

A line on one of the covered boats knocked against its cleat in the wind — a slow, metered count.

"Do you receive much mail from overseas?" she asked.

"Some," he said. "Rarely now." He paused a fraction. "I'm expecting something from Cambridge. I'm going to the post office."

"The overseas post is slow in December," she said.

"Yes," he said.

They nodded to each other, and she turned back to the dock, and he went on along the harbor road.

---

The letter was not there. Mrs. Aldrich at the counter looked through the K-L section with the efficiency of long practice and came back with nothing in her hands.

"Nothing for Klein today, Mr. Klein."

"Thank you," he said.

He stood at the counter a moment. Hector Brann was at the postal window — Marcus recognized the name from the October account of things, the lobsterman, the discovery on the breakwater — though they had not spoken and he would not have been able to match the name to the face if the town had not circulated both. Two other men at the stamp machine. The particular smell of a post office in winter: radiator heat, wet wool, the faint acidity of stamp glue. Outside the windows, the December dark was complete; the streetlamps had been on for some time.

He went out.

---

The walk from the post office to Elm Street: familiar to the point of being below the level of navigation. He had accumulated this route through repetition and without trying, until the soft board at the base of the Maple Street lamp post and the root-ridge at the elm corner of Birch registered as things to be managed rather than things to be noticed. He stepped over the root-ridge without looking down.

He was thinking about the word *handled*.

*The Coast Guard handled it.* Past tense — the verb of a thing taken up, managed, concluded, set down with finality, in the way that handled things are set down so that further attention is not needed. The statement was accurate. The Coast Guard had handled it; this was not in dispute; the ruling had been made and the case was closed; there was nothing in what she had said that was not true. It was also, he thought — stepping over the board on Maple Street — a statement of the specific kind that answers the question it was asked without extending to the question that exists one level below the question it was asked. The frame established in advance. The answer delivered cleanly within the frame.

He knew this form of statement from the inside out.

He had used it in rooms in Paris, in 1937 and 1938, when people asked what was happening in Vienna. *Complicated*, he had said. *But Vienna is far from Paris.* He had used it in London, when people at the Committee offices asked what the crossing had been like. *It was managed*, he had said. *By some generous people.* He had used it in Sherbrooke — or rather, he had learned its most precise form in Sherbrooke, in the specific conditions that made precision necessary — when the officials there asked how he had come to leave England on the vessel he had left on, and in whose name the arrangements had been made, and whether he had a contact address for the man in the country he declined to name. *The arrangements were made through informal channels*, he had said, on four separate occasions, to four separate officials, with four separate clipboards. *I cannot identify the individuals.* This was accurate. All of it was accurate. Accuracy was not the constraint he was working within; it was the instrument he was using.

The elm corner. He turned onto Elm Street.

He had wondered, sometimes, in the years between Sherbrooke and here, whether the compression was something that accumulated. He had seen it accumulate in Vienna, before he left — the way people began to answer certain questions in a certain way, the frame narrowing over months until what remained inside it was very small, very precise, and very carefully maintained. He had seen it in Paris. He had seen it among the people who traveled the routes he had traveled, the ones going in different directions on the same unreported vessels, each with their own particular frame and their own particular version of the accurate statement. You could always tell. Not from outside — outside it looked merely like careful speech — but from the angle he had, the interior angle, you could always tell. After a while, the compression was in the voice the way a person's specific gravity was in how they moved.

He had no certainty about what she knew. He had a recognition: the structure of a statement that does not extend beyond what has been decided. And he had, now, the fact of it — that there was another person in this town, at the foot of the commercial wharf, answering questions in precisely the way one answers questions when one has decided in advance what the scope of the answer will be.

There was nothing, at present, to do with it. He was walking home in the December dark past the school's east wall, which he did not look at, toward the Crow house on Elm Street, which had a light in the kitchen window that was visible from the end of the block.

---

He came through the mud room and hung his coat on the hook and his scarf on the hook beneath it and his hat on the shelf above.

Lillian was at the stove with a can opener and what appeared to be a can of tomatoes that was in the process of being persuaded to join a stockpot on the back burner. The kitchen smelled of onion and the early stages of something warm.

"Nothing?" she said, not turning.

"Not tonight."

She said it would come. She said that Whitmore was not, by nature, a man who was going to rush.

"He is not," Marcus said. "That is an accurate characterization."

She said something further about Whitmore and the general question of men who let things sit, and Marcus said yes, and she turned back to the stove.

He sat at the kitchen table and set his hands flat on the pine, which held the side-warmth of the stove, and looked at the dark window. His reflection was in it, imprecise. Behind his reflection, nothing — the December night, the street lamps making their circles on the pavement below his line of sight.

He was thinking about the harbor road and the woman at the wharf steps and the voice that had delivered its accurate statement in the same even register throughout — the wind, the body, the overseas mail — all at the same level, so that nothing he had said had changed the quality of the voice or given him any additional surface to read. He had given her nothing additional either. *Rarely now.* Accurate. Complete within its frame.

From below: Henry's footsteps on the cellar stairs, the solid sound of the kindling box being set down at the bottom. Lillian working the can opener, the stockpot beginning to consider its own temperature.

He pressed his hands flat on the table and held them there, and thought about the harbormaster's office, the rectangle of light in the cedar-shingled building against the December dark, the woman who was still there, probably, doing whatever it was she was doing — the gauge, the mooring piling, the next thing after that — at the foot of the commercial wharf.

He knew now something he had not known this morning. Not a fact about the body, not a fact about what she held. A different kind of fact: that she held something, and that she had decided what to answer, and that he had been, this afternoon, the person standing on the other side of that particular precision.

Henry came up through the cellar door and set the kindling box by the stove and said something about the cold in the cellar, and Lillian said something back about the cold everywhere, and Marcus looked at his hands on the table and said nothing, and the soup came slowly to temperature behind them all.
