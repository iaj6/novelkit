# Fifty Kilometers

The fence posts on the south side of the near field had heaved over the summer and needed straightening before the ground froze solid and made the work impossible until March. Albrecht had said so at breakfast in the tone that meant today, not this week. Hannah put on his old coat and met him at the south gate at half past eight.

The work had its rhythm: she gripped the post at the base with both hands and leaned her weight against it while he raised the driver and swung it down. The driver was a section of heavy iron pipe with capped ends, two feet long, and she felt every impact travel through the post and into her palms and up her arms, even when the swing went well. Her job was to hold the post true and her father's job was the swing, and they had done this enough times that she knew how to shift her weight for the angle and he knew how hard to swing for the resistance. They didn't talk. The work didn't need it.

Lonzo Reed had arrived at seven with the four prisoners and put them to work in the near field: the three older ones at the drainage ditch along the far boundary, and Karl Reinmar on the rock section — the near quarter of the field that gave up stones every year, heaved up by frost and thaw, needing to be cleared before spring tilling. He was working with his back mostly toward them, thirty feet away. She could see the effort of it when he lifted a larger stone — the set of his back, the shift of weight, the pause to gauge where it would sit on the wall — and then the rock placed and his hands going back to find the next one.

She was watching the fence. Then the line of posts toward the stone wall at the south boundary. Then the grey stubble of the corn rows stretching back toward the barn. The stalks had gone brittle after the first frosts and stood pale and discarded-looking, the way they did every year and the way she had not entirely stopped noticing. She breathed out and the cold made her breath visible and she breathed in and held the post steady for her father's next swing.

There was a sound from the field.

Not a word, exactly. More the sounds a person makes before words arrive — the consonants that come with lifting something heavier than expected, the involuntary noise that the effort produces before the person decides to say anything. An oath, maybe, or a complaint muttered at the ground. It came from Karl's direction, and it was in German, and she would have registered it and moved past it the way she moved past the German her father spoke at her back all morning, except that the German was shaped differently.

She could not have said how she knew this. Her German was the kitchen, the barn, her father's reading voice, the careful German Marcus Klein put on the board like a specimen pinned in place. What she heard from the field was none of those. The vowels sat lower in the mouth and had a particular rounded quality, the consonants softened in places she was unused to softening them. Like her father's voice when he was tired enough to stop attending to himself. Like something that had been shaped in one place and had traveled a long way without losing the shape.

Albrecht's arm stopped at the top of the arc.

The driver hung in the air. She felt the stillness of it as a change in the pressure she'd been bracing against — the downswing she'd been ready for, not arriving. One second. Two. She counted in the part of her that counted without being asked.

Then the driver came down. The impact traveled through her palms, the usual way.

He pulled the driver off the post and walked to the next one. He didn't speak and he didn't look toward the field. Hannah moved with him and took hold of the next post.

Karl set the stone down and went to find another.

---

At eleven they stopped. She felt the cold more in her hands now than she had at the start; the post-holding had held it off earlier, but standing still she noticed it. They walked back to the barn and she ran the pump at the corner of the barn wall and rinsed her hands in the cold water and stepped aside for her father.

She said: "Vater. Where exactly were you born? In Bavaria — which town?"

He looked at her. Not a suspicious look. More the look of a man who has been given something unexpected and is considering what to do with it.

"Feuchtwangen," he said.

She turned the name over: *Feuchtwangen*. She didn't know it. "Is it near Ansbach?" She had seen Ansbach on the map in the geography classroom — a city label somewhere in the right general region, the part marked Bayern.

Her father dried his hands on the side of his trousers. He thought for a moment. "Forty kilometers or so. South."

He went into the barn. She stood at the pump for another second with the cold still in her hands and then followed.

He did not ask why she wanted to know.

---

She walked to school in just over an hour. The road was frozen in its ruts from the last week's mud and the sky was low and white, the kind of November sky that committed to nothing. She thought about the fence and then about the pump and then about neither, or she thought about them without exactly thinking — the way a stone turns in water without going anywhere, just moving with the current.

*Feuchtwangen.* Forty kilometers south of Ansbach. She had a fact. She didn't yet know what it was a fact about.

The afternoon session was Latin. Marcus Klein was going over the gerundive of obligation — *legendus, legenda, legendum*, must be read, needing to be read, the construction that made a thing's necessity part of its name. She wrote down what he put on the board and the parsing that went with it, but the lesson was sitting on the surface of her attention without going in. Across the row, Ada Wheeler was taking notes with the absorbed efficiency of someone who intended to do well on the end-of-term examination. Hannah looked at her own notes. *Legendum est.* It is necessary to read. It must be read. She wrote it and turned the page.

The geography classroom was across the hall and empty in the afternoons except for study hall. She went there during free period.

The European map was on the center wall — the large one with the colored zones that had been updated twice since she'd started paying attention to it. She had looked at this map before, everyone had, everyone had stood in front of it since December 1941 looking for the names the radio announced. Tunisia. Sicily. The long boot of Italy with the colored lines marking where the armies currently were. She moved past those now and found Germany.

Ansbach was there, labeled in the southern half of Germany, in the region the legend marked *Bayern*. She put her thumb on it. Forty kilometers south. She moved her finger down, measuring against the map's scale by eye. Feuchtwangen was not labeled — it was not a large enough town for a map that fit the continent on a single sheet — but she could find the rough zone of it. A territory about the size of her palm, south of Ansbach, in the country that was her father's first country.

She thought about fifty kilometers. She didn't know Karl's town. She hadn't been told and she couldn't determine it from a sound heard once across a field. But she could see the region on this map. What looked like a broad area was small in the way that mattered — the kind of distance where a phrase, a particular way of shaping a vowel low in the mouth, would carry from one town to the next and still arrive recognizable a generation later. Less than a morning's drive, if there were such a thing as driving for pleasure, which there wasn't, not anymore.

Her thumb was still on Ansbach. She moved it slowly south and then stopped.

There was a shape to it — the pause, the name, the fifty kilometers she was approximating here with her eye — but she couldn't yet say what the shape was the outline of. She had the geography. She didn't have the rest.

She heard the door.

Ruth Stanhope came in carrying her lunch bag and stopped when she saw Hannah at the map. "Bavaria? Are you doing a report?"

"No," Hannah said.

Ruth sat down and opened her bag. Hannah looked at the map for a moment longer, then went to sit across from her.

---

The cows were already in from the pasture when she got home. She could hear them through the barn wall — the shifting weight, the low sounds they made when nothing was wrong. She changed her clothes and went out.

The evening milking took until six. She did the far half of the stalls and her father did the near half and they worked toward each other in the usual way, the sound of the milk in the bucket changing pitch as the bucket filled. Her father spoke to the cows in German, the occasional word, *ruhig, ruhig*, nothing that required an answer. She spoke to hers too, in whatever came, English mostly. The cows didn't distinguish.

She was at the barn door when the prisoners came in from the field at half past four, before she'd started. Lonzo Reed had them moving back along the fence line toward the icehouse: the heavyset one first, then the thin one who walked with his weight shifted left, then the older one, then Karl. Lonzo walked behind. They moved in the way they moved at the end of the day — finished, the work behind them instead of ahead.

Karl passed within ten feet of where she was standing.

He didn't look at her. His eyes were on the ground in front of him or on the icehouse door. But when he came level with Lonzo's stool he turned his head and nodded — just that, a brief drop and lift of his chin, nothing elaborate. And Lonzo, folding his newspaper to tuck it under his arm, looked up and nodded back. The same register. Neither of them spoke.

Karl went into the icehouse. Lonzo set his back against the outer wall.

She went into the barn.

---

Supper was the white bean soup Magda made on Mondays. By Wednesday it had developed further in the pot, richer, the beans softer. Hannah ate it without thinking about it and then noticed she had eaten most of the bowl.

Her father said grace in German. His hands were flat on the table and his voice was quiet in the way it was only for the grace — unhurried, without performance. The same words she had heard every evening of her life. They had become a texture she moved through rather than words she followed, and she felt the usual faint guilt about this.

The radio in the corner was on and turned low. The six o'clock news, or possibly already past it. She couldn't have said which front the announcer was reporting on tonight; the voice arrived in the kitchen as a murmur rather than language, general meaning without specific fact. Her father did not turn it up. He had stopped turning it up some weeks ago.

Magda was at the table. She had set it, as she always did, and she was eating now in the same efficient, present way she did everything in the kitchen. She was not absent. She was precisely here. She looked at her bowl and occasionally at nothing in particular.

Hannah looked at her father's hands around his soup bowl. Wide across the knuckle. The skin at the back darkened in the deep creases the way farm skin got.

*Feuchtwangen.* The word came up and she held it quietly, without pushing at it. Two seconds of stillness at the top of a post-driver's arc.

She had what she had tonight. Tomorrow she would have whatever tomorrow gave her.

The kitchen smelled of the soup and the wood smoke and underneath both, through the west wall, faintly but reliably, the silage from the barn. The smell that was always there.

Her father's hands around his bowl.

The lamp on the table.

The radio, low, saying something about somewhere far away.
