# The School Board

Henry had told him on a Monday, at supper. Someone had filed a question with the selectmen — *question* was the word Henry used, and Marcus registered both the accuracy and the careful choice — about the German-language instructor at the school, and the selectmen had passed it to the school board. The board would address it at the February meeting. Three members would be present, not the full board: Dwight Hatch, Agnes Vane, Gordon Sears. Marcus knew Hatch by sight and Vane by name. He had not encountered Sears.

Henry said: "Your credentials are on file."

Marcus said: "Yes."

He had gone upstairs after supper and opened the bottom drawer of the chest. The file had been with him since Cambridge — since before Cambridge, since London, some of it dated from the years between Vienna and Paris when there had still been institutions willing to put letterheads on things. He laid the papers on the desk and went through them. Emergency Committee in Aid of Displaced Foreign Scholars, dated November 1940, signed by Harrington, Whitmore's predecessor. His Vienna university credentials: the teaching certification, the faculty appointment letter. These were dated 1934. The letterhead was the university's, as it had existed before the annexation — the institution itself, its seal, its address, the name of the rector who had signed. He was aware that the letterhead cut both ways. The same date that documented his legitimate academic standing also marked it as belonging to an institution that no longer functioned as it had, in a country no longer sovereign. A board member predisposed to suspicion could read the letterhead as easily as one predisposed to fairness.

He included the documents anyway. A man who withheld his best evidence because the evidence had a complicated history was not a man who would be persuasive. The Cambridge letter. His Maine teaching certificate, issued in the spring of 1942 after the state board had reviewed his credentials and satisfied itself that a man with his qualifications could instruct the children of Maine in the German language. He thought briefly about the draftsmanship of that particular irony and put it aside.

He arranged the papers in order of probable utility. Emergency Committee letter first — it was the document most legible to an American institutional mind, because it was American, and because it named what he was: a scholar displaced by circumstances the Committee had determined to be genuine. His credentials after that. The outcomes list at the end, because the outcomes list was the argument that would land with a school board, and it should arrive last.

He spent the next day as he spent most Tuesday afternoons: teaching the Latin third-year through the gerundive, then the German second-year through the passive voice, then correcting the third-year examination papers in the empty classroom while the building went quiet around him. He had considered the probable objections while walking the three blocks home. Agnes Vane would raise something articulate; she had the reputation of a woman who chose her concerns precisely. Gordon Sears would raise something direct. Hatch, who organized such meetings, would say the least and carry the most weight.

He thought about what Vane might mean by *articulate but not specific*, which was the phrase Henry had used. He had encountered this category before. It was more resistant than outright hostility, because outright hostility could be countered with evidence, and this other thing — the concern that takes care to avoid stating itself as prejudice while remaining, at its foundation, exactly that — could not be countered by the same instruments. It required a different approach. Not engagement on its terms, but refusal to engage on its terms while appearing to engage. He had made an art of this, over the years, in several languages.

Henry had said: "I'll be there." And Marcus had said he didn't need a witness; Henry had said he knew; and without either of them elaborating further Marcus had understood that Henry would come anyway.

---

The Legion hall on February evenings had the quality of a room borrowed from its proper use. The rationing board used it from nine to four on weekdays; the folding tables were still arranged in that configuration — two parallel rows facing the front — and the school board had simply relocated the chairs and positioned a single additional chair, unambiguously solitary, before the table. The American Legion banner ran the width of the back wall, its gold fringe losing color at the folds. The electric light was two bulbs in ceiling fixtures and was adequate and no more. The windows on the north side showed the street: black, empty, the temperature below ten and still dropping. It was seven twenty-two.

Marcus was early. He had learned this from experience in rooms not unlike this one — preferred the physical fact before the people arrived, preferred to know where the door was, where his chair sat in relation to theirs, how the light fell across the table, whether there was anything in the room that would require management. He had done it in Vienna before the meeting where they had informed him that his faculty position would need to be reviewed in light of recent developments. In Paris before two consular appointments. In London at the Home Office. In Sherbrooke — not preparation, there, because in Sherbrooke one arrived as instructed rather than early, but in the waiting rooms at Sherbrooke he had learned what the same instinct looked like when it had no purchase on the room. In Cambridge, twice. In Coldwater Reach, in March 1942, before the school board had offered him the appointment.

He straightened his documents once and did not touch them again.

Henry came in at twenty past. He crossed directly to the chair along the right wall — not at the table, not at Marcus's chair — settled into it, and opened the Bangor Daily News he had folded under his arm. He did not look at Marcus; he gave his attention to the newspaper with the ease of a man who had arranged himself exactly where he meant to be and saw no reason to comment on it.

The positioning was correct. Henry knew it was correct, and Marcus knew it, and there was nothing to say about it. The one wrong thing would have been to acknowledge it. He kept his eyes on the table.

Hatch came in at twenty-eight minutes past, followed by Vane and then Sears. They were carrying their coats — they had not been here long enough to have hung them anywhere — and they settled into their chairs with the efficiency of people who had occupied positions at this table before and had no need to establish the configuration. Hatch had a yellow legal pad and a pen. Vane had a folder.

Sears had nothing. He folded his hands on the table and looked at Marcus with the directness of a man who found the whole proceeding tiresome in ways he was not at leisure to say.

---

"We've had some concerns raised," Hatch began, with the tone of a man measuring how much weight to give the word. "From parents. About the, ah — the current situation. Whether it's appropriate to have a German-language instructor at the school, given the national circumstances."

"The national circumstances," Marcus said.

"The country of the instructor's birth being currently at war with this country."

"I was born in Austria," Marcus said. "Which was annexed by Germany in March 1938, against the will of its government, and which I departed in 1936 — two years before that annexation — for reasons that I can detail for the board if it wishes." He moved the Emergency Committee letter across the table. "I have been on the rolls of the Emergency Committee in Aid of Displaced Foreign Scholars since November 1940. I was hired by Coldwater High School in March 1942. I have been teaching here for twenty-three months."

He laid the outcomes list on the table. Examination results, progression rates, the comparative data from the three years prior to his hire when the German course enrollment had been running at a different level. The numbers were legible. He had prepared them to be legible to a school board, which was different from preparing them to be legible to a faculty committee — less subordinate structure, more column alignment — and he had done it without particular satisfaction but with the same care he brought to anything that was worth doing correctly.

Hatch read the Emergency Committee letter. Vane opened her folder and produced a copy of something — Marcus could see from across the table it was his teaching certificate, already on file as Henry had said. She compared it to the document in front of her, set both on the table, and looked at him.

Agnes Vane was a composed woman. She had the specific composure of someone who had been in rooms where things were decided, who had learned that the most effective way to carry weight was not to display it. She said: "We understand your history, Mr. Klein. The concern that was brought forward has to do, specifically, with —" She paused. She was, he thought, genuinely searching for a formulation. "The language itself. At this particular time."

Marcus waited.

She let the sentence end where it was.

"I teach German," Marcus said, "to American students who may need to speak it. In a military capacity, in an administrative capacity — in whatever capacity the period following this war creates a need for Americans who can speak German to German-speaking people. The utility of that instruction does not diminish because the language is currently spoken by an enemy government. It increases, if we are thinking about the war's conclusion and what follows it."

"That's a long view," Vane said.

"The instruction of languages generally requires a long view," Marcus said. "One does not teach a child the subjunctive for immediate tactical deployment."

Something crossed Vane's face that was not quite a reaction and was not quite suppressed. She looked down at her folder.

"The thing I don't see," Sears said — he had been watching Marcus throughout with the patient expression of a man waiting for the moment when the real question became available — "is why we need it at all. German. I don't see that the school was worse off before we had a German-language course."

"The school had a German-language course before I arrived," Marcus said. "I replaced the previous instructor when he enlisted."

Sears considered this. "Well, I know that." He appeared to feel this was a beside-the-point correction. "What I mean is, why should we have one now. Given everything."

"Because the language does not belong to the government using it," Marcus said. "And because the students in my classroom will live through whatever comes after this war, and they will find the capacity to speak German more useful then than it has been in the last two years."

Sears looked at him for a long moment. His expression was the expression of a man who understood perfectly well that the person in front of him had the better argument and who had not yet decided whether the better argument was sufficient.

"You understand our position," Sears said.

"I understand it perfectly," Marcus said.

There was a pause in which the room was very quiet. Outside one of the north windows a car passed on the street — the only vehicle Marcus had heard in the twenty minutes since he arrived. Then it was quiet again.

Hatch cleared his throat. "I think we have what we need here." He looked at Vane, who nodded. He looked at Sears, who held Marcus's gaze for another half-second before he looked down at the table and nodded as well. "The board votes to continue Mr. Klein's appointment. Unanimous." He made a notation on his yellow legal pad. "We'll note in the record that a formal review was conducted and the appointment confirmed."

Marcus gathered his documents. The Emergency Committee letter, the credentials, the outcomes list, back into the file in the order he had removed them. He was aware, as he did this, of the quality of the action — the same action he had performed at the end of several other meetings of this kind, the returning of papers to the file that had accompanied him through too many such rooms, and the specific texture of collecting those papers from a table where they had been sufficient for the purpose.

The meeting had lasted thirty-eight minutes.

---

Outside, the night was clear and cold in the particular way of a February in coastal Maine — the cold not heavy or wet, as it was in a storm, but dry and still and absolute, the kind that entered the collar and the cuffs and made the walk home a matter of economy: the fewest unnecessary motions, the steadiest pace, the breath that went out and came back cold and went out again.

The street was empty. Coldwater Reach cleared itself by seven on nights like this; the residents who were inside were inside and the ones who had gone out had already returned. The door of the Legion hall closed behind them, and the sound of it carried in the cold air farther than the same door would have carried in any other season.

They walked without speaking. Down to the corner of Bridge Street, left toward the harbor road. This was not the most direct route and Marcus was aware that he had not chosen it, had simply found himself beside Henry on this bearing without registering the decision. The harbor in February showed itself as it did in January — the dark water, the moored boats, the lights on the commercial wharf, the breakwater running out from the north shore, the algae line black at the waterline. The cold sat on the surface of the water without disturbing it. Very still.

They turned onto Birch Street. They turned onto Elm.

Three blocks from the Legion hall Henry said: "You were very calm."

"I have had some practice," Marcus said.

"I know," Henry said.

They walked.

The footsteps on the cold pavement were the only sound between the buildings. Marcus watched the light in the kitchen window of the house ahead — it was their house, theirs — and did not look away from it.

"I have too," Henry said.

Marcus did not answer.

They walked the rest of the way home in silence.

---

The house on Elm Street had a light in the kitchen window and the quality of the light was warm — the particular quality that distinguished a lit window from a lit-and-heated window, something in the color of it, in the depth, that Marcus had learned to read from the outside as he learned to read the inside of the house itself. Lillian had left the stove going. She was not in the kitchen — he could see through the glass that the kitchen was empty — but the pot was on the back element and the room was warm.

Henry hung his coat on the hook inside the door and went to the sink to fill the kettle without discussing it.

Marcus sat down at the kitchen table. He set his file folder on the chair beside him. The table was pine, the surface marked with years of use — the rings from cups and glasses, a long pale scratch across one corner from something Lillian used the table for that he had never identified, the general worn smoothness that accumulated in things that were used and attended to and neither replaced nor preserved. He put his hands flat on it.

The stove ticked. Henry filled the kettle.

Outside, February continued: the cold still, the street empty, the harbor somewhere below the last rise with its winter shape in the dark. He had his documents in the file. He had his classroom tomorrow, the Latin third-year in the first period, the German second-year in the third.

The kettle began to warm.
