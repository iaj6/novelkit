# August

The harbor in August looked like money, and in a working harbor that was exactly what it was. Two dozen boats' worth of fuel and catch and dock fees, the sardine shed processing three hundred cases a day against its peacetime two hundred, the mooring fields at capacity for the first time since the summer of 1941. The breakwater algae line sat dried and pale at the low-water mark. The gulls were loud. The smell from the processing shed — brine and the sharper, organic smell of the sardines themselves, the whole compound of a building working at capacity — rode the afternoon wind south and settled over the commercial wharf and didn't move.

She noted it the way she noted the fuel figures. Not with pleasure, exactly. With accuracy.

---

The *Halcyon* and the *Eda L.* needed to move to free up room for the two seasonal boats that had come up the coast on the sardine run: the *Nancy Marie* out of Spruce Head and a double-ended lobster boat called the *Ida May*, working north from Penobscot Bay. Getting the four boats into a configuration that worked had taken two mornings and three conversations with captains who did not want the conversations. By Thursday morning she had them settled. She walked the inside wharf with a measuring line to confirm the clearance, which was what she had calculated it to be, and wrote the new positions in the mooring log and moved on.

The rest of Thursday was the fuel log and the accounts. The allocation she had argued for in December — in Portland, in a room with seven other harbormasters, the one from Searsport doing most of the talking while she sat with her figures and waited — had held through the season. She entered the week's consumption and set the log on the shelf and went outside.

Fourteen working boats. All of them running consistent schedules, logging catches above the July average, the whole fleet moving in and out of the channel like a mechanism that had been correctly adjusted and left to run. She had the departure and return log for July and the first weeks of August in front of her when she came back inside, forty-odd pages of entries in the standard format, and what those pages showed was a harbor operating within its own patterns. Nothing short. Nothing disrupted.

She noted this. The way you noted a boat's trim when it was running right — not because you were surprised, but because you kept checking.

The fleet operational. The accounts balanced. The tide log accurate and complete.

She put the ledger on the shelf and went back out to count the morning departures.

---

The news about Galen Fuller came on a Tuesday, mid-morning.

She was at the office with the provisioning log when Henry Cates came in. Robert's father — she placed him by vessel before face, the way she placed everyone: the *Bethia*, dock 7, a steady man who had worked that boat for over twenty years without presenting her with anything she could not solve in a conversation. He stood in the doorway without speaking, and she understood from this that he had not come about the boat.

She waited.

"Bertrand was on Bay Street this morning," he said. "The Fuller house."

She set her pencil down.

He told her the rest of it in the flat, factual manner in which such things were delivered. Galen Fuller. June seventeenth. Normandy — the telegram said Normandy, it did not say where in Normandy, and she did not ask. Two months and a few days between that date and this Tuesday morning in August.

She held the numbers. June seventeenth was eleven days after the landing. Sixty-six days between the seventeenth and this Tuesday morning in August. Both figures presented themselves and she let them sit without deciding what to do with them, the way she sat with a weather forecast that was only probably right.

She had known, since June sixth, that both of those young men could be dead and their families might not know yet. The notification system was not indifferent, only slow — no pattern she had been able to establish in the lag between the fact and the word of it, no correlation with branch of service or theater of operation or any other variable she had tried. Some telegrams came in two weeks. Some took months. She had set this knowledge aside the way you set aside what you cannot act on, and she had watched June go into July and July into August, and she had kept the log and managed the fleet.

Now one of them had resolved.

"The family all right," she said.

"As well as you'd expect." He said it carefully, the words measured, the way Bertrand measured his own words on Bay Street. "Ruth's sister came up from Tenant's Harbor last week. She's still there. Bertrand stayed until nine."

Calvin Fuller had been working the *Helena* alone since the spring of 1943, sixteen months now, since the Army took Galen and Calvin's back gave out the same season. She saw him in the departure log most Tuesdays: *Helena*, dock 3 row, one operator, going out and coming back in without notation beyond the required times.

Robert Cates had been on the same beach. She looked at Henry Cates standing in her doorway and understood that the only thing she could offer him was the absence of bad news, which was not the same as good news, but was what she had. You did not say: *your son has not been killed yet.* You did not say it because the sentence was true and incomplete in equal measure.

"Thank you," she said.

He nodded and went.

She sat with the provisioning log for a moment without working through it. The morning's entries were a sardine supplier's count and two mooring payments.

June seventeenth. She did not know what the front's position had been on that date — whether the worst of the landing's chaos had passed by then or was still present, whether eleven days was enough time for anything she might call a consolidation. It did not change the date. She picked up her pencil and returned to the provisioning log.

---

She saw Ruth Fuller two days later, at the harbor store.

Beal's ran a counter at the harbor end — not the full general store up the hill on Main Street, but the harbor account, closer to the dock: flour, salt, kerosene wicks, tobacco, a few canned goods. She went Thursday mornings. She had not expected to find Ruth Fuller there, and she supposed Ruth had not expected to find her.

Ruth was at the counter when Eira came in, a provisioning slip in her hand, waiting for Myra Beal to come out from the back. The store was not large. The counter was the counter. Eira set her own list down.

She had known Ruth for nine years in the way you knew everyone in a town of eight hundred: by context rather than occasion, by sight at the general store and the Legion hall and the dock, by the accumulated familiarity of years of shared weather and errand. She knew that Ruth ran her dragger herself, on the days the fuel allocation allowed, and had been running it since Galen went to the Army in 1943. She knew that Ruth Fuller was not a woman who came apart in front of the harbor store when she could avoid it.

She was aware, standing two feet from Ruth, of the inventory of things she might say. She ran through it quickly. Something about how sorry she was. Something about what Galen had been part of — about the campaign, its progress, its meaning, the several framings that arranged a man's death in the vocabulary of something large enough to hold it. She knew these framings. She had received versions of all of them after Owen, and she had accepted each one with something that had felt, at the time, like gratitude, and none of them had reached the actual thing, which was that Owen was gone and the harbor was not and she had the work to do.

What she could offer Ruth Fuller was what she knew from the inside of the harbor work. Not the other kind.

She looked up.

She said: "He was a good man with a boat."

Ruth Fuller looked at her. Not grateful, not surprised. She looked at the sentence the way you looked at a measurement that came out right — as if confirming something she had already known.

"He was," she said.

Myra came through from the back with Ruth's order. They moved aside. Eira paid for her flour and her wicks and walked back down to the harbor.

He had been a good man with a boat. She had watched it from the dock log and from the mooring rotation and from the fifteen years of entries in the departure record. That was her authority.

The rest of the afternoon was the ordinary work: the mooring rotation check, the fuel certificate for the *Eda L.* that had been sitting in the district mail tray since the week before, a note to the sardine shed foreman about the truck-pickup window she had moved in July. She dealt with each of them in turn and entered the results in their columns and set the logbooks on their shelves.

---

She did the breakwater walk at four o'clock, as she did each week in the season.

The summer tides ran lower than the spring or winter tides, which pulled back the harbor enough to expose the lower granite courses — the places where winter storm damage showed as cracking or displacement that the high water covered the rest of the year. She went block by block, running the seams with her eye, checking the mortar courses for new fracture, noting the algae line at the waterline and its position against the August low-water prediction. The algae line in August had a different quality than in November: dried pale and brittle on the upper face, the smell less sharp in the heat, the whole surface texture of it different from the slick November version that cost you your footing if you weren't careful.

Nothing to flag. The two blocks on the north-facing run she had marked in May were unchanged — the crack in the lower seam had not progressed. She would note them stable and carry them on the winter watch list.

She walked to the seaward end and stood.

The harbor was behind her. The afternoon light came from the southwest at this hour — flat on the water, turning the surface pewter-gray where it ran in shadow and a thin, particular silver where it caught the sun direct. The chop was easy, the kind that came off the channel in a moderate southwest. A lobster smack on the north approach, hull-down still, making its run in. She watched it until she had the hull line and the approach angle: the *Bethia*, coming in on the north track that Henry Cates always used for dock 7. Expected in the count. She had expected him.

She had been standing at this end of this breakwater for eight years.

The first time had been in November of 1935, a week and a half after Owen's funeral, with the December cold already working its way in from the north and the fleet uncertain who it was taking direction from. She had not stood at the seaward end very long that first time. There was too much to do, and she had been in the part of learning the harbor that was also the part of learning to be someone the harbor would take seriously. She had walked the breakwater for the inspection and she had done the inspection and she had walked back.

At some point in the years since, the harbor had stopped being a thing she held at a slight remove, the way you held something that had belonged to another person. It was not an event she could find in the log.

Galen Fuller was dead in France, his telegram sixty-six days in transit. His wife was going to run the dragger herself and the departure log would show it beginning this fall and probably through the next several years, and Eira was going to enter those departures without annotation because they required none.

The body that came in on the ocean face of this breakwater last October — she was standing at the approximate end where Hector Brann had found him in the lower course on the morning of the twenty-eighth — had been a man named Jan Václav Horak, Czech national, displaced person, last address Halifax in 1942. He had gotten this far. He had not gotten the rest of the way to wherever he had been going. The record had his name now, and the official ruling, and the date.

She was still here. The harbor was still here.

The *Bethia* made the turn at the harbor mouth and came down the line to dock 7, clean and even, the way a man worked a boat he had worked for twenty years and would work until his body stopped letting him.

She turned and walked back to the office.

---

The tide was at three-foot-eight on the ebb, on schedule. She entered the reading in the log and set her pencil on the book.

Through the window: the commercial wharf settling into its evening quiet, the last of the day's work done, the two new boats in their adjusted positions on the inside. The light going long and gold over the water the way it went in August and only in August, already tilting toward the ordinary dark.

She sorted the week's mooring fee receipts in order, the *Halcyon* and the *Eda L.* and the *Nancy Marie* and the *Ida May* in their new positions, everything accounted for. The harbor smell came through the bottom crack of the window — brine, diesel, the last of the processing shed's day.

She had the receipts sorted and the desk cleared by five-thirty.

She closed the ledger. She turned off the lamp. She went out, and the harbor was behind her, and the hill road was ahead, and the August evening was doing what August evenings did in Coldwater Reach — holding its light past the end of the work day, the sky going pale and clear in the west before it finally, gradually, let the dark come in.
