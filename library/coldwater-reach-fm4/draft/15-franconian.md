# Franconian

The building had been mostly empty for an hour. He could hear the custodian's cart somewhere down the east corridor — the particular sound of a bucket set down and not yet retrieved — and the last of the afternoon light came through the west windows at a low angle, illuminating the chalkboard's residue in a way that was not visible during lessons. The third declension. A tense paradigm. He had been trying to erase the board to something approaching clean when Hannah Pfeiffer knocked and came in.

She was carrying her Latin textbook and, under it, the German grammar she had been borrowing and returning in alternation since January. She set it on the nearest desk and did not sit.

"I heard something," she said. "One of the prisoners — Karl — he was talking to someone through the icehouse window. I was at the south fence, and I wasn't listening on purpose. I was there to reset a post."

He put the eraser down.

"A phrase," she said. "About the harvest. I'm going to try to say it right."

She gave it to him.

Her German was her father's German, fundamentally — the vowels slightly flattened in the Bavarian way, the consonants not quite Munich and not quite rural. For a girl who had spoken it since before she spoke English, she had the unselfconsciousness of a native and the slight displacement of someone who had learned it in translation, in a country where it was not the native tongue. She was trying to reproduce what she had heard, and what she had heard was not her father's dialect.

He received the phrase as two things simultaneously. As a linguistic object, first — the particular rounding of the *a*, the contraction that shifted stress from the first syllable to the second. As a problem, second. He had been aware of the problem for some months without committing to its full assembly; the phrase she had given him was the kind of precision that made assembly unavoidable.

"That is a harvest idiom," he said. "An Upper Franconian form. Not Bavarian. Not standard German." He came away from the board and sat at the teacher's desk. The chair was the school's regulation chair, wooden, the finish gone from the arms. He had been sitting in it for two years and had stopped noticing it. "The idiom comes from the communities along the Upper Main River. The country between Nuremberg and the Czech border."

Hannah was quiet. She had the quality — he had observed it across several months of watching her in Latin, and on the few occasions their other conversations had warranted this kind of attention — of hearing things completely before she responded.

"He grew up hearing that dialect," Marcus said. "The phrase, at minimum, belongs to someone who learned it at home. It is not something you acquire as an adult."

"Where in Franconia?" she said.

"Where in Franconia is he from?"

"I think Ansbach. Someone said Ansbach."

Ansbach was Middle Franconia — the flat country west of Nuremberg, the agricultural plain, the market town that had been a regional center for several centuries without being particularly close to anything. The speech there was recognizably related to what she had given him. It was not identical. The stress pattern she had reproduced was not the Ansbach form.

"The phrase is from further east than Ansbach," he said. "Twenty, perhaps thirty kilometers east of there. The valley between Feuchtwangen and Dinkelsbühl, or the villages east of Dinkelsbühl toward the Bohemian hills."

She looked at him.

"That's where my father is from," she said.

"Yes," Marcus said.

There was a pause. Outside the window the afternoon went on being April — the specific quality of late light in a month where the cold was not yet gone but the angle of the sun had changed, so that the warmth and the cold arrived on different sides of the same air. He had found April in coastal Maine stranger than November. November he had understood immediately. April was something he was still learning.

"Thank you," Hannah said. She picked up her Latin textbook. The German grammar she left on the desk.

"The translation," she said from the doorway.

He had been waiting to see if she would ask. "The field takes what it is owed, and the man who worked it walks home lighter." He paused. "More or less. Some things do not translate exactly."

She nodded. He heard her footsteps recede down the corridor.

---

He sat for some time in the teacher's chair.

The precise measure of the phrase's origin — Feuchtwangen and eastward, toward the Czech border — was not information he had possessed before. He had possessed the general geography: Karl Reinmar was from Franconia, Hannah had established that much at the fence in October, and Albrecht Pfeiffer was from Feuchtwangen, which was in the same county. Fifty kilometers between them, approximately, which had been the phrase Hannah had brought to him in November with the word *Landsmann* — a word he had translated precisely, and had declined, at the time, to follow to its geographic conclusion.

Now the phrase she had given him resolved the fifty-kilometer estimate into a specific corridor. Not the broad territory of Franconia but the particular landscape east of Feuchtwangen — the country running toward the Franconian Heights, toward the Bohemian Forest, toward the border that the maps had marked and the communities on either side had spent several centuries treating as somewhat negotiable.

The Czech border. He stood and went to the window.

He had heard the name Horak in late April — it had come into the school the way things came into the school from the official mail, which was to say through the post office and Beal's general store and by the end of the week through three different conversations he had not sought. Jan Václav Horak. Czech national. Displaced person. Halifax, 1942. He had heard it and had not needed to ask any follow-up questions, because he knew what the category meant.

He was perhaps the only person in Coldwater Reach who knew from the inside what the category meant. That was not a distinction he had sought. He had simply been, for a period of years, a person who moved through the administrative machinery of displacement — the waiting rooms, the transit papers, the offices that determined your right to remain in a given place — and the movement had given him a specific kind of knowledge. The kind that was not listed in a record but was present in the body, in the acquired habits of a person who had learned when to move quickly, when to move quietly, and how to take the measure of a room before committing to any action that might require its cooperation.

He knew what a displaced Czech national in Halifax in 1942 was. Not Jan Václav Horak specifically — he had not known the man — but the category of person. The category meant you were moving along routes that did not appear in any official file. It meant the people who moved you along those routes were people who had their own reasons for keeping their names out of the record. It meant your next station was reached through someone who knew someone who knew someone — a chain of connection that ran through origin, through language, through the specific geography of shared community.

Halifax was a port. From Halifax, moving south along the coast toward Maine, you would look for the contacts you could authenticate by origin. Not German-Americans generally. The German-American population along the Maine coast was not organized into a network of this kind, and a Czech displaced person would have no particular reason to seek it. What he would seek — what anyone in that position would seek, in Marcus's experience — was the specific German-Americans from the borderland communities, the families who had come from the country where the German and Czech populations had lived alongside each other for centuries, where the dialects carried inflections from both sides, where the family names crossed the border in both directions without particular comment, where you could authenticate a contact not by documentation but by the sounds of the dialect they had grown up in.

He was constructing this. He was aware that he was constructing it. The distinction between recognizing a shape and inventing one was not always as clear as he would have preferred — he had learned this too, in the waiting rooms and the transit offices, where constructed pictures sometimes replaced available facts because the available facts were insufficient and the constructed picture was more tolerable. He had seen what happened to people who committed to a constructed picture before the evidence warranted it. On several occasions he had been the subject of such a commitment.

He would not do that here.

And yet the shape was there regardless. A Czech displaced person, moving south from Halifax along the coastal routes. Reaching, at some point in the year between October 1942 and October 1943, the coast of Maine. Seeking a contact he had been given — a farm name, a family name, an address on a route that ran through the Franconian-Czech émigré network and terminated, if the network ran as such networks ran, at the one farm in the area whose owners came from exactly that borderland country.

The Pfeiffer farm. Three miles inland on Route 9. Albrecht Pfeiffer, from Feuchtwangen, who had paused for two seconds in October when a prisoner in his field used the vowels of his home country's dialect — and who had been speaking German at home, only German, since the declaration of war, as if the language were a room he was withdrawing into.

He moved to the window and looked at the street below. The elm branches had their April leaves — pale green, tentative, the color that lasted only a week or two before deepening. Henry had been remarking on the elms since March, watching for the first leaves the way a man watches for something he has been waiting on. He had been at the school sixteen years; he knew the elms' schedule.

The harbormaster would have the same letter he had heard about. The identification of the body — Jan Václav Horak, the Czech national, the displaced person — would have come to her office first, officially, and she had been holding whatever she held about the night of October twenty-sixth since October. He was certain now that she had been at the dock. He had been certain of this since December, when he had met her at the breakwater and recognized in the particular quality of her compression — her professional exactness, the way she answered the question she had decided to answer — the same instrument he used about Sherbrooke and Vienna.

He did not know if she had assembled what he had assembled. He did not know if she would want to.

He went back to the desk. His lesson book was open to the ablative notes — the third iteration, because the ablative absolute did not stay with students until it had been approached from three different directions, and he had not yet found the third direction. He closed the lesson book.

The German grammar Hannah had left was on the corner of the front desk. She had been doing this since January. He straightened it and set it where students left things for him. She would be back on Friday for Latin. Whether she came back for the grammar was her decision.

He took his coat from the hook by the door.

He did not know what had happened at the dock on the night of October twenty-sixth. He did not know whether the man who had come south from Halifax across the year had found what he was looking for when he found the Coldwater Reach commercial wharf, or whether he had arrived at the right address too late, or whether the address had been wrong from the start, or whether the Pfeiffer farm had nothing to do with it and the shape he had assembled was wrong in some fundamental way.

He put on his coat and hat.

What he had was the structure of a probable picture, assembled from a phrase a girl had reproduced at the end of a school day and from a year of not-quite-looking at the question the phrase had now made unavoidable. He knew what such shapes usually were, when the pieces fit as these pieces fit.

He pulled the door until the latch caught. Down the east corridor the custodian's cart was still.

He went down the stairs and out into the April air, which was cold on the shaded side of the building and not cold at all on the other.
