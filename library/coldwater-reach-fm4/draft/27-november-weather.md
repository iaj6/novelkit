# November Weather

The animals were inside by the first week of November, a week earlier than usual, which meant more silage than Hannah had planned for and a barn that never quite emptied of its warmth once the doors were closed. Her father had brought them in on the night the temperature dropped without warning—the 31st of October, the fourth cow already stiff-legged when he found her at supper—and after that it was clear the season was done. The fields the prisoners had spent October picking over for the last of the corn and the last of the squash were bare now under the sky, which had gone the flat white of early winter. The first real snow came the second week of November and stayed.

She did the morning milking at five and the evening milking at six and the late check at nine, which she had taken on in October and not set down. Three times a day into the barn, and between those: the school run, the Latin exercises at the kitchen table, the cooking on the evenings her mother did not come to make it herself.

The prisoners were doing maintenance. The four of them patched the north-facing boards of the barn wall in the mornings, working in pairs with oakum and pitch from the supply shed, their breath making small clouds in the cold air. By afternoon they were in the side yard splitting firewood, and she could hear it from inside the barn: the swing of the maul, the pause, the sound of wood giving. Lonzo sat at his stool near the yard gate with his rifle across his knees and his newspaper, his coat buttoned to the top.

Her father cut the work hours shorter as November went on. By the second week Lonzo was walking the four men into the icehouse by three in the afternoon, and the yard went quiet. The firewood stack grew against the south wall of the icehouse, and the barn wall held its new pitch, and the farm contracted the way it did every year: the fields empty, the animals inside, everything that had been spread out through the working season pulled back into a smaller perimeter.

---

Several evenings, when the late check took her to the barn's far end and she came back up the aisle toward the door, she stopped.

From the barn door she could see the icehouse window.

Karl's lamp was on most nights by that hour—a low warm square in the dark, steady. She could not see him clearly at this distance and did not try; she could see the light and the shadow of the table, and sometimes, if the lamp's angle was right, the small movement of a page turning. She had started noticing this in the first week of November. She had not gone closer.

She had not spoken to him since the first week of October.

The decision had been easy enough to make in Mr. Klein's classroom, with the four categories laid out and repatriation as the word that made the timeframe comprehensible. She had walked home from school that afternoon with it settled, and it had not wavered. Not going to the south fence felt, each time the work took her near it, like something she was doing rather than not doing. She was making a choice in October and she was still making it in November, every time the late check brought her to the barn door and the icehouse window was lit.

He looked different in November than he had in June. Not physically—not thinner or marked by the work in any way she could name. In June he had held himself with a particular watchfulness, the readiness of a person who does not yet know what a place will ask of him. She had seen it in the way he stood at the fence, the careful attention he brought to everything, the alertness of someone who had not yet learned where the fence lines were. By November that was gone. He knew the farm. He knew what it asked. He was reading the newspaper in the icehouse by lamplight with his head bent to the page, and his hands, when the angle of the lamp showed them, were still.

She knew what he was reading. The front page of the Bangor Daily had been running the word counterattack since the middle of December—the German advance in eastern Belgium, the Ardennes, the Allied lines pushed back. She could read the American reports; he could read them too, and could subtract from them what the American papers did not know how to say. What he did with that, when he bent his head to the comics page instead, she could not see from where she stood.

The cold came through the open doorway.

She went back to the Holsteins.

---

Her father turned the radio up in December.

He had been turning it down for two years, since the fall of 1942, when the news began going the direction it went. She had grown used to the low volume—the announcer's voice in the kitchen, available but not quite filling the space. In December he turned it up.

She was at the table doing her Latin exercises when the six o'clock broadcast began, and he turned the volume up before the announcer had finished the date. He sat in his chair—the straight-backed chair he had pulled to the radio in October of last year and kept there—and he did not speak.

The announcer was naming Belgian towns.

She heard them and held them inside her head for the geography book she would look at later, after her father went to the barn. The Ardennes, which the announcer said with a flat American vowel. The German counterattack—that was the phrase the radio used, the phrase the Bangor Daily used, the phrase her father listened to with his hands on his thighs and his face toward the speaker.

He held very still.

The lamp made his shadow long on the kitchen wall. He was not looking at her; he was not looking at anything in the room. She had watched him listen to these broadcasts across the fall, and she had thought at first that it was his way of paying attention, and later she had thought it was grief, and now she thought it was neither of those exactly. He was somewhere else. He was here and also in the country he had left, in the hills and the winter forest that the announcer was describing in flat American syllables. She had not understood this before the last year—not what it meant, not why the radio made the kitchen a room he was only partly in.

The broadcast ended. He turned the volume down. He stood, took his coat, and went out for the last barn check. She put the geography book away without consulting it.

---

Magda's spell came the second week of November.

Four days. The bedroom door closed when Hannah came to the kitchen at five-thirty; breakfast carried to the hall table and left, the coffee half-drunk, the bread gone. Her father went through his work without comment—the barn, the morning feeding, the maintenance work alongside the prisoners—as if this were the farm's normal pattern, which in one sense it was. He had been carrying these days for as long as Hannah could remember. She had seen him do it before the war, when she was twelve and fourteen. He did not speak about it. She had learned not to ask.

She did the cooking. She kept the kitchen warm. She went to school and came home and started supper, and her father came in from the barn and they ate. Three days, four days. The farm ran on fewer conversations and did not stop.

On the fifth morning she came in from the six-thirty milking and her mother was at the stove.

The lamp was on. The smell of coffee already—Magda must have been up a while. She was standing at the stove in her dress and apron, hair down and unbrushed, making the coffee with the care she brought to it: the measuring, the cold tap water, each step in the order it went.

She said: "Es ist heute kalt."

Hannah took off her coat and hung it on the hook. She sat down at the kitchen table.

"Ja, Mutter," she said.

That was all. The most ordinary sentence in the world—not apology, not announcement, not I'm back, just the temperature, the simple fact of November. It was cold today. Magda turned to get the cups, and Hannah set the table, and the kitchen moved through the ordinary morning.

She had stopped expecting this to be the last time. Not this winter—some time before this winter, without being able to say exactly when. She had also stopped believing the returns were fragile, had stopped watching her mother with that particular care as if looking directly at her would send her back. Whatever Magda moved between—whatever the distance was that she traveled when the door stayed closed—it was older than the war and would not end with it. What she could do was be in the kitchen when her mother came back to it.

The coffee came. They sat. Magda drank hers without speaking again and then moved to the icebox for breakfast, and Hannah set out the bread and the plates, and the morning went.

---

On a Tuesday morning in early December she was cutting across the south yard from the barn, going back to the kitchen for the seven o'clock breakfast, and she saw it on the outside sill of the icehouse window: a Bangor Daily, folded open, its corners wind-curled from being outside.

Not inside the window. On the outside ledge, where the cold had been at it overnight.

She stopped when she saw the fold. Even from where she stood she could read the page it was open to: not the front page, not the war news. The comics—the small square panels with their outlined figures and their speech bubbles, the idiom gathered in short lines that sounded nothing like the articles. She knew whose paper this was.

She picked it up. The date in the corner put it two Tuesdays back, not this week's edition. The paper was slightly swollen from the cold, the fold stiffened. She turned it over: the other side was the crossword, worked through in pencil—English words in German-shaped handwriting, the across clues and some of the downs.

She carried it inside and set it on the kitchen table.

She did not mention it. Her father came in at seven-thirty and she had coffee and bread ready, and the newspaper sat where she had put it. Later, when she came home from school, it had been moved to the side of the table where he kept his papers. She did not ask him about it.

---

The nine o'clock check, the last round of the night.

She went stall by stall with the lamp, checking the water and the feed, watching how the animals stood. Number four was eating steadily—good; she had been watching the fourth cow since October. Number twelve was against the back rail, and Hannah stopped and put her hand on the cow's neck until the animal shifted and settled. The barn was the warmest place on the farm at this hour, the twenty-two Holsteins in their stalls generating a warmth that had nothing to do with the stove.

She came back up the aisle and stopped at the barn door.

The icehouse window was lit.

She stood there—the cold on her face from the open doorway and her own lamp in her hand and her breath making small clouds—and looked at the amber square in the dark. No movement she could see, just the steady lamp and the dark around it and the snow between here and there, unmarked except for the boot-print path going to and from the icehouse door.

Behind her the Holsteins shifted in their stalls, the slow sounds of animals settling.

She stood until the cold had come through her coat.

Then she pulled the barn door closed and went back to the house.
