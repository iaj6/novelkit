# What Language

"I'm looking for whoever manages the school's rowing dock," she said.

He was coming out of his classroom. He stopped when he saw her and took her in: the notebook, the working clothes, the look of someone who had come from the harbor.

"Mrs. Bowman," he said.

She had come at half past three on a Tuesday. The school was letting out—she'd had to step aside twice on the east stairs, students going down in a rush. Up here the hallway was quiet now. A door at the far end stood open; she could hear someone erasing a blackboard, the long soft strokes of the eraser going back and forth.

"The rowing dock," she said. "The mooring anchor registration is expired. Two years at least. I need to speak with whoever is responsible for the facility."

"I don't manage the dock," he said. He thought for a moment. "I believe it falls to athletics. Mr. Folger." He reached back and switched off his classroom light. "But I know where the dock is. I can walk you to it, if that is useful."

It was useful. She said so.

She had been to the school three times in eight years. Once for a spring fair when Owen was still alive, when that was still the kind of event she attended. Once for the fuel allocation matter in '43 that had sent the wrong delivery order to the school's account and required a correction in person. Once when Hannah Pfeiffer had been sent home early in November with a fever and there was no one else to walk her the three miles to the farm. She had come to this building and now she had come again, and the fact that she had the dock question—an actual dock question, a real permit matter—made the trip a trip she could enter in no ledger but the one she kept for herself.

They went down the east stairs and out through the side door.

---

The school's east side faced a gravel lot and a row of birch saplings planted too close together, their trunks beginning to crowd. Past the birches the ground dropped toward the Reach. She could see the water between the trees—flat and gray-green today, the tide close to full, the late May light lying on it without any shadow.

"The athletics director," she said. "Folger. He'll need to come to my office before June. Vessel description and two dollars. There's a compliance matter as well—I'll document that separately."

"I will tell him," Marcus said.

They went down through the birches. The May ground was soft underfoot still, giving a little with each step, and the smell coming up the slope was mud and brine and the cut-grass smell of the bank. She had grown up on that smell.

The dock was at the foot of the slope: a twenty-foot timber platform set close to the waterline. Old construction—she could see it in the framing before she was down the bank. The stringers were mortised rather than bolted, the joints pegged with wood. Before 1937, probably. Before the new tide-study figures had gone into the compliance requirements for floating structures on the Reach. She made a note of it.

Two rowing shells were tied on the outside—cedar strip, matched pair, the school's name in black stencil on each bow. She checked the dock lines first. No rot at the hitches. Proper leads on the cleats, the bitter ends coiled and tucked. She checked the cleats: older hardware, the heads worn smooth, but the bolts were set well and there was no corrosion at the wood line. She stepped out onto the dock. The planks were sound—no give, no flex at the joints, no sound of water getting into the grain. A dock that had been looked after, whatever else it might be missing.

She walked the length of it and knelt at the landward end.

The mooring anchor was a forty-pound block, chain run through a fairlead and down to an eyebolt in the sill. She pulled the chain. No play at the eyebolt—that was correct, and she wrote it down. She ran her thumb along three feet of chain: corroded on the surface, the surface links gone orange, but the metal was solid underneath. She had seen worse. She had passed worse. This would do for another season if Folger got the registration filed.

The mooring tag was looped through the ring at the top of the chain. A hardware-store tag, the kind the office issued with each permit, the registration year stamped in red.

Nineteen forty-one.

She wrote it down. She wrote the chain scope and the platform height above the mean waterline and the dock's approximate position on the Reach. She would mark it on the chart when she got back. She would write the note about the platform height—four inches below the new compliance standard, a matter of record, not urgent—and Folger could come in and they would deal with it in order.

She stood up.

Marcus Klein was standing at the shore end of the dock, his hands in his coat pockets, looking at the water. He had not moved or spoken while she worked. He was the kind of man who did not fill a silence—she had noticed this the first time at the harbor in December, the way he had stood at the foot of the wharf and waited until she was ready to speak, not finding words to put in the space.

A lobster smack was crossing the middle water—she could see its shape, too far out to hear. The tide was as full as it was going to get. In an hour it would be running out.

She closed the notebook.

---

"There's something else I want to ask you," she said. "Not about the dock."

He turned.

She kept her eyes on the Reach.

She had not said this to anyone. She said it the way she said things that required care: directly, without preamble.

"Last October," she said. "The night before the body came in. I was doing a late check of the commercial moorings. Past eleven." A pause. "There was a man on the dock—the commercial dock, not this one. He said something. Two or three words, quietly. Not to me. I was thirty feet away, maybe more."

The anchor chain shifted once against the fairlead, the metal sound small and clear.

"It wasn't English," she said. "I've been trying to determine what language it was. I understand you speak several."

He was quiet for a moment. He was watching her face.

"Say it for me," he said.

---

She had been holding the sounds for seven months. They had worn down in that time—she knew that. Memory of sound was not like memory of a date or a measurement. She had done what she could to keep it: not calling it up too often, the way you kept anything fragile by not handling it. She had said it to herself a few times, in the early morning on the commercial dock, the harbor empty and the tide going out, standing where he had been standing. Trying to hear it again by saying it in the same place.

She said it: "'Prish-teh.' Something like that. And before it—a consonant cluster I didn't know. A zh sound with something harder behind it."

He was listening. The stillness of a man listening carefully, not waiting for his turn to speak.

"Say it again," he said.

She did. She could hear, saying it a second time, how imprecise it was. She had not known, when she first heard it, that she was committing it to memory. By the time she understood that she was, the man had already gone still and the moment was already going and what she was left with was this: two syllables, the stress, the consonant she couldn't reproduce properly. She had worked with worse instruments.

"Can you give me more?" he said. "Anything else you remember."

She thought. "The stress was on the first syllable," she said. "Hard on the first—harder than English. Harder than German. I know some German from trade, over the years. The stress in German follows the word. This didn't. It was fixed on the first syllable, and the rest was lower."

She stopped. That was all she had.

He was quiet. Not long.

"I think it was Czech," he said.

She looked at him.

His expression was precise. He was giving her what he knew, and careful not to go past it.

"I cannot be certain," he said. "From that fragment, certainty is not possible. But the consonant cluster you are describing—the sound behind the zh—and the stress pattern: first syllable, invariable, hard. That is Czech. It is also consistent with Slovak. Czech is more likely."

"Czech," she said.

"Yes."

She looked at the Reach. The smack had gone behind the point.

Czech. He had been speaking Czech on the commercial dock in October, in the dark, past eleven at night.

She had spent seven months not knowing what she had heard, and now she knew. The knowledge was smaller than the not-knowing. It did not close anything. It fit.

---

"The Coast Guard sent a letter in April," she said. "They identified the body. The name was Jan Václav Horak. Czech national. Last known location Halifax. October 1942."

She was watching his face when she said the name.

He looked at the Reach. One long beat. Then he nodded.

"Yes," he said. "That would be consistent."

She waited.

"Consistent with what?" she said.

He was still for a moment. She understood he was not avoiding the question—he was selecting.

"With a certain kind of person," he said. "A man moving through unofficial arrangements, in wartime. There were people who needed to reach places and had no official channel. There were networks for this—not organized in any central way, not documented in any record. Arranged, person to person, contact to contact. A man who needed to move from Halifax to Maine and had no other means." He paused. "I know the general shape of it. I don't know more than that."

The water moved small and steady against the pilings below.

She nodded once.

---

"Did you know him?" she said.

"No." A clean beat. "I knew the kind of person he might have been."

She looked at the Reach. She thought about what she was going to say. She had had it, she realized, since the letter came in April and she had read the name and the nationality and the last known location and understood what they added up to. She had not known she had it until now.

"So did I," she said. "After a fashion."

Neither of them spoke.

The May afternoon held the dock in its warmth. From somewhere in the school above them, a window came down—the sound of the sash, brief and ordinary, and then quiet. From where she was standing, she could see over the birch tops the edge of the commercial wharf. A quarter mile south and east. The place where she had been standing in October.

"Thank you," she said.

"Of course."

---

She stepped off the dock and went up the slope. The May grass gave softly under her boots, the ground still carrying the winter's water. She went through the birch saplings—their branches beginning to reach across the path, a few more years and the gap would close—and around the east side of the building and through the front lot to the main road.

The harbor was ten minutes down the hill.

The tide was turning when she got there. She could see it in how the boats had swung at their moorings—all of them around now, noses pointed toward the harbor mouth. The working day was almost done. The smacks were in. A man was coiling line at the end of the commercial wharf and did not look up when she passed.

She unlocked the office. Set the notebook on the chart table. The end-of-month register was still open on the desk from the morning, the last entry in her own handwriting: fuel delivery confirmed, Pelletier account, May 19.

She would need to add the rowing dock: anchor registration lapsed, platform noncompliant by four inches, Folger to come in before June. The fee. Two dollars.

Czech. Jan Václav Horak. Last known location Halifax, October 1942.

She picked up the pen.
