# D-Day

The field telephone rang at two minutes past three.

She was awake — had been since midnight, sitting at the chart table with the logbook open and the kerosene stove turned to its lowest heat. She had the prior week's entries in front of her. She was not reading them.

She picked up the receiver.

"Eira." Lawrence Cary's voice, Rockland, who had been on the Portland relay since at least midnight. She heard the quality of it before the words arrived. "It's on. The landing."

She held the receiver. Outside the harbor made its ordinary sounds: a hull settling against the dock, water at the breakwater base.

"When," she said.

"Hours ago. By now they're ashore."

"Thank you, Lawrence."

"I thought you'd want to know."

"I did."

She replaced the receiver. The kerosene stove ticked. Through the window the harbor was dark and still — the boats at their moorings, the breakwater a low shape against the sky that was not quite sky yet, the channel running south beyond it in the dark.

She opened the logbook to June sixth and wrote the notation. Time, source, content. *At 03:02, L. Cary, Rockland, relays: landing at Normandy underway, troops reported ashore.* She kept it to the facts.

She read the notation back. She closed the book.

She sat.

Three hours yet before the boats would move. She had the five o'clock tide reading, the mooring check, the loose cleat on dock 3 she had been carrying the wrench for since April without stopping to use it. These things were not going anywhere. She sat at the chart table for a while with the stove ticking and the harbor outside, and she let the hour be what it was.

---

Dawn came at four forty-three. She had the tide reading by five: six-foot ebb, running clean past the breakwater on schedule. She entered the figure in the column beside the prior days — June fourth, June fifth, June sixth, the same eleven-foot range the harbor had been running since the tidal surveys of the 1890s. She wrote the numbers in the column. They were correct. She shelved the book.

The boats began going out before the light was fully up. The morning was the finest of the early summer so far — clear sky high and pale, no cloud anywhere, the air holding the particular stillness of a calm June morning before the southwest wind found the channel. She stood in the doorway and watched the *Fair Weather* clear the breakwater at five thirty-nine, the *Edna May* following at five forty-two. The diesels carrying south on the flat water. A fine morning for it.

She went out to the dock at six for the mooring check. South row first, then north. Fourteen boats in harbor this morning — she counted them by name and knew each berth, each owner, each boat's usual schedule. The count was down from the twenty-two she had managed before the fuel allocations reduced the working fleet to what it could sustain. Fourteen. She walked the south row and made her notes.

Dock 3. She stopped at the second post. The cleat was iron, surface-rusted, the near bolt working loose since the ice-out, and she had been carrying the wrench in her coat pocket for six weeks at least and had noted the cleat each time and kept walking. Not this morning. She pulled the wrench out and put it to the bolt and tightened. The cleat held. She checked the second bolt — still sound. She stood up and put the wrench back.

---

Galen Fuller was twenty-two. She had watched him on this dock since he was twelve years old, carrying his father's bait boxes and getting his hands wrong on the lines and learning the dock the way you learned it. Before the Army took him in the spring of 1943 he had crewed the *Helena* for a season and a half after his father's back gave out, and she had signed off on his vessel certification herself. She knew his hands from the bowline. She knew his father's boat, registered to Calvin Fuller, laid up since the back gave out entirely.

Robert Cates was twenty-four. Third generation on this dock. His grandfather had fished the Reach before the harbormaster's records existed in ledger form. His father had the *Bethia*, dock 7, which was at dock 7 this morning with Robert Cates not aboard it. The family had not received a telegram since the spring. This was, for now, what they had to go on.

She did not know which beach. She did not know if either of them had made it off it. She did not know if either of them was alive.

She entered the morning departures. Time, vessel name, estimated return. Weather notation: *clear, no wind, sea state 1.*

---

At Beal's the radio had been on since early morning. She could hear WLBZ through the screen door before she pushed it open. The signal was clear today — the weather giving it a clean run down from Bangor, the BBC relay coming through with only the background static that never entirely went away.

Small crowd for noon. Bertrand Hatch off his lunch break, standing near the wall with his coat on — she looked at him once. Two women from the canning cooperative, a fisherman she didn't place, possibly from Spruce Harbor. Agnes Vane in her winter coat, collar up despite the June warmth. A boy of twelve or so on the counter, told to wait and waiting. Dumont behind the register with his hands flat on the counter, listening.

The broadcast filled the room. The voice on the other end of the wire was flat and formal, the cadence of a text prepared in advance for reading, not speaking. She stood near the door and listened.

*...this great crusade, toward which we have striven these many months. The eyes of the world are upon you. The hopes and prayers of liberty-loving people everywhere march with you...*

The static ran under the words. The words ran over the static. She listened to the whole thing — the voice going on with its large sentences, its nouns assembled for history. She noticed the distance between the voice and this room, the compression of the wire and the signal that was the only reason the voice was here at all. She thought about the two men. She let the voice finish.

She bought her ration items: two points of tinned goods, the sugar allotment, a spool of wicking for the stove. She brought them to the counter. Dumont totaled the points without speaking.

Agnes Vane, near the radio, said: "God willing they'll come home by Christmas."

Nobody answered. The static continued. The broadcast continued. Eira counted back her change and put it in her coat pocket.

---

At two o'clock a boat came in with a bent prop shaft.

She could see the trouble from the dock. The vibration in the stern as it came through the harbor mouth — a particular shudder she knew, the kind that meant the shaft had found something solid and the boat had made the run back running rough, the blade bent off true and the whole aft section shaking with it. She went out.

The captain was already over the transom looking at the shaft when she reached the dock. A forty-footer from up the coast, out of somewhere north — Castine, maybe, or Sedgwick. She had seen him once at a Knox County meeting and not since.

"What did you hit," she said.

"A ledge, I think. Past the Thornton island marker. Not on my chart."

"It won't be on your chart." She looked at the shaft. Blade bent thirty degrees off true, maybe thirty-five. "You've been running like that since the marker."

"Almost two miles. I didn't know if she'd hold together."

"She held." She went to the office and called the repair yard at Searsport. She gave them the damage description — bent shaft, probable twenty-two inches, unclear if the strut bearing had taken secondary damage. They had a slip on Thursday. She came back out to the dock and gave the captain the day and told him what he needed for the documentation.

"There's also a shoal notification to file," she said. "Your encounter goes to the chart service. What's your best position on the ledge?"

He told her. She wrote it down. When he went off to see about an interim berth she returned to the office and wrote up the shoal report: the coordinates, the depth estimate, the vessel name, the date of encounter. She filled in the date — June 6, 1944 — and looked at it and looked at the rest of the form and finished the form.

The afternoon was the afternoon. The shoal notification. The vessel registration. A call from the Coast Guard station about a dragged buoy needing reset on Tuesday. She logged it for Tuesday's schedule. She answered the calls and wrote the entries and did the work, which was here regardless of what was in France, which required what it had always required.

At four-thirty she opened the logbook again. *At 03:02, L. Cary, Rockland, relays: landing at Normandy underway, troops reported ashore.* She read the notation through once. She turned the page and checked the prop shaft documentation. The figures were correct.

---

At five she walked out to the end of the breakwater.

Not a habit. She walked the granite blocks out to the harbor mouth and stood at the end in the June evening light — the long kind that lingered past the end of the work day, high and pale, the kind of light that would not go down on schedule.

The fishing boats were coming in on the evening tide. She counted them: the *Edna May* first, clearing the gap at a few minutes past five, Amos Frye at the wheel. The *Fair Weather* behind it, then the *Persephone* with Gus Hollis running the deck loaded, throttling down as he made the turn. She knew the sound of each engine by now in the same way she knew the voices in the post office or the shape of the handwriting in the dock registers. Eight boats in tonight, the full count of those that had gone out.

The *Bethia* was in the count. She watched it come through the gap — Robert Cates's father, running it alone, bringing it around to dock 7 with the practiced ease of a man who had been putting a boat in this berth for thirty years and would put it there without thinking as long as his body let him. She watched the *Bethia* make the turn.

Behind each boat was a family's calculation. Not a safe one — she had learned what happened when the safety failed, had learned it in the harbor before dawn, eight and a half years back, standing on the dock with a coil of line she had no use for anymore. Not safe. Reasonable. The calculation that a working life on the water was worth the terms it came on. She had been watching this calculation from this breakwater since before the post was hers.

The *Helena* was not in the count. Hadn't been since the autumn of 1942, the berth taken over by Winslow's *Penelope Ann* on a monthly arrangement. She did not look at the *Helena*'s berth. She looked at the boats that were there.

Galen Fuller might be dead. He might have been dead since before the harbor woke, since the dark hours when she was sitting at the chart table with Lawrence Cary's voice in the receiver. He might be in France tonight, alive, eating whatever they had given him, writing or not writing a letter home. She did not know. She would not know for some time. Robert Cates the same.

The last boat through the gap was the *Margaret Jean*, running behind as it sometimes did. She listened to the engine at idle — a vibration she didn't like, a loose fitting somewhere in the cooling system.

The harbor quieted, boat by boat. The diesels going down. The water at the breakwater base doing what it did at this hour — a low sound, not the harbor sound of noon or the harbor sound of the winter gales, but the specific sound of the harbor at the end of a June evening when the tide was turning and the boats were in and the light had not gone yet but was going.

She turned and walked back along the granite toward the shore.
