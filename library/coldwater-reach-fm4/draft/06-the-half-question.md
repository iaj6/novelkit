# The Half-Question

The conjugation *fero, ferre, tuli, latum* was on the board in his handwriting, which was smaller than the board required and which he had been compensating for, with limited success, since his first day at the school in March of the previous year. Third-period Latin, third-year students, eleven-thirty in the morning. The furnaces were working at full effort and losing to the cold that had flattened itself against the building sometime overnight. The panes fogged from the inside — not ice, not yet — but the thick condensation of two temperatures meeting each other at the glass. Through the clear portion at the upper edges of the nearest window, the schoolyard was grey and approximate, the far spruce a dark shape against a sky that had committed to nothing.

He went through the conjugation with the class. *Fero, ferre* — the shift from the root *fer-* to *tul-* in the perfect that required what he had come to think of, privately, as an act of trust: the language had been that particular way for long enough that arguing with it was beside the point. The passive forms were further difficulty. Michael Bates, third row from the left, had been having trouble with *fertur* since the Tuesday of the previous week — the same slight adjustment before the word arrived, as if it were sitting half a degree off-center in his mouth and needed correcting before it could be produced. Marcus had not mentioned this yet. He had been watching the gap narrow on its own.

"Bates," he said. "Once more."

"*Fertur*." A half-second. "Is carried."

"Again."

Bates said it without the gap this time. Marcus noted this without comment and moved on.

The exercises occupied the rest of the period. He moved between the rows as the class worked, pausing to confirm a form here or redirect an error there. Clara Walsh had finished early and was reviewing her answers with the composed efficiency of someone who was not checking her conclusions so much as reacquainting herself with them. In the back half of the room, the work was slower, more visible — the pencils pausing, the pages turned back. *Arma ferenda sunt.* Weapons must be carried. He looked at it on Donna Packard's paper and thought, not for the first time, that the textbook's choice of example sentences had a certain bluntness to it that the writers probably had not considered before 1941.

The bell. The chairs.

---

He was collecting the exercise papers from the desks when Hannah Pfeiffer came up.

She stayed after class with some regularity — not every week but with a consistency that established it as a pattern rather than an exception. Latin questions most often; German questions occasionally; sometimes both, when she was working through a construction that her two languages were resolving in conflicting ways. She had been in his courses since September of last year, and he had formed the opinion early and not revised it: she approached language as a system of logic rather than a series of items to be memorized, which was the quality that made the work interesting. He had noted this about her and considered the noting sufficient.

She set her notebook on the nearest desk and did not open it. Not a Latin question.

He waited.

"What does *Landsman* mean?" she said. "I think I have the German approximately right — *Landsmann*. Or is it —"

"*Landsmann*," he said. "The plural would be *Landsmänner*, yes." He looked up from the folder. "Where did you hear it?"

She considered for a moment. "One of the men at our farm. I think he said it to himself."

He placed the remaining exercise papers in the folder and closed it. He did this without hurrying.

"The word," he said, "is a term of affiliation. Literally: a person from the same land, the same region. It is not a synonym for *Landsleute* — for countrymen in the national sense — though it can be used loosely that way. Used precisely, it implies a shared *Gegend*." He paused. "Do you know the word?"

"Home district," she said. "Home ground."

"Near enough. It names a specific common origin — not *I am German and you are German*, which is nationality, but *we come from the same part of the world*, which is something more particular. A man from Munich would not naturally call a man from Hamburg his *Landsmann*, or not with any content to it. The word belongs to a smaller geography than the nation."

She was listening with the posture he had identified as her taking-in posture — not yet assembling, simply receiving. He waited to see what she would do with it.

"I see," she said.

"He said it to himself?" he said. "Or to someone?"

"I don't — I'm not sure." A small motion of her hand on the notebook cover. "Maybe to someone. I wasn't close."

"Which farm?"

"Pfeiffer." He knew the Pfeiffer farm the way he knew most things about Coldwater Reach — indirectly, through accumulation, through the town's habit of circulating information about itself through every available channel. The German family on Route 9. The dairy farm, the milk that Lillian picked up Tuesday mornings when the week permitted. The prisoners placed there in October, under the Houlton program — he had heard this at Beal's, at the school, without seeking it.

"It is an interesting word to use in that context," Marcus said.

He did not elaborate. There was more to elaborate on — rather a lot more, in fact — and he set all of it aside, on the other side of the sentence, where it would wait. The word was doing its work without his assistance. Hannah Pfeiffer was seventeen years old and one of his better students and she had asked what was, for her, a vocabulary question, and he had answered it accurately, and that was the scope of what had happened here.

She looked at him for a moment, waiting for further, calculating whether it was coming.

"He's from Franconia," she said.

"I see," Marcus said.

He did.

She gathered her notebook and her coat from the back chair. "Thank you, Mr. Klein."

"Fräulein Pfeiffer."

She went out.

---

He stood by the window.

The schoolyard was grey and still. A line of dead leaves at the east fence. The spruce boughs bending under nothing in particular — the cold air held enough moisture to make the light heavy. Two boys on the stone wall at the far end of the yard, small from this distance, doing something that did not require motion. He looked at the spruce, the boys, the fence, the flat grey sky above the roof of the woodshop, and registered none of it with any precision, which he recognized as a sign that he was looking at something else instead.

He had been not thinking about something for three weeks.

The body had been found in the last days of October — the storm, the breakwater, Hector Brann's boat coming in with the discovery. The town had settled quickly on its preferred account. U-boat, or something connected to submarines, or the relevant period of submarine activity along this coast and what it might have left behind — the exact theory had softened with each retelling until what circulated was a general agreement that this was a wartime thing and the proper people had addressed it. Parsons had come and gone. The record was presumably closed.

Marcus had not offered his version of events. He had been careful, in fact, not to develop his version to the point at which it would require offering, which was a more precise operation than simply keeping quiet.

The detail about the calluses had reached him through Edna Price and then through one of the fishermen at the counter at Beal's — centers of the palms, she had said, though by the time it had moved through two or three people the precision had softened somewhat. He had filed the softened version alongside the version he held, which was somewhat more specific and which had arrived in his mind fully formed and unwanted approximately three days after the body was found: a man in European civilian clothes with palm-center calluses and no papers was not a military operative. Military operatives had documentation, or the specific absence of documentation that constituted its own kind of record. What this man had was no papers — which was a different category entirely, not the no papers of a spy but the no papers of a person who had been traveling by means that did not generate documentation, which was to say by means available to people for whom the documented means were not available.

He had known this since October. He had spent November not following the thought further.

The reason had to do with what following it required.

He knew the logic of wartime displacement from the inside — not from study, not from the reports and lectures that circulated through refugee-aid organizations and emergency committees, but from the specific, physical experience of having been a person who moved through it. His passage from London to Sherbrooke in the summer of 1939 had involved, among other things, arrangements of the unofficial kind: a contact in a city he did not name, a boat that did not advertise its route, a farm somewhere in maritime Canada where he had spent two days in a room he was not registered as occupying. The people who had made these arrangements had not made them for personal advantage. They had made them because the official channels had stopped working for people in his category, and they understood this, and they had built something that worked in the spaces the official channels left empty. He had been grateful. He was still grateful. He had not discussed any of it since, partly from caution and partly because the people who had helped him had helped him in order to get him somewhere, not in order to become part of a story he told at dinner tables in coastal Maine.

He had spent two years in this town working at the project of being a different kind of person: one who taught Latin and German irregular verbs and walked the same three blocks to Elm Street every school day and told Lillian Crow about the Prater in October, a piece at a time, and ate fish chowder at a kitchen table that was warm enough to stay in for as long as the evening allowed. This was not a performance or a disguise. It was where he had arrived after everything that came before it. He valued it, and he had been, since the end of October, protecting it from the question that was waiting for him at the window.

A German prisoner from Franconia.

On the Maine coast. On a farm owned by a family from Feuchtwangen, Bavaria, which was Franconia — the same region, the same regional dialect, the specific vowels that would make a man use the word *Landsmann* and mean it precisely, in the sense of the same ground, the same particular piece of the world, down to a day's walk.

And a body on the breakwater in European civilian clothes with palm-center calluses and no papers, deposited by a three-day blow.

He was not forming an argument. He wanted to be careful with himself about this: what he had was a probable shape, assembled from inadequate evidence, which was not the same as a conclusion, and he was aware of the distinction. The shape suggested — not a spy operation, not the machinery of military intelligence, nothing of that genre — a different logic. The logic of a long chain of people trying to reach people, across distances that were not primarily geographical. A Czech man in transit — he held *Czech* as inference, not fact; the clothing, the route, the kind of person who was still in irregular motion across the Atlantic in the autumn of 1943 — who had known someone, and that someone had known of a farm on the Maine coast where a Bavarian family had come from the right general region of Europe, a family that might have been known to someone somewhere along the chain, or known of, or simply represented a recognizable kind of contact: the immigrant family with the old country still in them, in the right place at the right time, with a farm and a fence and the cold New England distance from official scrutiny.

Not spies. Refugees. The long effort of people who were trying not to be lost.

He did not know this. What he had was the probable shape of it, and the fact that a seventeen-year-old girl asking about a vocabulary word had made the shape visible to him in a way that three weeks of deliberate not-looking had not. He had no obligation to pursue it. He had no means to pursue it and no standing to pursue it and the official record was, by all accounts, closed. He was a man who taught Latin and German at a school in a coastal Maine town and who had been, for two years, the person this particular version of himself required him to be.

He turned away from the window.

The exercise papers were in the folder. Bates's *fertur* was in there, finally said without the gap. He had the afternoon.

---

Ralph Garland was at the table in the teachers' room with the Bangor Daily opened to the sports section. Edna Price was marking tests, red pencil, the focused efficiency she brought to everything she put the pencil to. The gas ring had been on since before seven o'clock that morning and the coffee in the pot had arrived at the particular density of late-session conviction.

Marcus rinsed his cup at the small sink, dried it once on the dish towel, filled it at the pot. He stood by the counter.

Ralph said, without looking up: "Cold today."

"Yes," Marcus said.

He stood by the counter and drank the coffee and thought about the gerundive of obligation, which was the afternoon's subject. *Legendum est.* It must be read. Needing to be read. The construction in which a thing's necessity was built into its grammatical form, inseparable from the name of the thing itself. He thought about how to present this. The passive periphrastic, which was the technical name for the construction, was not a phrase he would open with; he would arrive at the name after the students had the feel of the form, which was the order that worked best. He had the afternoon's preparation in the folder with the exercise papers. He would look at it before the period began.

Edna made a mark on her test and turned to the next one. Ralph turned a page. The gas ring made its small continuous sound.

Marcus finished the coffee and rinsed the cup. He retrieved the folder from the shelf, opened it to the afternoon's notes. *Legendum, legenda, legendum.* The thing that must be done. He read through what he had written on the walk in this morning and made one small change in the margin, a different example sentence, something with less bluntness to it.

The question at the window was still there. He was aware of this in the way you are aware of the contents of a drawer you have just opened and then closed again — it did not require anything of him yet, and there was work to do.

He put on his coat. The afternoon was waiting.
