# Mutter

At five in the morning the barn was warmer than the house, which Hannah had stopped thinking about as fortunate and had started thinking about as simply the way December worked.

She moved through the left row first — Marta, Brunhilde, Sophie, Clara, and then the eight she'd given her own count-names when her mother stopped naming them and her father declined to take up the practice. Her hands knew the sequence without her deciding to follow it. Pull, strip, pull. The particular resistance at the start, the rhythm finding itself after the first thirty seconds. The steam off the cows' backs going up into the dark of the rafters where the lamp couldn't reach. She had hung the lamp on the second hook from the far end because the first hook swung it into her eyes when the drafts came in through the door seam, and she'd learned this in October and moved it and hadn't thought about it since.

She had heard her father's truck at four-thirty. Not the starting of it — she'd slept through that — but the sound of it going out of the yard and turning onto Route 9, the engine going quieter by degrees until she couldn't hear it anymore. By the time she came downstairs the kitchen lamp was low on the table, still burning, and the coffee was warm in the pot and the overnight case was not in the mud room. Twice a year, Rockland. The feed cooperative ran in June and December.

She tied her coat, pulled on her father's old barn coat over it, and went out.

The ice at the base of the east wall was still the same inch it had been since Tuesday. She checked it in passing — not because there was anything to do about it but because she had started checking it and now she checked it; the habit was its own reason. The wind outside was clean and cold and smelled of nothing. December wind sometimes lost its smell when the snowpack hardened enough that there was nothing left to carry. She went back to the cows.

Forty-three minutes for the full row. She dumped the milk pails and cleaned up without rushing and came back through the dark to the house.

---

Upstairs: the hallway smelled of cold wood and the cedar sachet her mother kept in the linen press. Hannah stood in the doorway of her parents' room.

Magda was on her side of the bed, eyes open, looking at the ceiling.

Not asleep. Not distressed in the way that meant the doctor. Hannah had been in this doorway enough times now to know there were three kinds, and this was the third kind — the one that required nothing from anyone but time and company at the edges. Magda's chest rose and fell steadily. Her hands lay flat on the coverlet.

Hannah went to the nightstand and set a cup of water there.

"Ich bin gleich wieder da," she said.

Her mother's eyes did not move. Her chest continued to rise and fall.

Hannah straightened the edge of the coverlet where it had bunched at the pillow and went downstairs to put on her school coat.

---

She sat through English and through history without being called on in either. Had Magda moved. Had she drunk any of the water. Had she gotten up to use the bathroom or to find something to eat.

She had no way to answer any of these questions until four o'clock.

In Latin, toward the end of the period, Marcus wrote a sentence on the board and set the chalk down and waited with his back still turned. He did this — this particular pause, chalk still, back to the class — and Hannah had figured out by October that it was for Michael Bates, who always needed the extra two seconds and knew that he needed them and knew that Marcus knew and that Marcus wasn't going to say so. The pause cost Marcus nothing and cost Michael Bates nothing and nobody had to speak about it. She found this precise in a way she didn't have a better word for.

Marcus turned, said Bates's name in the tone that was directive without being reproachful, and Bates read the form from the board and it was right.

At lunch she sat with Ruth and unwrapped her sandwich and ate half of it and then the inventory in her head came back louder than the sandwich and she folded the other half back into the wax paper.

"Saving it?"

"I'll eat it later."

She let it go. Hannah put the sandwich in her coat pocket and walked through the afternoon.

---

The prisoners were in the icehouse by the time she came up the farm lane — she could see the lamplight through the door seam and hear the low murmur of German from inside, not words, just the rhythm of the language settling in for the evening. Lonzo had already gone. He did that in December when the short days cut the work hours short enough that his signing-out made sense; he'd be back at seven in the morning, the route to the door and the stool and the Springfield rifle as familiar to him as the route to his own kitchen.

She went to the barn.

The afternoon milking was slower. The cows more settled, more inclined to take their time, Hilde in particular requiring three minutes of patience before she'd cooperate.

She came out of the barn and the light was already gone. Not fading — gone. The sky in the west a dark blue above the spruces, no color left in it. She stood a moment in the yard.

The icehouse lamp was still going. She turned and went inside.

---

Upstairs, Magda had not moved. Or nearly not — there was a difference in how her hands lay on the coverlet, one over the other where before they'd been flat, which meant she had shifted at some point in the eight hours Hannah had been gone. The cup of water was untouched.

Hannah went back down and heated the broth — Sunday's broth, the last of the chicken carcass she'd been stretching for a week, careful with the fat because they were down to the last two months of their fat allotment and it was only December. She brought it up on the tray with a folded cloth underneath and sat on the edge of the bed and held the bowl.

Magda did not reach for it. But after a minute she turned her head.

Not toward the window. Toward Hannah.

Hannah kept still. She had learned that the stillness was what mattered — not talking, not asking, just the presence in the room and the bowl of broth and the lamp going on the nightstand. Her mother's face in the lamplight was calm and far and also present, the way someone is present at the edge of sleep: there and alive to it, somewhere behind the eyes.

She put the bowl on the tray and reached for her history textbook, which she'd left at the foot of the bed when she'd come up that morning to check the curtains.

She began to read aloud.

*In the summer of 1787, delegates from twelve of the thirteen states gathered in Philadelphia to revise the Articles of Confederation. The convention met in secret; the windows of the State House were kept shut against the July heat despite the conditions, to prevent speeches from carrying to the street below.*

She read without performing it. Marcus had said, once, that Latin should be read for its sense, not its sound — the meaning moving through the voice, not the voice moving over the meaning. She wasn't sure that was quite the right distinction but she understood what he was getting at. She read about James Madison and his notes. She read about the Great Compromise. She read two full pages and did not look up.

Magda's eyes were not closed.

Then, without planning it and without a pause, she went back to the top of the page and started again in German. Or in the German she had, which was not her father's German — it had the seams of its assembly showing, the words she reached for and the words she substituted when the first ones didn't come.

"Im Sommer des Jahres siebzehnhundert—" she had to count through the compound number carefully, and the counting broke the sentence, but she said it and continued — "versammelten sich die Abgeordneten von zwölf der dreizehn Staaten in Philadelphia." She got stuck on *revise* and said *ändern* and wasn't certain it was right. The Articles of Confederation she left in English because she had no German for it and her mother had been in Maine for twenty-one years and would know the English phrase. "Das Treffen war geheim gehalten. Die Fenster"—she checked the page—"blieben geschlossen, trotz der Hitze."

The German in her mouth had the shape of something she'd carried without knowing she was carrying it. Not her father's formal compounds, not the Sunday church German, not the German of the kitchen after supper when her father talked to himself over the accounts. Something more ordinary. Something that had gotten into her by living alongside it for seventeen years.

Magda's eyes were moving slightly. Tracking the sound.

Hannah read to the bottom of the second page and stopped. The room was quiet. The broth on the tray had cooled. Her mother had not taken it and was not going to take it tonight, which Hannah had known before she came upstairs and which was all right — the being there was its own thing, separate from the eating.

She collected the tray. "Ich mache das Abendessen," she said, and went down.

---

She was at the stove, the white bean soup coming up to a simmer — dried beans from the pantry shelf, onion, the bit of salt pork she'd been saving since last week — when the phrase came to her.

It arrived the way phrases sometimes did: completely, without preliminary, the way a smell arrives when you open a drawer you haven't opened in a long time. She was stirring and heard herself say it under her breath:

*Wenn das Wasser steigt, verlässt man das Dorf.*

She stopped stirring.

It was not her father's German. His sayings were about the worth of things, about the weather, about what a day's work required — practical and specific and never poetic, the German of a man who used language to move information from one place to another. It had the density of something old. Not literature-old, but grandmother-old, the particular weight of a sentence that had been in use in someone's mouth for a long time before it reached her.

She thought it might be Inge. Magda's mother — a woman in the hall photograph, small, upright, grey braid pinned, looking at the camera the way people looked at cameras then, as if the image were a thing to be done correctly. Hannah had met her once, at five or six, and remembered nothing of it except a table edge at eye height and the smell of something baking. Inge had died in 1937 and Hannah had heard it from her mother in two sentences and not again. She might have said the phrase. Or it might have been Magda, before.

Before was a word with no firm edges in Hannah's understanding of her mother. Before the quiet. Before the worst of the quiet. Before whatever it was that had drawn the line between the Magda who had named the cows in German and the Magda who lay in the room upstairs and tracked the sound of language without responding to it.

She said the phrase again, quietly. *Wenn das Wasser steigt, verlässt man das Dorf.*

It was about water and about a village and about leaving. Not about whether to leave. Not about whether it was the right thing or the wrong thing. When the water rises — this is what happens. You leave the village. The sentence had already decided. It was reporting a fact about behavior, the way weather reports the temperature: not a question, not a negotiation.

Her parents had left. They had left Bavaria in 1922 and come to Maine and she had been born here and the stone walls and the barn and the icehouse and the dairy operation were what you built after a leaving. She was seventeen and had grown up inside that construction and had never been told much about the water. Whether it had already been rising when they left. Whether leaving was before the worst of it or during it or after it had gotten as bad as it could get. Whether Magda's grandmother had been right and the leaving was the correct response to the water, or whether the water had followed them somehow.

The soup was ready.

She turned the burner low and set the table for three.

---

Her father's headlights came across the kitchen wall at nine-fifteen. She heard the truck, the kitchen door, his boots on the mat — the particular sound of a man returning from somewhere, a stiffness in the coat that was not their own cold.

He went upstairs without speaking. She heard his voice from the bedroom — brief, in German, two or three sentences she couldn't make out through the floor — and then silence, and then his footsteps on the stairs.

She set his plate in front of him and he sat and ate with the focused attention of someone who had been driving for four hours and had not yet come all the way back. She sat across from him and did not fill the silence.

After a few minutes he set down his fork.

"Sie hatte diese Zustände schon, bevor wir hierher kamen," he said.

Nothing else. He picked up his fork and finished eating.

She did not ask.

She cleared his plate when he was done and ran the hot water. The radio was on the counter, its dial at WLBZ, its face dark. She washed and dried and set the plates back on the shelf. Behind her she could hear her father packing his pipe, the tin of tobacco opening, the small wooden sound of the bowl being knocked clean against his palm.

She could hear the Holsteins from the barn.

Not a distressed sound. The settled sound of animals in winter, the shift of weight, the occasional low of a cow adjusting her position. The farm going on in the dark the way it did, the way it did when she was asleep and when she was awake, the continuous sound of it.

She dried her hands and hung the dish towel on the hook.

The lamp on the table threw its light across the pine. Her father's hands, rough at the knuckle, cupped around the pipe. The windows were blue-black with the clear December night.

She said good night.

The phrase was still in her head at the bottom of the stairs, and at the top, and in the hallway where the cedar smell came off the linen press and the door to her parents' room was dark underneath. She did not yet know where it had come from. She did not know yet what it meant that she couldn't put it down.
