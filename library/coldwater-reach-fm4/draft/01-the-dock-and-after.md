# The Dock, and After

The three-day blow had backed around to the northwest and gone quiet by Tuesday afternoon, but the harbor was still running rough when Eira Bowman left the office at quarter past eleven. She pulled the door shut behind her — the latch was stiff in cold weather; you had to lift the handle as you turned it, a small piece of knowledge the carpenter could have fixed in an afternoon but that she'd left as it was — and stood a moment on the planking outside to let her eyes adjust. The lantern was already lit. No moon. Cloud cover from the Reach to wherever the sky ended. She could hear the fleet working against their lines in the chop and feel the planking underfoot where it moved: that particular rhythm of float and strain that wasn't distress but wasn't settled either.

Standard late check. After every major blow she walked the dock once at night: confirm the Osgood brothers' trap boat, which had dragged on Monday and which she'd reset herself; then the far end, where Bertrand Foss kept three smacks she hadn't liked the look of going into the storm. Three boats. Twenty minutes out, back to the office, then home. She'd been doing this check, or something like it, for eight years — since November of '35, when she'd taken Owen's post because she'd been the one who knew the harbor and the selectmen had run out of other options.

She walked out onto the commercial wharf.

---

The harbor smelled of low tide and diesel and wet rope. The channel was still rough — whitecaps at the mouth, spray coming up off the breakwater blocks and catching in the lantern light before it fell — but inside the water was a heavy chop, nothing that threatened. She walked the planking at a working pace, holding the lantern to her right where it would show her the deck and lines of each vessel she passed.

She checked what she passed: a smack with the new mooring line she'd authorized in September — holding; good — the trap boat that belonged to the Gavel boy, who'd gone to the merchant marine in '42 and left his father paying the slip fee, and two gillnetters in for the season that she noted and passed on. Eight years of this harbor and she'd built the catalog Owen had started and added to it herself: which owners checked their rigging and which left it to luck, where the tide ran strange in a northerly and what happened when it did, which lines were aging out and which owners would argue about it when she told them. Owen had left gaps in the log where his head-knowledge had covered the ground. She'd found most of the gaps in the first two winters and had filled them in, in her own handwriting, in the same columns.

The Osgood trap boat was fine. She held the lantern down, checked the chain at the waterline, and found it right. Good.

She went on toward the far end of the dock.

---

The dock turned at a right angle near the breakwater foot, and she came around this angle — past the last set of cleats, past the empty slip where the MacPherson boat had been before it went for scrap in August — and she saw the two people.

One had their back to her: a figure standing close to the man at the bollard, turned inland, already in motion when she saw them. They went quickly and without noise and were into the dark beyond the streetlight on the wharf road in two seconds. She'd registered: a back, a movement, gone.

The man at the bollard didn't move.

She slowed. She kept walking — stopping at a distance wasn't procedure, and she was going to do this correctly — until she was thirty feet from him, and then she stopped.

He was sitting, or nearly sitting, against the bollard at the dock's end, facing the harbor mouth. She could not see his face from here — he wasn't looking at her; he was watching the water where the mouth opened into the outer harbor — but she could see his coat clearly enough in the lantern light.

His coat was wrong. She registered this before anything else: before the absence of any vessel he might have come from, before the question of the person who had walked inland. The coat was not oilskin. Not the heavy canvas or the wool watch coat she saw on every man who worked this dock in October. The cut of it was European — she could see that in the silhouette, in the fall of the shoulders, in the line of the collar. A traveling coat, a city coat, and it was wet through at the shoulders and back, and it had not been made for a harbor.

He had his hands in his pockets and he was watching the harbor mouth and he had not turned at the lantern.

Unauthorized persons on the commercial dock. Harbor Commission regulation — she'd handled it twice since Pearl Harbor, when the war had given the regulations teeth they'd lacked before. She was the harbormaster. Authorized and obligated to challenge.

She stood there for longer than she needed.

A cold reached her that wasn't the wind off the harbor mouth. It was in her hands, which had nothing wrong with them.

She turned back.

She went to the far smacks and checked Bertrand Foss's first mooring line and found it tight, then the second and third. The chafing gear on the last boat's bow line was rubbing against the dock cleat in a way she'd been watching since September. She pressed it back against the line and held it a moment before releasing it. Then she stood at the seaward edge and looked at the harbor mouth, at the spray still coming white off the breakwater granite even now, this long after the blow had eased, and then she walked back along the dock.

She did not look at the far bollard as she passed.

In the office she put the lantern on the chart table and opened the tide log. She wrote the 11 p.m. gauge reading — she'd taken it before she'd gone out — and wrote the mooring notation: *Osgood trap, all fast. Foss smacks, all fast.* She read what she'd written. She did not write anything else.

The stove had died down while she was out. She considered relighting it and did not. She put on her coat and walked home.

---

The 6 a.m. tide was 4.3 feet above mean low, running out. She'd been at the office before five, lighting the stove, running the gauge, making the first notation of the day. Clear morning after the blow, north breeze, cold. The harbor was going quiet in the way it always did after three days of rough weather — the swell flattening toward a long, low pull, the chop settling. The lobster boats had gone out before first light and she could see their running lights from the office window, already small against the pale.

She wrote a wicking requisition in the requisition book — the stove had been burning crookedly; the wick needed replacing before the end of the month. Owen's handwriting for the first thirty-seven pages of the book, then hers. The changeover was visible if you looked. She had not needed to look since the first year.

She processed the fuel-allocation chart. She made two calls on the field telephone: the sardine cannery about their September billing, the harbor office in Rockland about a slip-fee dispute that had been sitting unanswered since August. The Rockland man was apologetic and slow, and she was writing up the notation for that call when Hector Brann came to the door at half past ten.

He had his hat in his hands.

Hector Brann did not take his hat off. He'd kept it on at the ration board, at the Legion hall, in the general store through the whole of last winter. She'd seen him hold it over his chest during a prayer and put it back on after. She looked up from the chart.

"Morning, Hector."

"Morning, Eira." He was looking past her left shoulder. "Something on the breakwater end. You'll want to see."

She looked at him.

"What kind of something."

"A man," Hector said. "Dead. He's in the seam, ocean side. I think the tide brought him around."

She got her coat from the hook by the door.

---

The breakwater ran two hundred feet from the north shore out to the harbor mouth — granite blocks from the 1890s, green-black below the waterline, gray-brown above, algae-striped at the tidal bands. The ocean side took the full run of weather. In a blow the spray came over the blocks clean. Even now, the storm over, the swell was still running heavy enough at the harbor mouth to break white at the granite's foot.

The man had come in on the tide and settled in the joint between two blocks, in the seam, face up. His coat was spread around him in the way moving water arranges things. He had not arranged himself.

Eira picked her way out over the granite.

She put her hand to his throat because that was procedure. No pulse. He was cold in a way that was past accounting for with weather and exposure time — a finished cold. His face had gone the color she'd seen twice before and had no interest in seeing again: the blue-white at the lips and jaw, the flatness of the closed eyes.

Somewhere between thirty-five and fifty. Medium build. Several days of beard.

His coat was the coat from the dock.

She looked at his hands.

The calluses were wrong. She knew what a fisherman's hands looked like, and a farmer's, and a cannery worker's — the base of the fingers, the inner edge of the palm, where rope or shovel or the packing table worked across years. These calluses were in the centers of the palms. She didn't know what work put them there. It wasn't any work she recognized from the harbor, from any harbor she'd been in.

No papers visible. She didn't search the coat — that was for the Coast Guard — but she looked at what was accessible: nothing in the outer pocket, no wallet bulge at the breast.

She went back across the breakwater to the office and called the Coast Guard station on the field telephone.

---

While she waited she went back out and stood at the foot of the breakwater and looked at him from there. He was visible clearly enough: the coat bright against the gray granite, the harbor mouth behind him with its running swell. A pair of terns worked the inside water, and the sound of them came and went on the wind. The harbor was the harbor — the chop, the diesel smell, the sardine cannery boat at the far dock. He was small at this distance. Everything was the size it was supposed to be.

She'd known it was him when Hector said *a man*. Before that, even — if she were being precise about it, though she was not required to be precise about it to anyone.

She went back inside and stood at the chart table and did not open the log.

---

The lieutenant's name was Parsons — Arthur Parsons, his Coast Guard uniform correctly pressed despite the drive down from the station, a man of forty or so with the manner of someone who had done this before and intended to do it properly. He had a clipboard. He asked to see the harbor log from the previous night.

She gave it to him.

He read it without hurrying. The 11 p.m. gauge reading. The mooring notation. The columns ruled through where there was nothing to enter.

"Anything unusual last night?" he said. "Any vessels in the harbor you didn't recognize? Anyone on the docks who shouldn't have been?"

"The fleet's in," Eira said. "Storm's been over. Nothing I need to note."

She heard those words come out of her. They were accurate and they were not complete.

Parsons nodded and wrote on his clipboard. He asked about the body — position, whether anything had been moved. She gave him the exact description: the seam between the blocks, the face-up orientation, the coat, the hands. He walked out to the breakwater with one of his men and she watched through the window as he looked at the coat, then at the hands, and noted both without comment.

He came back.

"Drowning appears to be the cause," he said. "Storm exposure, possibly — we'll know more after the examination." He looked at her. "Do you know the man?"

"No."

"Seen him before? On the dock, at the fuel float?"

"No."

"Papers on the body?"

"None visible. I didn't search."

"That's right, you wouldn't." He made a note. He paused. "Anyone you know of who might have been on the water last night, or in the past few nights? Unauthorized vessel traffic?"

"No," Eira said.

Parsons held her gaze a moment, then wrote it down. "We'll be in touch." He turned toward the door and back. "The man who found him?"

"Hector Brann. He lobsters out of here. The *Irene M.*, green hull, third slip."

Parsons wrote the name and thanked her. He directed his men to the stretcher and she watched them carry it across the breakwater granite and the wharf planking and load it into their vehicle. The engine turned over. She listened to it go up the wharf road and onto the main road, and then the sound diminished and was gone.

The breakwater was empty.

She went back inside.

---

The tide log was open to Tuesday. Her own handwriting: the 23:00 gauge reading, the 00:10 check, the mooring notations for the Osgood boat and Foss's smacks. The columns ruled through where there was nothing to record. Complete, legible, accurate in what it contained.

Nothing about the far dock. Nothing about a figure walking inland with their back turned before she'd had time to see their face. Nothing about a coat that was wrong for the harbor, or thirty feet of planking between her lantern and a man watching the harbor mouth, or the length of time she'd stood there, or what she'd decided in that time.

She had the pen in her hand without remembering picking it up.

The tide log lay open on Owen's chart table, in the office that had been his office and was hers — the stove going properly now, the room warming in the way it did when it had been burning long enough — and she was reading what she had written and what she had not written.

Outside, the harbor went on.
