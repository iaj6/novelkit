# After Eira

He was past the school road corner before he became aware of his own pace.

Slow — not the brisk deliberate pace he made in the mornings, when there was weather to account for and a furnace to arrive before Henry had to bank it up. The unhurried kind, the kind that had nothing to do with time remaining or legs that were tired. Three of his afternoon students were ahead of him on the road and moving fast, the way students moved at three-thirty — a purposeful erasure of the school day behind them — and he watched them recede and then did not watch them, his eyes dropping to the road surface, the late May afternoon doing whatever it liked above him. On the south wind the harbor was present: brine and low-tide mud, the flat particular note the channel made at half-ebb, the smell that was this town's smell and that he had been breathing for two years and two months and had not yet stopped noticing, which he considered, on balance, a small mercy.

He had three blocks. The students had gone around a corner. The road was empty in front of him.

---

The name had been with him since the rowing dock.

Jan Václav Horak — she had said it the way she said things, directly, without ornament, and he had heard the name and the nationality and the last known location and understood at once what they added up to, which was not quite knowledge but was the shape that knowledge would take if it arrived. He had been working toward this shape for several weeks before she gave him the name. The name itself had come to him first through the ordinary channels of a small town: the post office counter, the Beal's store noon hour, the way information moved in a place where everyone picked up their own mail. He had heard "Czech national" and "Halifax" in April, in the same week, from two separate sources neither of which connected them to anything, and he had noted the conjunction and set it aside and then found it not staying aside.

The Pfeiffer farm.

He had not gone looking for the connection. It was the kind of thing that arrived if you had enough pieces in a certain order and the patience — or the training — not to reach for an explanation before the pieces were actually pointing to one. Albrecht Pfeiffer: Feuchtwangen, eastern Franconia, the emigration of 1922. A farming family from the part of Franconia that sat closest to the old Bohemian border — fifty, sixty kilometers of hilly country between the German-speaking towns and the Czech, with generations of agricultural contact across it, the kind of contact that produced not a common language but a willingness to meet in one. If a displaced Czech national had come south from Halifax in the autumn of 1943, seeking a connection in the German-émigré community scattered thinly along the Maine coast, the geography would have pointed to the Pfeiffer farm. Not certainly. Not by any proof that could be brought before anyone. But the direction of probability was not ambiguous, and he had learned, over a long time, to regard the direction of probability as a thing worth knowing.

And Karl Reinmar.

Karl was from the same border country. Marcus had heard it before Hannah confirmed it — in the flat vowels and the particular way the final consonants were handled, the specific regional texture that distinguished eastern Franconian from the Bavarian spoken further south. The dialect that shaded toward Czech in its rhythms, the vowel sounds that had been rubbing against Czech for centuries across that border. He had taught enough German in enough rooms to know the difference between a speaker from Munich and a speaker from fifty kilometers from the old Bohemia line.

He walked past the lilac hedge at the Whitmore property — bloomed out by now, the flowers going brown at their edges, the branch-ends heavy and nodding — and crossed to the far side of the road where the afternoon shadow ran cooler.

What he had: a Czech national, last known Halifax, October 1942, found dead on a breakwater twelve months later. A figure on the dock in the dark, speaking Czech, walking inland after. A probable network of the kind that had moved people south through Nova Scotia and into Maine during the worst of the Atlantic years — not documented, not organized in any formal sense, arranged person to person along chains of people who knew people, the links often having nothing in common but geography and a willingness to be a link. And a farm, three miles inland, run by a man from eastern Franconia who had been in this country for twenty-one years and who stopped in the middle of a field when a prisoner with the same vowels spoke to him in a language they both, by the routes of their separate lives, had come to carry.

What he did not have: any certainty that Albrecht Pfeiffer and Jan Václav Horak had ever met. Any knowledge of whether Albrecht was the connection Horak had sought, or whether the figure on the dock — the person who had been there in the dark and had walked inland before Eira reached the water — was Albrecht, or someone else, or someone Marcus had no access to. Any understanding of why Horak was dead.

He held this for a moment, on the road, in the ordinary light. The probable shape, honest about its own edges.

Someone had been on the dock with Horak. She had heard one voice — his. She had not mentioned a second, which did not mean there had not been one, only that the second had kept quiet, or that she had not been close enough. The second figure had walked inland. Whether they walked because they saw her coming, or because the exchange was complete, or because something had gone wrong and walking was the only available response — he could not determine, and he had trained himself, over a long period of being in situations that required it, not to fill the space between what was probable and what was known with anything more substantial than the acknowledgment of the space.

---

He had, over his years of movement, developed what he considered a working ethic about this kind of knowledge.

It was not dangerous — not to anyone living, not in this particular summer of the war. The coast was quiet; the patrols had relaxed by degrees since 1942; whoever had moved Jan Václav Horak south from Halifax had no reason to fear anything from Marcus Klein's private assembly of a probable picture. It was a burden in the other sense: it involved people who had not invited him into their lives and who were already carrying enough.

Albrecht Pfeiffer was a naturalized citizen with a dairy operation and a wife who had not been well. Whatever he had done or not done, he had done it for reasons that belonged to a life Marcus did not have access to — a life that had started in Bavaria and crossed an ocean and arrived at a red barn in Maine and had remained, for twenty years, in a kind of half-exile that Marcus recognized in its quality without claiming equivalence. Albrecht Pfeiffer did not need Marcus to take a probable picture and walk it up the road to anyone's office.

Karl Reinmar was nineteen years old. He had been a soldier since he was seventeen. He had been a prisoner for more than a year. He had done nothing in Coldwater Reach except work a field and speak his regional German to an old man from the same border country, which was not nothing — it was, in fact, something Marcus understood precisely and would not have had undone — but which was the furthest possible thing from anything that required attention from Marcus Klein.

And Eira Bowman had made her own choices. He recognized them as judgment rather than negligence: the judgment of someone who understood what the harbor log required and what it did not, and who had answered for what she had seen in the way she considered honest. She had not asked for his opinion about this, and she would not. She did not need him to carry her portion of this further than she had chosen to carry it herself.

He crossed the next street. One block left.

---

There was something else. He had not examined it directly in some months and he was not going to examine it directly on the public road, not today, possibly not anywhere; there were things that could be held in a particular internal drawer, accessed only when the drawer was essential, and this was one of them. He was aware of it — the awareness itself was sufficient.

The picture he had assembled was a picture he had been inside.

He had been the person moving through an unofficial channel. He had been the one who needed to reach a city where he knew no one and who arrived at an address given by a contact who had a contact, who was handed a room for two nights and a name for the next city and a time of departure and then not handed anything more, because the person who handed it over did not know anything more. Paris to London, 1938. London to a boat that was not advertising its route, 1939. He had been the person who relied on the chain's holding and had not examined the chain's individual links too closely, because examination was one of the things that made chains break.

The chain had held. He was here — walking three blocks home in late May, in a town that had not yet fully decided how it felt about him, carrying someone else's probable picture.

Horak's had not held.

He came to Elm Street and turned toward the house.

---

Lillian was at the kitchen stove when he came in. The smell reached him before he was all the way through the door: real coffee, not the ersatz barley mixture that had been the household's standard since the early part of the spring. He stopped in the doorway.

"Where did you find this," he said.

She turned from the stove. She was smiling. "Ellis. Off the supply boat. He found two bags at the back of the locker and said he couldn't account for how they'd gotten there — said we might as well have them."

"Ellis," Marcus said, "is a man of considerable virtue."

"He is." She lifted the pot. "Sit down."

He hung his coat and hat on the hook and came to the table. Henry was there with the evening edition — the Bangor paper, which came to Elm Street two days behind schedule most weeks and three days behind on weeks when the delivery arrangement found something to object to. He had folded it to the inside pages. Marcus pulled out his chair.

"Later than usual," Henry said. This was not a complaint; Henry did not complain about the household's schedule.

"I had a few things to finish at the school," Marcus said. He considered for a moment. "The harbormaster came by as well. There was a matter with the rowing dock."

Lillian set a cup in front of him — the china one she brought out for occasions she had decided deserved it. The smell of the coffee was more particular than anything else in the house — a specific dark insistence, the real thing distinguishing itself from its substitutes without any effort at all.

"The school has a rowing club?" she said.

"It does," Marcus said. "Or it did, apparently, at some point." He picked up the cup. "The mooring registration has been expired since 1941. Two shells at the dock and no one using them."

Henry lowered the paper. "Three of the rowers enlisted," he said, with the measured evenness he used for facts he had already considered from several angles. "Hennessey's boy. The two Vance brothers."

They were quiet for a moment — the three of them, the expired tag, the two shells, the year and a half.

"The athletics director will need to come to the office before June," Marcus said. "There is also a compliance matter — four inches, a height question. Mrs. Bowman mentioned it." He drank. "I will tell Folger."

Henry returned to the paper.

---

After a little while Henry turned to the back pages — the tide tables, which Marcus had observed him reading every evening for two years without quite understanding what pleasure it gave him, though he suspected the pleasure had something to do with the certainty of them, the way they told you what the water was going to do before it did it — and said, without looking up: "The news is better from the Mediterranean. The Anzio situation."

He turned the paper to show the headline.

Marcus read it. The Anzio beachhead had been static since January, the winter dispatches full of grid coordinates and tactical stalemates that had stopped meaning much to anyone reading them in a Maine kitchen. The line was moving now. "They will be in Rome before June," Marcus said.

"That's what the paper is saying."

"In this case I believe they may be right."

Lillian sat down with her own cup. They talked about the Mediterranean for a few minutes — what it implied for the invasion everyone knew was coming but that had not yet come, whether the push north out of Anzio meant the German defensive line in Italy was genuinely broken or only interrupted. It was the conversation of people who had been listening to the same broadcast each evening, assembling from dispatches what they could, which was never quite enough and was always more than nothing.

---

After a while Henry went back to the tide tables and Lillian got up to see to the stove. Marcus held his cup. The coffee was almost gone.

"I had an interesting thing today," he said. He had not planned to say it — it arrived the way small true things arrived when you were sitting in a kitchen with decent coffee and people who were not requiring anything of you.

Lillian turned from the stove. Henry lowered the paper two inches.

"A student," Marcus said. "In the afternoon section. The passage they were working on concerned soldiers far from home — a standard enough passage, and not a difficult one, but it required a specific word. The Latin word for the longing a person feels for someone absent. Not sadness generally. Not grief in the broad sense. The particular ache that belongs to absence itself — to the missing of a specific person from a specific place."

Henry set the paper down.

"Most students reach for 'tristitia,'" Marcus said, "which means sadness, or 'dolor,' which means pain — grief, suffering, the word that covers a great deal of ground. Either is usable. Neither is the right word." He turned the cup slightly in both hands. "This student wrote 'desiderium.'"

Lillian said: "Which is the one that means the other thing."

"Yes. Exactly the other thing. The precise word for the precise feeling." He was quiet a moment. "I had not expected it. I found it —" He considered. "Genuinely pleasing."

She said: "That's the best part of teaching, I think. When they find the right one."

He thought about it. The afternoon classroom, the pencil moving across the page, the student looking up to see if it was correct.

"Yes," he said. "I think it is."

---

Henry refolded the paper. Lillian got up for the pot. The kitchen held its warmth — the day's heat collected under the low ceiling, the west window still carrying the evening light, the slow generous light of late May that went on longer than was useful, longer than the workday had any claim on.

Marcus sat with his hands around the empty cup.

Outside, somewhere down toward the harbor, a screen door banged once in the wind off the water. Then it was quiet.
