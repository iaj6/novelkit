# The Unfinished Letter

The school had released him for the summer at the end of June, and by the second week of July Marcus had not yet found the right orientation to days without schedule.

Not that the days were empty. He had a list — *Virgil to page ninety; the third decade of Livy; irregular verb forms for the fall section's second year; composition notes he had promised himself he would work through before September*. He had been at the desk at eight in the morning for three days running, and the list was present on the desk, and by ten he was at the window, having done none of it.

Henry left for the school each morning at his usual time. The summer maintenance schedule was apparently as various and demanding as the school-year one — more so, Henry said, since in summer you could finally get into the boiler room without the fire burning and do what needed doing. He left at a quarter to seven with his thermos and his key ring, and Marcus heard the door and came down for his own breakfast and was back upstairs before the kitchen had fully settled, and then the day was his.

Lillian was mostly in the garden. The tomatoes were coming in.

The window faced the street. Elm Street in July was not the same street as in October — the trees were full, the light moved differently through them, a filtered green-gold that arrived at the desk at an angle that changed across the morning. Two boys went past on bicycles at nine o'clock, arguing over something neither of them was conceding. A woman with a market basket turned the corner at nine-thirty. The harbor smell was there, as it always was — three blocks, the south wind — but summer muted it; the cold months concentrated it to something penetrant and specific, but July gave it back as simple salt.

He had the composition paper out by nine.

---

The difficulty was not beginning the letter. He knew who Professor Stern was — had known him since the autumn of 1940, when the Emergency Committee was still accommodating itself to the scale of what it would be asked to do, and Stern had been one of the men on the routing committee who moved names from the waiting list to the housing list to the placement list. Specifically, he had been the one who placed Marcus's name in front of the Coldwater High School hiring notice in the spring of 1942, when the previous Latin instructor's enlistment left the board with a classroom and no one for it. They exchanged letters occasionally. The last one had come in November — a brief report that the displaced scholars network was still processing applications, that two men from Prague had been placed at small colleges in Ohio, that the backlog in Halifax remained substantial. It was the kind of letter that said, between its lines, that the work continued, that the people moving through the channels were still moving or still trying to, that Stern himself was still at his desk in Cambridge six mornings a week doing what he could with what he had.

Marcus had begun a reply in December and not finished it. Then the school board review in February had taken whatever attention he might have given to correspondence, and then there had been the spring term, and then there had been May.

What he sat down to write this morning was not the December letter.

He drew the date at the upper right — July 10, 1944. He wrote the salutation. Then he considered, for what was probably longer than it needed, what the first sentence of the letter should be and what claim it would be making.

The first paragraph was not difficult. It was the official paragraph: Jan Václav Horak, Czechoslovak national, displaced person, last known location Halifax, October 1942. Found on the breakwater of Coldwater Reach, Maine, October 27, 1943. Official ruling amended April 1944: accidental drowning consistent with storm exposure, identification supplied. This was the public record, and to set it down was not to commit himself to any interpretation of it.

He wrote it. Five lines. He reread it, changed one phrase from *having arrived in Coldwater Reach* to *having apparently reached the Coldwater Reach area*, which was more precise and also more honest about what the record knew and did not know. Then he moved to the second paragraph.

The second paragraph was where he had to be careful — which was the first sign that *careful* might be the wrong principle for it.

What he wanted to write — what the letter needed, if it was to be a letter that could produce a useful reply from a man of Stern's knowledge — was an account of how he understood the probable route that had brought Horak to a breakwater in coastal Maine. Not what he could prove. What he understood, from his own understanding of how such movements worked: the way a name was passed from one contact to the next along a chain that no single person held entire, the way a farm in the right county of the right state could be a stopping point that appeared in no official record, the way a route that began with a legitimate transit document ended, sometimes, in an arrangement that was not quite anything the paperwork anticipated. He had been inside it — Paris to London in 1938 along a line of contacts whose names he had been given in the form of short descriptions only; London to a boat in 1939 that was not advertising its itinerary, a crossing that had ended in Sherbrooke, which had been the wrong end, the locked end, the one he had not anticipated and that had cost him two years.

He wrote the second paragraph. He wrote what he understood about the probable shape of such a route without naming specific people, specific farms, specific vessels. He wrote as a man of some structural knowledge who wished to provide context. He was accurate. The paragraph was precise. And when he reread it, what he found was that a reader of Stern's experience — which was to say, a reader who had spent four years moving people through exactly the kind of channel Marcus was describing — would read the paragraph and understand at once that Marcus was not an observer of these arrangements but a man who had traveled inside them himself.

He sat with this for a moment.

He was not afraid of Stern knowing. Stern almost certainly already knew — the Emergency Committee's files on Marcus's own case were not thin, and a man who read files as part of his daily work would have assembled the shape of the transit from the paperwork before he ever wrote about the Coldwater position in 1942. What Stern would read between the lines of the second paragraph was not new information. It was confirmation.

The third paragraph was the actual question: was Jan Václav Horak known to any of the networks moving displaced persons through the Halifax-to-New England corridor in 1942 or 1943? Had anyone expected him at a particular point along that route? Was there a family that had not received word?

He wrote it. Four sentences, asking for only what could reasonably be shared.

He read the third paragraph again, and what he saw was this: if he sent the letter, and if Stern made inquiries, those inquiries would begin with the Halifax end — the last known location — and work toward the point of terminus, which was Coldwater Reach. Which meant they would work toward whoever had been the contact node in Coldwater Reach. He did not know with certainty who that had been. He had assembled a probable picture in May, walking home along Elm Street in the late-afternoon light, and the picture's most likely answer was the Pfeiffer farm, three miles inland on Route 9 — a German-American family, naturalized citizens, with four German prisoners on their property and a wife who had retreated from the world in ways that predated the war and would outlast it. A family already living at the margin of a social position that had grown considerably more precarious since December 1941.

If Stern inquired, and if the inquiry reached Albrecht Pfeiffer, what light it would bring would not be the kind Albrecht could manage without difficulty. He was a careful man who had held things carefully for a long time, and the light that came with official inquiry — even the unofficial inquiry of a well-meaning professor — was not careful. It spread. It did not stop where one asked it to stop.

Marcus set the pen down. He looked at the street.

There was also Eira Bowman. She had been at the commercial dock the night of October 26, 1943, and she had told him this in May at the school rowing dock — given it to him across a distance that was not quite close enough to be overheard and not quite far enough to require raising her voice. She had given him the language fragment she could not place, and he had placed it for her. Czech, probably Czech. She had received this without adding anything to what she had already said. She had trusted him with something by saying it. He did not know what a letter to Cambridge would do to her position if an inquiry found its way back to this harbor, to this dock, to the night of October 26. He did not know that it would. He also did not know that it would not.

The fourth paragraph was simpler: he had written in a private capacity; he understood the sensitivity of the question; he asked only for what could be responsibly shared. He was reachable at the Elm Street address through September, and at the school from September forward.

He read all four paragraphs through.

The letter filled one and a half pages of composition paper, his handwriting smaller than usual because the questions required a certain density to sit on the page without spilling. It said what it needed to say. It asked a question that deserved asking — about a man who had died in October, whose name had arrived nine months later, whose family, if there was family, had not been told. The letter was correct.

He considered whether the precision of the second paragraph could be removed without losing the letter's usefulness, and took up the pen and tried — a fifth paragraph, a revision, working around the network knowledge with more circumspect language, gesturing at the probable route rather than indicating it. He wrote seven sentences, read them, crossed out three, rewrote them. What he had was a paragraph that a man in Stern's position would read as the work of a well-meaning outsider offering his vague impressions, which was not what Stern needed and was not what Jan Václav Horak deserved. The precision was what made the letter worth sending. Removing it removed that.

He crossed out the fifth paragraph.

He put the letter — four paragraphs, the fifth crossed out, one and a half pages of composition paper — in the right-hand drawer of the desk and closed the drawer.

---

He walked at two o'clock. The July town moved at its July pace — slower than April, but a different kind of slow; summer's was deliberate where winter's had been reluctant. He went north on Elm Street and then turned along the harbor road, the long way, which had better shade. Two lobster boats were in at dock and two still out. He saw Abel Holt working his stern post from a distance and raised a hand; Holt raised one back without looking up.

The harbor smell was the summer version of itself: the diesel still present but the cold brine softened to something almost mild, the kind of smell that in October would sharpen back to something that got into one's coat. He walked the length of the commercial wharf. At the near end he could see the breakwater running two hundred feet out, the granite gray in the flat afternoon light, the algae line at the waterline dark and consistent. He did not walk out to the seaward end. He continued along the wharf road and turned back at the fish house.

He crossed Elm Street and went up the walk.

---

Lillian had made a cold supper, as she had been doing on warm evenings since the middle of June: tomatoes from the garden, sliced, with salt; bread; a wedge of cheese; water poured and waiting at his place. The tomatoes had come in that afternoon, and they were still warm from the field, the skin tight, and she said that they were ahead of schedule — usually she wasn't cutting them until the second week of August, but the warm June had pushed everything forward. He said they were excellent. He meant it.

Henry came in at six-thirty with his work bag and the day's Bangor Daily News. He set the paper on the corner of the table and read the front page standing up, the way he had done for as long as Marcus had lived in the house, then folded it and said something about weather building in the northwest, which usually meant a change in the next two days. They sat. Lillian poured from the water pitcher. Henry took the bread, and they ate in the comfortable quiet of an evening in the third year of a household arrangement that had found its settled shape some time ago.

Marcus did not mention the letter. He ate his tomatoes and his bread and his cheese, and the kitchen was the kitchen on an early July evening — the window open to the garden, the south wind carrying what it carried, the Bangor Daily News folded on its corner with whatever the day had sent down the coast. The light went slow and golden through the west window the way it went in July, and in July only, and Henry and Lillian were at the table, and supper was supper, and the harbor was three blocks south in the dark that had not quite arrived yet.

He went upstairs after. He read four pages of the Aeneid — the second half of Book Four, which he had been working through at no particular pace since June — and then closed the book and lay in the narrow bed with the window open to the street.

The drawer was closed. The question inside it was the same question it had been at nine in the morning, unaltered by the afternoon — still present in the way that some questions were present not because they awaited an answer but because they marked a position: *this is what you know; this is what you cannot do with it; this is what remains*. There were people he had come to have some small obligation toward — not heroically, not in the language of obligation, but in the ordinary way that came from inhabiting the same rooms, the same winter, the same harbor road for two and a half years. He did not want to be the thing that lit them up. He had chosen, when it was a choice, not to be.

Somewhere down toward the harbor a door closed. The July insects had begun to thin, and what filled the dark outside the window was closer to silence than it had been a month ago. He turned on his side.

He slept.
