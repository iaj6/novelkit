# Albrecht and Karl

The fence post she wanted was at the north corner, where the property line turned and the drainage from the upper field ran along the stone wall. She had taken the ratchet and a coil of wire from the hook in the barn, in case the staples had pulled through, and was walking the fence line in the gray of the morning before the full light came over the maple ridge to the east. The frost was gone from the ground by the second week of April but the posts were still working themselves in places — she had found three heaved since March, one of them badly enough that the cedar had split at the base, and she had replaced it herself on a Saturday while her father was in the south field. This section was the last one she hadn't walked since the thaw.

May. The smell of the turned earth was in everything — in the air, in her coat, in the fold of her scarf. Albrecht had been plowing the north field since Monday, the team working east to west across the upper slope and stopping at the drainage dip, working around the low place where the clay held snowmelt into the third week of spring. She could see the rows from the lane, the dark parallel cuts going nearly black where the soil was wet, the topsoil thin over the clay subsoil in the north corner and impossible to do much about except build the planted rows higher, above where the water sat. Her father had done it this way every spring she had been old enough to watch.

She walked the fence line. The cedar posts were still holding in the upper section; she pressed each one with her shoulder and found them true. At the north corner she crouched to look at the bottom wire where the stone wall met the post — the junction was always where the frost pushed hardest, lifting the base of the stone and tilting the post with it — and found it solid. She ran her hand along the staples anyway. The wire was tight.

She heard voices before she stood up.

German.

Her father's voice was the lower one, and she knew it immediately — the register of it, the particular quality of how he shaped a sentence in his first language, which was different from his English in a way she had always felt rather than been able to name. As if German were where the sentence went when it was finished; as if English was where it went when it was only getting somewhere. She stayed crouched at the fence post.

The voices were coming from the other side of the barn, around the corner where the field fence ran along the east wall. She had heard them from thirty feet away, which meant they were not being quiet. Two men in the morning, talking about the work.

She straightened slowly and walked back along the fence line toward the barn. At the corner there was a gap where two boards had been replaced after the ice damage the previous winter, the new planking not quite flush with the old, and through the gap she could see a section of the field fence and beyond it the near plot, the dark rows her father had turned. Her father was at the fence with his hands on the top rail. Karl was on the field side of it, perhaps three feet away.

She stopped.

They were speaking the same German. That was what struck her first — not that they were speaking, but that the language was the same in the way that mattered, the way that two instruments tuned to each other were the same. Her father's dialect had always been the particular German of home, of the kitchen and the dinner table, and she had never heard it exactly replicated outside those walls. Now it was coming from both sides of the fence rail. The vowels deep and rounded in the same places. The way the final consonants were handled. She could not have described it in linguistic terms the way Marcus could, but she could feel it — the fit between the two voices, the way they were working in the same grammar.

Karl was speaking. She caught: *der Boden* — the soil — and then *Wasser*, water, and *zu nass*, too wet. Her father said something she couldn't make out because he was turned partly away from her. She could see the profile of him, the set of his shoulders. He was listening with the quality she had learned to read: fully, without interrupting, turning the information over.

Karl crouched and picked up a handful of the field earth and held it out on his palm so her father could see the color, the clot of it, the way it kept its shape when the pressure eased. Not offered — just shown. Her father looked at it.

Karl spoke again. She caught *die Rücken* — the ridges — and watched him make a gesture with his free hand, the palm cutting flat through the air at an angle. The same gesture repeated with the hand tilted higher. She had watched her father work the north-corner rows since she was six, building the planted beds up with the hoe so the roots would sit above where the water sat in the clay. He set the earth back down on the fence post. He said something more, and she caught *bei uns* — with us, where we come from — and *der Frühling*, spring, and a phrase about the timing of it, the March ground. How early you could start, if the clay was this kind of clay. What you watched for.

Her father was nodding slowly.

They went on like that for a few minutes — she couldn't have said exactly how long, because she had stopped thinking about time and was only watching the way two men talked when they were talking about something they both knew from the ground up. Karl asked a question; her father answered. Her father pointed toward the north end of the near field, the worst of the drainage dip, and Karl looked where he was pointing. There was something easy in it that she did not have a word for. Not friendship — she had seen her father with friends, with the neighbors at the feed cooperative meetings, with the men from church who came sometimes in summer. It was the ease of two people who had the same information in their hands and were exchanging it without having to explain where the information came from.

Then Karl said something that included a place name.

Two syllables. A proper noun — she could tell by the shape it made in the sentence, the way it stood apart from the words around it. She didn't know it. She had learned, since October, the few Franconian place names she could attach to what she knew about her father's first country — Feuchtwangen, Ansbach, Dinkelsbühl, names she had pulled from the grammar and the atlas in the school library. This was not one of them.

Her father let go of the fence rail.

He didn't step back. He simply released the rail, slowly, and put both hands into his coat pockets. He looked at Karl. Not the sharp stop of October, not the mid-swing pause she had counted to two seconds and not been able to forget. This was a slower stillness.

He said something very quietly. She could not hear what. Just the register of it — low, almost flat.

Karl nodded once.

They stood at the fence. Ten seconds, no more. Then her father looked back toward the field and said something that was probably about the drainage, something practical. Karl went back to his row. Her father walked toward the far end of the north plot without looking back.

She waited at the barn wall until she could no longer hear either of them moving. Then she went back the way she had come.

---

The milking took until a quarter to seven. Twenty-two cows, the same twenty-two, and she knew every one of them — knew which would shift when she came up on the left side and which needed a hand on the flank before she could start, knew the sounds each one made in the sequence of filling the pail. She worked from right to left along the row, same as always, the pail moving, the smell of the warm animals around her.

She thought about her father's hands leaving the fence rail.

She had been watching her father for two years with a particular attention — not deliberately, always, but the way you watched something that had become weather, that told you things about what was coming whether you chose to read it or not. She had come to know the quality of his attention when he was receiving news he had already partly assembled. It was different from the quality of his attention when something was genuinely new. New things made him slower, more careful, the way he was with unfamiliar equipment. Already-assembled things made him still in a different way. He absorbed them into what he already had; the stillness was the sound of the pieces finding their places.

Karl had named a place and her father had gone still in the way that said: I know this already, or I have been waiting for it, or it is the thing that connects what I knew to what I now cannot pretend not to know. She had felt that stillness herself, in Marcus's classroom, in April, when he had said the valley east of Feuchtwangen toward the Bohemian hills, and the geography of her father's country had suddenly become a specific shape she had not known she was standing inside.

Her father knew exactly where Karl was from.

Not Franconia in the general way that she had understood since October, since the phrase that stopped him mid-swing in the field and the fifty kilometers she had mapped in her head afterward. More specific than that. The town Karl had named — the two syllables she didn't know — had a meaning to her father that showed in his hands leaving the fence rail, in the particular quiet of what he had said. He had the name already.

She didn't know what that shape was. She had pieces of it — the same pieces she had been accumulating since October, since the first *Danke* through the fence in the fall, since the mid-swing pause, since the bird in her coat pocket. She had the shape of something, outlined by what she couldn't see rather than by what she could.

*Die Erde hier ist schwer.*

The phrase came up again, the way it kept doing. She had stopped being able to place it exactly — had lost the seam between the first time she'd thought it and all the times since. It was in the grammar from Bavaria, she thought. One of the field-prayers in the back pages, the ones she'd never asked her father to explain because she didn't know if they were prayers or something else. The earth here is heavy. The clay in the low places, going dark after rain, keeping its cold into May. The rows Karl had been showing her father how to build — the raised beds, the same technique her father had been using in the north corner for as long as she could remember, the technique that turned out to have a name in the same language as the soil it was meant to answer.

She worked along the row. The pail rang against the stanchion where the fourth cow shifted.

She thought: her father has something in common with Karl Reinmar that he has not told her. He has not told her because it is his to keep. She did not know what to do with this — whether to want to know, whether the wanting to know was the thing she needed to examine more carefully than the knowledge itself. She had been taught, more by weather than by instruction, that there were things her parents carried that were not hers to enter. The weight of the first country. The particular sorrow that had no name in English and that her father did not name in German either, in front of her.

She moved to the last cow. Outside the barn the light had gone from gray to pale gold.

---

When she came back across the yard, pails in hand, she looked up at the kitchen window.

Magda was there.

Standing at the window with a dishcloth in her hands — not drying anything, just holding it — and looking out toward the field. Not toward the yard, not toward Hannah coming across with the milk, but toward the far end of the north plot, where her father was working the second pass of the plow. Hannah stopped in the yard. She could see the side of her mother's face: the stillness of it, the quality of attention she had turned toward the field.

She couldn't tell if Magda had been at the window since before the gray light came, if she had seen the conversation at the fence, if she was standing there now because of what she had seen or because she had not seen it and didn't know. She couldn't tell if the dishcloth in her mother's hands was the evidence of something interrupted or something never started. She couldn't read Magda's face from here, at this distance — the face her mother wore in the kitchen, the one she could sometimes read, was not available to her from the yard.

Magda looked down at the cloth. Then she turned from the window and was gone back inside.

Hannah stood in the yard for a moment. Then she went to the pump to wash her hands and brought the milk in.

---

Lonzo came at seven, the green truck up Route 9 in the morning light, the Springfield in the rack behind his seat, the paper folded on the dash. He came across the yard at his usual pace and tipped his chin at Hannah. She said morning. He said morning.

He looked at the field, where her father was turning at the far end. She watched him look. There was nothing in his face that told her what he had seen or when he had arrived; he had the quality, she had noticed over many months, of a man who took in information the way a tide gauge took in water — registering, marking, not commenting. He went and retrieved his stool from the barn and set it at the gate and opened his paper. The prisoners were coming out of the icehouse — the three older ones first, then Karl behind them, Karl with his collar up and his hands still in his coat pockets though the morning was warming.

Lonzo watched them cross the yard the way he always watched: the count that wasn't counting, the assessment that looked like morning habit.

Hannah watched him for a moment and then stopped. She didn't know what she wanted to know. Whether he had been there early enough to see. Whether it mattered if he had. Whether anything about the morning at the fence had been the kind of thing Lonzo put in a report, or the kind of thing he had decided, months ago, was not that kind of thing.

She went inside to change her boots.

---

She walked the road to school with mud on her boots, the same clay mud from the north field, dark and dense, the kind that held its shape. The air had the particular quality of a May morning that was not yet sure of itself — cool still in the shade of the maples along the road, warmer in the open stretches where the sun had been working since first light. She walked past the south field where the Holsteins were grazing along the fence line, their brown eyes following her with the patient indifference of animals who had learned that she came and went and always came back.

She was thinking about the conversation at the fence. Not thinking, exactly — turning it in her hands the way you turned something in the dark to understand its shape.

Her father had heard a town name and gone still.

She didn't have the rest of it. She had the shape of the silence, the ten seconds at the fence, the hands going into the coat pockets, the quiet sentence she hadn't been able to hear. She had Magda at the kitchen window, looking toward the field. She had twenty-two cows and a school a quarter-mile down the road and mud on her boots from a field two men had just compared to a field in Franconia thirty years and thirty-five hundred miles away.

The morning opened around her. The crossroads, the church, the school building ahead where Marcus would be unlocking the door at this hour, the same hour he unlocked it every morning. The ordinary hours of the ordinary day proceeding through themselves, her father at his second pass in the north field, Karl in the far rows, Lonzo at the gate with his paper, Magda somewhere in the house with her dishcloth and her window.

The light on the road was the light of a morning that was going to be warm by noon, that was going to smell like turned earth and new growth all the way to the harbor and back. *Die Erde hier ist schwer.* She carried a little of it on her boots, going to school.

She went on.
