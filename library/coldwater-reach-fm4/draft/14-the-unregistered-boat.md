# The Unregistered Boat

The spring mooring assignments ran fourteen pages in the ledger that year — more changes than any season since she had taken the post. She worked them in order, north dock first, going south. The order was Owen's. He had invented it in his first year and she had inherited it with the ledger, and she saw no reason to alter a system that worked.

April fourteenth. Cold still, but a different cold — the active kind, the kind that came with the smell of the water back in it. The harbor had been open since mid-March, the inner-cove ice gone in the second week, and the fleet was returning from the winter moorings and the drydock yards and in some cases from the decisions made over the winter about whether to return at all.

This season she had two deletions.

Robert Cates, berth fourteen. His boat the *Idabelle* had been registered at fourteen since the summer of 1938. He had shipped as mate on a merchant vessel the previous September — she had the letter from the company, which was the way it was done, the company notifying the harbor office as a matter of form. His wife had written separately in February to say the berth could go. Eira had kept it open through the winter. She did that when a man left: hold through the season, then decide. Now it was April and she reassigned berth fourteen to Archie Lunt of Tenants Harbor, one of two men who had come down from Tenants Harbor when the Price fish house folded in February.

Harold Vose, berth nine. She had processed his papers in December and mailed the original registration certificate back to his mother in Searsport, because his mother was the named next of kin and there was no wife and no partner and no one else on the form. She had held berth nine empty through the winter. She marked it available and did not reassign it yet. She would hold it through the spring in case a buyer materialized.

The three additions: Archie Lunt, Eddie Lunt his brother — no relation to the Lunts from Port Clyde — and Roy Tremblay from Spruce Head, vouched for by Elias Hollis, whose judgment she trusted for this purpose. Tremblay would have berth nine provisionally; she would do the hull inspection on the fifteenth.

She finished the mooring assignments by half past eight.

Then the depth soundings. The District office had sent the winter survey in February with three corrections. She worked them into the tide log and entered a penciled flag on the chart for the shoal building at the south entrance, up two feet since 1942.

She worked until ten, then took her coat and walked the dock.

She did this most mornings — the north berths and then south along the commercial wharf, the whole fleet in order. The harbor in April had a specific quality she had been noticing for eight years: the cold and the activity at once, the season not yet settled, the fleet reassembling itself after the long quiet. Today there was a supply tender at the outer dock and the Lunt boats rigging at the far berths and someone on the commercial wharf with a hand truck. She walked through it without hurrying.

She knew every registered boat in the harbor by sight. She could name each one going along the dock — by hull shape, by waterline, by the way it sat when loaded or empty, by the specific shade of the topsides after years of paint and weather. She had learned this from Owen and she had learned it again through her own watching.

---

At dock post seven, which she had left open pending reassignment, there was a lobster boat she did not know.

She stopped and looked at it. Small — twenty-six feet, perhaps twenty-seven. An older hull, the topsides faded from something that had been red, a standard work design, the lines of nothing particular. The kind of hull you saw from one end of the coast to the other without being able to place the builder. It sat at the post with its lines double-tied, which meant someone had secured it properly and not simply run a line through and walked away.

She went to the stern and read the registration number.

She went back to the office and looked it up. The Maine registry — main volume, then the 1944 supplement. Not there.

She went back to the dock.

Hector Brann was at berth twelve, coiling line. He had been the one to find the body on the breakwater in October — she had thought about that the first time she'd renewed his berth since, and not since. He was a steady tenant and he didn't ask questions about what wasn't his business.

She asked him whose boat was at seven.

He straightened and looked down the dock. "Think it's someone from Owls Head," he said. "Been there a few days."

She thanked him.

Back in the office she opened the second drawer and found the Owls Head harbor contact list. She kept lists from four coastal offices — Knox County, the Owls Head harbor, Rockland, Vinalhaven — a practice Owen had started and she had maintained, updating when the offices changed personnel or the phone numbers changed. The Owls Head list ran three typewritten pages, alphabetical by registration number. She went through it twice.

Not there.

She took out the tide log.

*Unregistered vessel, dock 7. First observed morning of 14 April 1944. Registration number not in Maine registry (main volume or 1944 supplement) or Owls Head harbor contact list. Inquiry to be sent to District office, Portland. —E.B.*

She put the tide log back on the desk and drafted the inquiry: two paragraphs, the registration number, the date of first observation, the dock location, a request for identification or nearest-office follow-up. She sealed it in an official envelope and put it in the mail pouch for the three-o'clock run to the Birch Street post office.

In the afternoon she did the hull inspection on Tremblay's boat. Registration in order, hull sound, some cosmetic work needed on the topsides but nothing structural. She countersigned his certificate and filed the copy.

On the morning of April sixteenth she walked the fleet.

Dock post seven was empty.

The double-tied lines were gone with the boat. She stood at the post for a moment. The water under it was the same harbor water it had been yesterday and the day before. No mark on the post that would last beyond the week.

She went back to the office.

*Unregistered vessel, dock 7. Departed approximately April 15 or 16, unseen. Inquiry to District office posted April 14. No response received.*

She did not expect a response before May. Possibly June.

She noted it and turned to the day's other work.

---

The official mail came on Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays, dropped by the post office boy who had been doing the harbor rounds since before the war. It came on April sixteenth while the tide log was still open on her desk.

Four items: the weekly District bulletin, a supply order confirmation, the spring fuel allocation form, and a letter from the Coast Guard regional office in Portland.

She set the bulletin and the form on the left side of the desk. She opened the supply confirmation and checked it against the pending order list. She opened the Portland letter last.

Two paragraphs on official stationery. Dated April 12. Addressed to the Harbormaster's Office, Coldwater Reach, Maine, attention Harbormaster.

*In re: October 1943 recovered remains — Coldwater Reach harbor, follow-up communication.*

She read it through.

*Through liaison with Allied civilian records, this office has received identification of the remains recovered October 28, 1943. The individual has been identified as Jan Václav Horak, Czechoslovak national, status: displaced person. Last known location: Halifax, Nova Scotia, October 1942. No next of kin information is available through current records. The official record is herewith amended to reflect this identification. The classification of cause of death is unchanged: accidental drowning consistent with storm exposure. No further investigation is anticipated at this time. This communication is for your records.*

She read it again.

Jan Václav Horak.

She folded the letter and set it on the chart table and sat down.

Czech. In Halifax in October of 1942. On the Coldwater breakwater in October of 1943. Between those two facts there was a year of movement that the letter did not account for and that she had no account of from any other source.

The office was quiet. The stove had burned down while she was at the desk — she could hear that it had gone quiet, the iron no longer ticking — but she did not get up. Outside, the harbor was going about its midmorning work. Someone at the outer dock. The Lunt boats coming in early from the south. The tide log open to April 16 on the desk.

She knew his hands. She had been at the dock that morning in October when the Coast Guard lieutenant had done the inventory — everything that was on the body and everything that wasn't — and she had looked at the hands because that was one of the things you looked at, if you were trying to understand what kind of man you were dealing with. The calluses were in the centers of the palms, not at the base of the fingers. Not a fisherman's hands. Not a farmer's or a cannery man's. She had noted the coat he wore, the European cut of it, the quality of the wool, the way it was not built for this water or this cold. She had noted the several days' growth of beard.

She had known he was not from anywhere near Coldwater Reach.

She had his name now.

Jan Václav Horak. Czechoslovak national. Halifax in October 1942.

Halifax was a port. You could come into Halifax by certain routes — without the right papers, or with papers that weren't quite right, or with the kind of contacts that didn't appear in any official record — and from Halifax you could move south along the coast by routes that had no file in any District office. She knew this from Varnum. Last December, at the quarterly meeting in Portland, after the fuel allocation was settled and the room had relaxed: Varnum had used the phrase *refugee crossings*. More of it than people thought, he had said. Known, though. A kind of thing that happened.

She had ridden back to Coldwater along Route 1 in the dark with that phrase and the approved fuel figures and two cups of coffee. She had held the phrase beside what she had seen on the dock on the night of October twenty-sixth: the man at the far bollard, eleven in the evening, a second figure in the shadow between the fish house and the net shed. She had stood for a moment at the dock head and looked, and then she had gone back to the office and written in the log: *2300: mooring check completed, fleet secure.*

She had not entered the man. She had not entered the second figure. She had not entered what the two of them had appeared to be doing at the far bollard at that hour, or what the shape of it had looked like in the dark.

Jan Václav Horak. She had not known his name when she wrote that entry, and she had not gone back to annotate it, and now the Coast Guard regional office had sent her two paragraphs of official stationery and she had his name, and the log still read *fleet secure*.

She looked at the letter.

No next of kin. She turned that over. His name had been in the records somewhere — in some Allied civilian file, in some registry of displaced persons that someone in some office had access to, because they had found it and sent her this letter in April of 1944, and the name was here now. But no next of kin. No one the Allied civilian records had associated with him. He had moved through the records without leaving a person who would be notified.

He had been a displaced person. That was the status the letter gave him. Displaced from where to what, it did not say.

She tried to trace the year between Halifax and this breakwater. The ports where you could stop, moving south. The Bay of Fundy, the Passamaquoddy coast, the islands — she knew them all as harbors, as navigational facts, as names on the chart she looked at every working day. She could see the route in theory. She could not see who would have been helping him along it, or whether anyone had been, or what it had cost him and whoever was in that shadow at the far bollard to move him to this dock and to what happened after.

She had seen enough, that October night, to have entered something. She had not entered it.

Owen had left a gap in 1934 and named it. He had written *not entered at time; entering here as complete account* and he had made the record complete. She had thought about that in January, standing at the chart table with his 1934 log and her 1943 log open side by side, his October pages and her October pages. She had closed the 1943 log and not returned to it.

Jan Václav Horak.

Czech. Halifax. Displaced. No next of kin. Dead on this breakwater at the end of October, in the water twelve to thirty-six hours before Hector Brann found him on the granite seam on the ocean side. She had done the arithmetic at the time, standing in the dock office with the lieutenant's report in front of her, and the window had put him in the water between midnight and two in the morning on October 27, 1943. The night after she had seen him at the dock.

She knew more now than she had known in October. In December. She knew his name and his country and the city where he had last been registered, a year before she had seen him at the far bollard with whatever papers he did not have on him when the Coast Guard lieutenant turned out his pockets.

She did not know what he had been moving toward. She did not know what he had been running from. She did not know whose hands had put him in the water or whether his had been the only ones.

She had a name.

The chart table under her hands was Owen's — the same one he had worked at, the surface worn at the corners where you rested your forearms when you leaned over the charts. Outside the window the harbor was going about the business of mid-April. The fleet resuming. The season coming back.

She looked at the letter.

Jan Václav Horak.
