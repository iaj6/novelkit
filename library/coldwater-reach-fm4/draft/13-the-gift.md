# The Gift

The cold in her room was the March kind — not the dead weight of winter that had been sitting in the walls since December, but something different now, thinner at the edges, the light coming in gray at the window with a quality it hadn't had last week. She lay still for a moment. Eighteen. The word arrived, turned itself over once, and went quiet. The barn would want the same morning she had given it every morning.

She dressed and went downstairs.

She had milked before full light, as she had most mornings since she turned fourteen and her father's back started. The Holsteins in the dark, their breathing, the pail going full in the silence, the cold that was barn-cold now rather than outside-cold. By the time she came back inside and washed, the kitchen windows were beginning to show the gray-white of early morning. She went upstairs to change out of the barn clothes. There was no school until nine.

The smell reached her from the stairwell.

Butter, going warm in a pan. And underneath it — flour, eggs, the particular rich complexity that was not bread and not anything else, that she had not smelled on an ordinary morning since November, since the last time she had asked directly and her mother had made them and not looked up from the bowl the whole time.

She stopped at the bottom of the stairs.

Her mother was at the stove. Not at the table with her coffee, not standing at the window the way she had been standing most mornings for four months, but at the stove with the mixing bowl on the counter and the iron pan already going and her back to the room in the angle that meant she was paying attention to something in front of her. She had braided her hair. Hannah had not noticed this until now — the dark braid with its gray at the temples, pinned at the neck, the straight line of her mother's shoulders, occupied.

The coffeepot was on the back element. The stove was hot.

Hannah came in and sat down at the table.

Her father came in from the barn five minutes later, the cold still on his coat. He looked at the stove, then at Hannah. *Herzlichen Glückwunsch*, he said — she would hear it all day at school from Ruth and the others, in English, but here it was in German, the way her father said anything that mattered — and he set one hand briefly on her shoulder, then was pulling out his chair and reaching for the coffee.

Magda brought the pancakes to the table in two batches. On Hannah's plate she put two extra without speaking and went back to the stove for the coffeepot. She did not look at Hannah when she set the plate down. She was already turning.

They ate. Her father ate with the focused quiet he brought to early mornings — contained, his attention on the food and the sounds of the farm coming through the window: one of the Holsteins shifting in the far stall, the drip from the barn roof where the frost was already lifting. Hannah ate her pancakes and the two extra ones and drank her coffee. The plates were the German ones with the blue border that her mother had wrapped in her good dresses to cross the Atlantic and that had not, in twenty-one years on this table, developed a single crack.

She was aware of her mother at the stove, moving between the pan and the counter, then standing still. The way she was aware of the drip from the barn roof — something present, something that had not been there before, or had been but differently.

After breakfast she washed the dishes. Pan, mixing bowl, the plates, her father's cup, her own. Water from the pot she had put on before they ate. She set the dishes in the rack and dried her hands on the towel beside the sink.

She did not say anything about the pancakes.

---

The south fence post had been heaving since February — the frost and thaw cycling it out of true until there was daylight between the post and the stone wall on the east side, the wire gone slack on three runs. She took the bar and the wire gauge and went out in the late morning, after the frost had lifted enough to work with.

The south field still had some gray in it at the far end, but the stone walls were clear at the tops now, the snow gone in the last week, the first bare stone of the year coming back. The mud smell was in everything: the field, the yard, the air itself, the particular March smell that was cold and damp and carried something green underneath it that wasn't visible yet but was there.

The prisoners were at the north fence line. She could see them from across the farm — four figures at the far end, two with the manure spreader and two working the stone. Lonzo was at the gate with the Springfield and the Bangor Daily News across his knee.

She worked the corner post back into true. The ground took the bar at the surface and resisted six inches down — the March ground, the winter still in it underneath. She leaned into the reset and felt the post come level, drove the brace pin, tested it with her hand. Then she went along the fence checking the wire, running the gauge along each strand: first run, second run, third. The work was clean — her hands, the bar, the gauge, the wire. The Holsteins were out in the south field behind her now, moving in the slow good-tempered way they had when there was some warmth in the air.

The east face of the corner wall had heaved at the top, the frost lifting the stones until a narrow shelf opened in the shadow between the wall and the fence post. She was running the gauge along the base when she saw it.

It was back in the shadow where the heaved stone made the shelf. Not visible from the house. Not visible from the gate where Lonzo sat. You would not find it unless you were working this exact section, on this side of the fence, at this height.

She reached in and took it out.

Birch — pale, the bark stripped, the surface worked smooth with a small blade. About the length of her longest finger. A bird, clearly: the head angled slightly down, the wings folded close to the sides, the tail cut to a clean notch. Not fine work — the grain of the wood showed through, and there was a place on the breast where the blade had gone slightly wrong and been corrected rather than hidden, a faint ridge you could feel with your thumb. But the shape was right. The angle of the head was right. Someone had taken time with it, had come back to it more than once; you could tell where the work had continued past what the shape strictly required.

She knew whose hands had made it before she had finished the thought.

She looked up. Lonzo was at the gate with his back to her, the newspaper across his knee. The figures at the far end of the farm were the figures at the far end of the farm.

She put the bird in her coat pocket.

It fit against her palm when she closed her hand — the notched tail at the base of her thumb, the smooth birch against her fingers, the small weight of it. She stood with her hand in her pocket. The mud smell was in the air. The Holsteins moved behind her.

Then she went back to checking the wire.

---

Ruth met her at the corner of Bridge Street with things to say about Mabel Pearce's birthday last Saturday and the ration allocation at Beal's and a question about the Latin for Tuesday's quiz. Hannah answered the question about the Latin. The bird was in her coat pocket and she was aware of it against her palm the whole walk over — the notched tail, the corrected place on the breast.

She had not told Ruth.

In Latin, Marcus was explaining the ablative absolute for the third day running. He had written it on the board in his precise handwriting — letters close together, almost no slant — and was going through the same example sentence: *the city having been taken, the soldiers returned*. Hannah had understood the ablative absolute since October; he had explained it well the first time and she had carried it away whole. She watched him work through the parsing now. Half the class was still doing it the long way, treating every absolute construction as a subordinate clause, arriving at the same place by a harder route. She understood why he kept returning.

The bird was in her coat on the hook in the hallway.

Karl's hands had made this. It sat in her coat pocket and did not ask what she meant to do with it. She was eighteen today; this was the fact of the morning, and the pancakes, and this, and none of them was the same fact but they were all here in the same day.

She held this for the rest of Latin and into the German period, where Marcus had them reading a passage from a short novel about a family in Swabia. She read her passage aloud when called on and Marcus nodded. The German was easy — easier than the Latin, always, though she would not have said so in this room.

During study hall she went out to the hallway for her Latin notes and found the bird instead. She stood in the empty hall and held it a moment in her closed hand: the notched tail, the corrected breast, the small smooth weight of it. Then she found her Latin notes and went back to her desk and put her hand flat on the desk.

She did not take the bird out again. She did not show it to Ruth. She did not mention it to Marcus after class, when Ruth stayed behind to ask about the quiz. She walked home alone — her right hand in her coat pocket, the bird against her palm — and she did not take it out on the walk either.

It was hers.

---

The evening milking ran late — the cows had been outside all afternoon and were slow to come in, and she worked down the line in the good kerosene light. The barn smelled of warm animal and mud tracked in from the field and the milk going into the pails. The Holsteins were settled now, the quiet they had after a day with some warmth in it, their bodies turned into the stalls, the near ones watching her move past with the mild attention of animals who knew the order of things and found it sufficient.

From the barn door she could see the light in the icehouse window. A yellow rectangle in the dark fifty yards off, the curtain drawn, the light behind it steady. The prisoners would have been in since five, when Lonzo brought them in and locked the door and filled out his report and drove off down Route 9.

She stood in the doorway with her hands on the frame. The mud smell was in the air, sharp and cold both, the March smell at dusk. The early dark had come on, the sky to the west still holding a gray-blue line above the treeline, everything else already black. Karl was in there, in the icehouse.

She went back in and finished the milking and came through the barn to check the doors.

She came into the house and hung her coat on the hook by the door. Her mother was at the stove; her father was at the table with the farm account book, making his entries. Hannah took off her boots and washed her hands at the sink.

The kitchen was warm. Outside, the dark had come on fully now, the frost already returning to the ground, the mud smell going quiet under it. The thaw smell would be there again tomorrow, and the frost again tomorrow night, back and forth until the weather finally settled on one or the other — a few more weeks of this.

The coat was on the hook.
The bird was in the pocket.
She set the table for supper.
