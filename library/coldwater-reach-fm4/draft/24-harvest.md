# Harvest

The corn came in the last week of September. Twenty-two rows of it, the stalks dried to their final paleness and the ears heavy on the stem, and the work of it the same work it always was: pull the ear, snap the stem, walk the row. The prisoners worked two rows at a time, Karl and Fischer on the near side and Brunner and Krebs on the far. Lonzo sat at the field's edge with his rifle across his knees and the Bangor Daily News folded open in his lap, turned to whatever page he was on.

By the end of the second day she had the figure in her mind without meaning to count it: Karl was moving faster than the other three.

She had noticed it the way she noticed a slow cow before she could have named which one — without counting, and then with counting, and then the count confirming what she already knew. The other three moved with the efficient, conserving pace of men in their thirties and forties who had enough experience of physical work to husband it. Karl moved differently. He pulled the ears and walked the rows in a rhythm that was not rushing but was not conserving either. It was the rhythm of a man going at the work because the work was all he had to go at, and this was, in a way she could not quite close the door on, a thing she recognized.

She watched Lonzo once while she was watching Karl. His eyes had gone past the paper to the far row where Karl was working, and he was wearing the expression she had come to know over twelve months as the expression he wore when he was looking at a thing he had already thought about and filed. Not suspicion. Not concern.

She went back to her own work.

The corn took two and a half days. The late hay after that, two days of baling in the September warmth, the kind of work where everything smells of cut grass and diesel and you move from the field to the baler to the barn without stopping. Then the kitchen garden — the squash that had been ready since August and needed the last pulling before the first hard frost, the potatoes still in the ground, the few remaining tomatoes off the vines. Magda did most of the garden in her measured way, moving from the kitchen to the rows and back without hurry. Hannah did the heavy pulling. Her father moved between the garden and the barn and the field in the route he had worked for thirty years.

The icehouse light was on past nine o'clock those nights. She had noticed this before harvest, but in harvest it was more visible because the days were long and physical and should have put everyone to bed early. Karl, she thought, with Lonzo's newspapers. She had seen him through the small window some evenings — the paper spread across his knees, angled toward the lamp, lips moving slightly in the careful way of someone who was reading in a language he had built one word at a time. She had known about the newspapers since winter. She had not thought about what to do with knowing until spring.

---

The fence meetings had been going since June — or since before June, if she was honest, which she mostly was not.

Not every day. Only when the work put her at the south fence section and Karl at the corresponding part of his field, which happened twice a week, sometimes three times, sometimes once. She had stopped tracking it as an event. She had also not told anyone about it, which was its own kind of tracking.

They talked about the farm, mostly. The soil in the south section, which drained toward the lower field in wet years and could sit waterlogged into May if the winter was bad. The squash, which needed a particular pulling so the vine didn't rip. What cover crop was worth putting in after the corn stubble turned under — she favored winter rye, and so did he, though he had reasons that were different from her father's reasons and she had found this interesting. He knew farming; he knew a continental kind that had rules overlapping with the Pfeiffer farm's rules without being identical, and the differences were worth discussing with someone who understood the question from the inside.

In German he was fully himself. The Franconian vowels she had been hearing from her father since before she could name them were the vowels Karl spoke, and in German there was less labor on either side. She could not follow everything — there were idioms and agricultural terms that she would have needed more years of Albrecht's country to have. But she could follow the shape, and she could make herself understood, and there were conversations in German that she came back from feeling as though she had been somewhere. Not somewhere with Karl specifically. Somewhere where the language was working the way it was built to work.

She carried the carved bird in her coat pocket most days. She had recognized this as a habit — a thing she was doing without deciding to do it — sometime in July, when she'd reached for her penknife and closed her hand around the bird instead. It was small and smooth, worn at the edges in the way of a thing that had been handled often; she had been handling it without noticing. She had not given it back. The opportunity had not arisen, she told herself. She understood this was not everything that was true, and she let the rest of it lie.

What she told herself the fence meetings were: two agricultural people, a fence between them, comparing notes on what they both knew. This was accurate. It left things out.

---

The conversation with her father happened on a Tuesday evening in early October, the harvest nearly done, the kitchen garden pulled, the whole farm in its post-harvest settling. She was at the sink with the supper dishes when she heard him come in.

She knew his sounds as well as she knew any sound in the house — the particular pull of the chair from the table, the way his left boot came down differently from his right on the kitchen boards. She kept her hands in the water.

He sat for a moment without speaking. The kitchen had the smell of the end of a harvest day: soil, woodsmoke, the warmth from supper still in the room. The October light through the window was already at its early evening quality, pale and going.

"*Du solltest vorsichtig sein*," he said.

She kept her hands in the water. She knew, when the phrase arrived, that she had been waiting for it or something like it; she had known without letting herself know in the way she'd become practiced at.

"*Womit?*" she said.

"*Am Zaun.*"

She turned and looked at him. He was looking at the table — at the grain of it, or at a water ring two summers old that she had been meaning to work out with oil. His hands were flat on the wood. The right little finger with its old bend. She could see from the set of his shoulders that this was not something he was working up to. This was the speaking of it.

"*Ich bin nicht wütend*," he said. "*Ich sage dir, vorsichtig zu sein.*"

She looked at his hands for a moment.

"*Hast du etwas zu Lonzo gesagt?*" she asked.

"*Nein.*" A breath, quiet. "*Und ich habe nicht vor, es zu tun.*" He looked up then — not at her, at the wall past her left shoulder, and then briefly at her. "*Aber Lonzo ist nicht der Einzige, der an diesem Hof vorbeiläuft.*"

She turned back to the dishes.

He got up after another moment and went to his chair in the other room. She heard the German paper unfold — it came Thursdays, had come Thursdays for as long as she could remember.

She finished the dishes and dried her hands. She did not go into the other room. She went upstairs, and through the floor she could hear him turning the pages, and she lay on the bed looking at the ceiling and did not think about what she was going to do, because she did not know yet, and the not-knowing was its own country that she was not yet ready to leave.

---

In the morning she watched Lonzo more carefully than she had since the early months.

He arrived at seven the way he always arrived: truck on Route 9, the gravel, the stool and the rifle and the paper under his arm. Twelve months of mornings in this same configuration.

She was in the near field with the corn stubble, running the disk harrow behind the mule, breaking the stalks into the turned earth. The work required enough attention that she did not have to be obvious about where she was looking.

He opened the paper to the Pacific section first. She knew this habit; she had watched it since February, when he'd mentioned his son was on a ship she'd never heard of. Pacific first, then the front page, then the agriculture column he read without fail and sometimes showed her if he found something relevant. He was reading now and not looking at anything besides the page.

She ran two passes.

He looked up in the middle of the second — she was at the far end of the field, eighty feet of turned earth between them, and when his eyes came up from the paper she was looking at him.

He nodded.

She nodded.

He went back to the paper.

She turned the mule at the row end and came back.

She could not read the nod. She could not tell, from twelve months of observation, whether she was in his report or not in his report — whether what he filed at the end of each day at the Coastal Patrol office said *four prisoners, seven hours, no incidents*, the way it always said, or whether it said something else in some column she could not see from here. She did not know what it would mean to be in the report. She did not know what it meant not to be.

What she knew — not because she had been told, but because she was not stupid and she had been watching for a long time — was that Lonzo had looked at the south fence section and made a decision about it. Some kind of decision, its terms unknown to her. She was inside the decision. She did not know what she had been decided about.

Lonzo had a son in the Pacific who didn't write often enough. He read that section of the paper first. In December his left hand gave him trouble. He had been sitting on this stool since October of 1943 and he had not, as far as she knew, said anything to anyone.

That was what she knew. She ran the last pass.

---

The following afternoon she was at the south fence with the compost cart, spreading the last load before the freeze came and made the ground too hard to work it in. Fork work — heavy and rhythmic, the kind that didn't require thought and so let her mind go to wherever it went on its own.

Karl came to that section from the far end of his row.

He had been working the stubble turnover, the same work she had done on her side in the morning, and he moved toward the fence the way he moved through everything — steadily, without hurrying. She kept her fork moving. When she looked up he was there.

He did not speak. She did not speak.

The distance was the fence, which was not very far.

The first hard cold front had come through the night before, and the October air had the quality she recognized as the turning: clear and with an edge to it, the shadows long at this hour, the field reading differently than it had for four months. The whole quality of the light changed at some point in September — she had never been able to say exactly when — and now the south fence looked like October instead of August, the same fence with a different face.

She drove the fork into the compost pile and let it rest.

He was looking at the turned earth on his side. She was looking at the turned earth on hers.

She did not know what she was going to do. This was, for the moment, the only thing she was sure was true.
