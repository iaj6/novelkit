# Chapter 12 — The School Board

**POV**: Marcus Klein  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Season / time**: February 1944  
**Target words**: ~2,800  
**Chapter shape**: Single scene (the school board meeting, with brief before and after)  
**Ending mode**: Resolved-beat  
**Opening texture**: Set-piece

---

## One-line summary

Three school board members formally review Marcus's hire; he makes his case with the precision of a man who has done this before; the board votes to continue his employment.

---

## Setting

The Legion hall, Coldwater Reach — the rationing board offices by day, the school board meeting space when needed. February: the cold is at its worst, the streets empty by 7 p.m. The meeting is at 7:30.

---

## Goal / pressure

Marcus must defend his position. The goal is clear: keep the job. The pressure is the particular pressure of a man who has had his legitimacy questioned by institutional bodies before — in Vienna, in Paris, in Sherbrooke, in Cambridge. He knows how to do this. The question is whether knowing how to do this is a comfort or a cost.

---

## Action beats

**Before the meeting:**
1. Henry tells Marcus two days in advance that the board meeting is happening. Someone — Marcus doesn't ask who — filed a complaint about the German teacher with the selectmen, and it was passed to the school board. Henry's information is precise: three board members, not the full board; likely a formal review, not a termination vote; Marcus's credentials are on file.
2. Marcus spends those two days preparing the way he prepares a lesson: he considers the probable objections and constructs responses. He gathers his documents — the Emergency Committee letter, his faculty credentials from Vienna (pre-1938 letterhead, which he knows may cut two ways), the letter from Cambridge that secured this position, his Maine teaching certificate.
3. Henry says: "I'll be there." Marcus says he doesn't need a witness. Henry says: "I know." He comes anyway.

**The meeting:**
4. The Legion hall. Three board members: Dwight Hatch (a selectman, in his fifties, cautious rather than hostile), Agnes Vane (a school parent, whose concern is articulate but not specific), and Gordon Sears (a hardware man, who doesn't understand why a German teacher is necessary at all and says so).
5. Marcus sits at the table. Henry is in a chair along the wall — not at the table. This is the correct positioning; Henry knows it and so does Marcus.
6. Dwight Hatch opens: they've had questions from some parents about the "current situation" and the propriety of a German-language instructor whose origins are in a country currently at war with the United States.
7. Marcus responds: he was born in Austria, which was annexed against its will; he fled Austria in 1936, before the annexation, because of circumstances (he uses this word) that made remaining impossible. He has been on the rolls of the Emergency Committee since 1940. He has taught Latin and German at this school since March 1942. He produced a list of his students' academic outcomes.
8. Agnes Vane: "We understand your history, Mr. Klein. But the parents' concern is about—" She doesn't complete the sentence. Marcus waits. She says: "The language itself. At this particular time."
9. Marcus: "I teach German to American students. They will need to speak to German-speaking people in the future, whether in military capacity or otherwise. The value of that instruction does not decrease because the language is currently the enemy's language. It increases."
10. Gordon Sears: "You understand our position." Marcus says: "I understand it perfectly." He means both interpretations of that sentence.
11. The board votes 3-0 to continue his employment. The meeting is over in forty minutes.

**After the meeting:**
12. Marcus and Henry walk out together. They don't speak until they are three blocks from the Legion hall. The night is clear and very cold.
13. Henry says: "You were very calm." Marcus says: "I have had some practice." Henry says: "I know." A beat. Henry says: "I have too."
14. They walk the rest of the way home without speaking. The Crows' kitchen is warm. Lillian has left the stove on.

---

## Outcome / resolved-beat

His position is retained. The practical question resolves — he has his job, his classroom, his students. What resolves less cleanly is the cost of knowing how to do this: the particular practiced calm of a man who has defended his right to exist in institutional rooms. Henry's "I have too" is the chapter's most load-bearing sentence, and neither of them says anything more about it.

---

## New question raised

How many times can a person defend their right to occupy a room before the defense becomes the whole of them? And: Henry, who said "I have too" — what rooms has he had to do this in, and what did it cost?

---

## Continuity notes

- School board members named: Dwight Hatch, Agnes Vane, Gordon Sears. Functional characters for this chapter; may be referenced but don't need to recur in person.
- Marcus's credentials: Emergency Committee letter; Vienna university credentials dated before 1938; Cambridge letter; Maine teaching certificate. The 1938 letterhead detail is present but not elaborated.
- Henry's role: witness only, by design. His positioning in the room (at the wall, not the table) is his own choice and he knows why it's right. The prose should render this as Henry's knowledge, not as pathos.
- "I understand it perfectly" — both meanings are available (I understand your concern; I understand your position as prejudice). Marcus does not gloss this.
- The chapter is a set-piece: formal, compressed, the outcome never really in doubt (his papers are in order; the school needs him) but the procedural weight is real.
- Henry and Marcus walking home: the warmth of their parallel without naming the parallel. "I have too" is enough.
