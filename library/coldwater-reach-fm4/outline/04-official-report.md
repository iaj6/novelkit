# Chapter 04 — Official Report

**POV**: Eira Bowman  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Season / time**: Early November 1943  
**Target words**: ~2,800  
**Chapter shape**: Bottle (primarily the harbormaster's office; one brief exterior)  
**Ending mode**: Resolved-beat  
**Opening texture**: Routine-procedure

---

## One-line summary

Lt. Parsons takes Eira's formal statement; the official record closes on accidental drowning; Eira sits at Owen's chart table and reads what her own log doesn't say.

---

## Setting

The harbormaster's office at the foot of the commercial wharf. Cedar-shingled. Chart table, tide log, field telephone, kerosene stove. November light — grey and flat. One brief exterior: the dock, where she and Parsons walk to confirm the location.

---

## Goal / pressure

The formal investigation is closing. Parsons needs her statement on record. Eira's goal is to give him enough of the truth to be useful and none of the truth that would require her to explain the night before. The pressure is his questions: he is professional, not suspicious of anything specific, but thorough, and thoroughness is precisely what she must survive.

---

## Action beats

1. **Parsons arrives at 9 a.m.** He is in uniform, carries a clipboard. He is in his late thirties — too young for the last war, too old for this one, exactly Coastal Guard age. He is not unkind and not warm; he has a job.

2. **The formal statement**: she sits at the chart table; he sits across from it. He goes through the morning of the discovery methodically: time she received the report, time she arrived at the breakwater, what she found and in what condition, the tide data she recorded that morning, whether she touched or moved the body.

3. **She answers accurately in all these respects.** She is good at answering accurately within the scope of the question. She has the tide data. She has the notation in the morning log. She is precise.

4. **He asks about the previous night.** Routine question — did anything unusual occur in the harbor area during the blow? Any unauthorized vessels? Anyone on the dock who shouldn't have been?

5. **She says**: "The blow kept most activity down. The fleet was in. I did a late mooring check at approximately twenty-three hundred hours." She pauses. "I didn't enter anything unusual."

6. **He asks her to show him where the body was found.** They walk the breakwater together. November cold. The granite is empty and salt-stained. She shows him the seam where the body lodged. He makes a note.

7. **On the way back**: he tells her what the investigation has found so far. Cause of death: hypothermia and drowning consistent with storm exposure. The clothing is civilian, European in manufacture — the cut and buttons identify it as continental, probably wartime production, probably 1941–42. No papers. No identification. No military markings.

8. **"Any theory on how he got there?"** she asks. He says: "Storm exposure, most likely. Could have gone off a vessel that didn't report it, or come in from the water directly." He pauses. "There's been some talk about enemy activity, but we're not pursuing that line. The acute U-boat period is last year."

9. **Back at the office**, he thanks her. He says the case will be classified as accidental drowning, unidentified. He'll keep her number in case anything comes up.

10. **After he leaves**: she closes the door. She sits at Owen's chart table. The office is exactly as it was before Parsons arrived. The tide log is open to this morning's entry.

11. **She turns back to October.** The entry for the night of the dock: her handwriting, the time, the tide reading, the mooring notes. "2300: mooring check completed, fleet secure." That's all it says.

12. **She reads this entry three times.** Then she closes the log.

---

## Outcome / resolved-beat

The official chapter closes. Parsons has his statement; the case will be closed as accidental. What resolves is the formal requirement — she has fulfilled her official obligations as harbormaster. The resolution is technical and exact. What it costs her is the knowledge that the official record is complete in every respect except the one that matters. The chapter ends on this distinction, held quietly without analysis.

---

## New question raised

Now that the official record is closed, the knowledge she carries is hers alone. Is that a burden or a choice? Owen's ledger is in the same cabinet — he kept things out of the log too. What does she do with that precedent?
