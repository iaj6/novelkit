# Chapter 23 — August

**POV**: Eira Bowman  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Season / time**: August 1944  
**Target words**: ~2,800  
**Chapter shape**: Multi-beat (harbor work; news of Galen Fuller's death; the meeting with Mrs. Fuller)  
**Ending mode**: Literary-fade  
**Opening texture**: Observation

---

## One-line summary

The telegram for Galen Fuller arrives two months after his death at Normandy; Eira sees his wife at the harbor store and finds the only true thing she has to say.

---

## Setting

Coldwater Reach harbor — the commercial wharf, the harbor store, the breakwater (briefly). August: the best harbor season, the fleet at full operation, the summer's sardine-run money in. The war is in the radio; the harbor is in the boats.

---

## Goal / pressure

This is a chapter about the specific texture of wartime grief — not Eira's, but grief she witnesses and has to respond to. The war is going better than it has gone; Paris will fall this month. But the telegrams have their own timeline, disconnected from the front's progress. Galen Fuller is dead since June 17th; his family is only now finding out in mid-August.

---

## Action beats

**Beat 1 — The harbor in full season:**
1. The August harbor: peak lobster season, the boats in and out on schedule, the processing shed at the town wharf smelling of brine and engine grease. She is managing the mooring rotation — two new boats have come in for the high season, displacing the usual positions. This requires negotiation with several captains, all of which she conducts efficiently.
2. She is noting this: the harbor is working well. The fuel allocation she secured in December has held through the season. She is good at this job. The measurement of this goodness is specific — the fleet is operational, the accounts balance, the tide log is accurate and complete.

**Beat 2 — The news about Galen Fuller:**
3. She hears about the telegram from Henry Cates, Robert Cates's father (whose son is still alive and was at Normandy too). He comes to the harbor store and tells her: Bertrand Hatch delivered the Fuller telegram this morning. Two months late. Galen has been dead since June 17th, somewhere in Normandy.
4. She knew this was a possible outcome. She has been aware, since D-Day, that either or both of those men could be dead and the family might not know yet. The confirmation has its own quality — different from anticipation.
5. She asks: "The family all right?" Henry Cates says: "As well as you'd expect." He means: not well. He means: Mrs. Fuller is in her house on Bay Street and Bertrand has been with her for an hour.

**Beat 3 — The harbor store:**
6. She goes to the harbor store for her own supplies later in the morning. Mrs. Fuller — Ruth Fuller, forty-three, whose husband owns a dragger that is now Ruth's dragger — is there two days later, coming in for provisions.
7. They stand near each other at the counter. Eira thinks about what to say and immediately understands that most things she might say are wrong. Condolences that gloss what happened. Something about the war's progress. Something about what Galen gave.
8. She says: "He was a good man with a boat." Mrs. Fuller says: "He was." That is the whole conversation. It is the right conversation.
9. Eira pays for her flour and goes back to the harbor.

**Beat 4 — The breakwater:**
10. She does a late afternoon breakwater check — she does this once a week in season, checking the granite blocks for winter storm damage that's sometimes only visible when the summer tides are lower. She walks the length.
11. She stands at the end and looks out at the harbor. The August light on the water. The boats working. She has been standing at this breakwater for eight years.
12. Galen Fuller is dead in France. The body that came in last October is named Jan Václav Horak and was Czech and displaced. She is still here. The harbor is still here.
13. She turns and walks back to the office.

---

## Outcome / literary-fade

The chapter ends on the harbor in late afternoon light — the office, the tide log, the season's work. Not elegiac; this is what August looks like. The literary-fade is the working harbor itself: the boats, the brine smell, the tide going out on schedule. She has said the true thing to Ruth Fuller and gone back to work. This is all she can do. It is also what she is.

---

## New question raised

Robert Cates is still alive (this is not confirmed in this chapter, only implied — Henry Cates did not say his son was dead). But who knows, in August 1944, what winter will bring?

## Continuity notes

- Galen Fuller: confirmed dead, killed June 17th, 1944, Normandy. The telegram arrives in mid-August (two-month delay is accurate to wartime notification timelines).
- Robert Cates: not confirmed dead in this chapter; his father mentioned him as alive as of recent contact. He survives the war; this is mentioned in passing later.
- Ruth Fuller: Galen's wife, 43, who now owns the dragger. She does not break down in the harbor store; she receives Eira's sentence and answers it. This is her register.
- Eira's sentence: "He was a good man with a boat." This is the correct sentence and the chapter should make clear that she knows it is correct — not because it is the formal condolence, but because it is true and complete and does not ask anything of Ruth Fuller.
- Bertrand Hatch: Western Union clerk, 70, mentioned in the world file as having delivered forty-three telegrams since December 1941 and not gotten used to it. He appears here by reference only.
- The August harbor is physically distinct from November or April harbor — render the seasonal texture: warmer water, different light, the sardine smell, the fuller fleet.
