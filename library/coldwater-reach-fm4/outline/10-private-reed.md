# Chapter 10 — Private Reed

**POV**: Lonzo Reed (fourth POV)  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Season / time**: December 1943  
**Target words**: ~1,700  
**Chapter shape**: Single scene (a full work day at the farm, rendered as observation)  
**Ending mode**: Literary-fade  
**Opening texture**: Routine-procedure

---

## One-line summary

Lonzo watches the Pfeiffer farm from his post: he sees Albrecht and Karl's moment at the fence, knows what it means, and keeps it out of his report — because some things are not dangerous, and he knows the difference.

---

## Setting

Pfeiffer farm. Lonzo's post at the barn door, from which he can see the field, the icehouse, and the near fence line. A full December work day — short light, cold enough for the prisoners to work in their issued coats.

---

## Purpose of this fourth-POV chapter

This chapter reframes what we have seen inside Hannah's perspective: the farm from the outside. Lonzo observes without the investment Hannah has — he sees Albrecht's pause at the fence, the exchange of dialect, the nod between Karl and Hannah, and he makes a judgment call about each. He is not neutral; he is specifically positioned as a man who knows what dangerous looks like and has decided most of what he sees here is not that.

The secondary function: Lonzo's son in the Pacific provides the chapter's emotional resonance — the war is elsewhere, very specifically, and the German boy in the field is a fact that sits alongside that.

---

## Action beats

1. **Lonzo at his post, early**: He arrives at 7 a.m. The prisoners are finishing their icehouse breakfast. He checks the Springfield — he has not fired it in the twelve weeks since this assignment began, and he doesn't expect to. He settles on his stool.

2. **The morning work**: The prisoners go to the field. He watches their patterns. He has learned their names in twelve weeks but uses them in his head only — Fisher, Brunner, Krebs, and the young one, Reinmar. Reinmar works the hardest. He doesn't know if that's character or nineteen.

3. **The fence moment**: Albrecht Pfeiffer is repairing something along the south fence. Karl is working near the same fence line. Karl says something under his breath — a Franconian phrase, a regional thing — and Albrecht stops.

4. **Lonzo sees this.** He sees Albrecht's pause — the two seconds, the stillness, the continuation. He sees Albrecht's face go somewhere and come back. He makes a note of it in his head: Pfeiffer recognized something. Those two men are from the same place, near enough.

5. **He does not write this in his report.** His report covers: number of prisoners, hours worked, no incidents, no violations. What Albrecht Pfeiffer recognized in Karl Reinmar's vowels is not an incident and not a violation. He knows the difference.

6. **Midday**: He eats the sandwich he brought. He reads three pages of the Bangor Daily News. The front page is Pacific — he reads every word of the Pacific news. His son Jimmy is on a destroyer escort, the USS Buckley class, somewhere Lonzo can't locate on any map he has access to. Jimmy writes once every six weeks if things are good. Things are currently uncertain.

7. **Afternoon**: Hannah comes out of the barn at some point and she and Karl are briefly near each other at the fence. A few words — he can't hear what from his position. He watches without moving.

8. **He makes another note in his head**: the girl is interested in the young one. Not dangerously — not in a way that's anything but seventeen-year-old interest in a person who's different from everyone else around her. He's seen dangerous. This isn't it. He'll watch.

9. **End of work day**: He walks the prisoners to the icehouse, confirms the lock, and writes his report on the clipboard. He reads it back. Seven hours of field work. No incidents. No violations. He signs it.

10. **Driving home in the Coastal Patrol truck**: He thinks about Jimmy. He thinks about the German boy Reinmar, who is nineteen and tired and has probably seen more of this war than Jimmy has, though from a direction Jimmy would object to. He doesn't think about this long. It doesn't go anywhere useful.

---

## Outcome / literary-fade

The chapter ends on the drive home — the December road, the truck's headlights, Lonzo's hands on the wheel. He has done his job. His job is watching, and he has watched, and what he has decided is: not dangerous. The literary-fade comes from the road itself, the night closing in, Jimmy's last letter in his jacket pocket.

---

## New question raised

How much does Lonzo know — or suspect — about the Pfeiffer farm and its growing entanglements? And: what does it mean that the person closest to witnessing all of this has decided, on his own authority, what to record and what to leave out?

## Continuity notes

- Lonzo's son: James Reed ("Jimmy"), serving on a destroyer escort in the Pacific. This is established here; it is the emotional weight that runs beneath his professional observation.
- Lonzo has a Coastal Patrol report form to fill out at the end of each day. He fills it out honestly — no incidents — while knowing that "no incidents" is a description and a choice.
- This chapter is deliberately shorter and more compressed than primary-POV chapters. It is a lens, not an arc. Do not develop Lonzo's interiority beyond what is shown through his watching.
- The parallel to the novel's central mystery: Lonzo is making exactly the same kind of editorial judgment that Eira made about the dock — deciding what goes in the record and what doesn't. Neither of them thinks of it that way.
- Render Lonzo's voice: direct, practical, specific, not given to abstraction. He thinks in observation, not analysis.
