# Chapter 08 — Mutter

**POV**: Hannah Pfeiffer  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Season / time**: December 1943  
**Target words**: ~2,700  
**Chapter shape**: Interiority-heavy (a day in which the external events are ordinary and the interior is not)  
**Ending mode**: Literary-fade  
**Opening texture**: Routine-procedure

---

## One-line summary

Magda's silence deepens to its worst yet; Hannah covers the household alone and reads to her mother in the afternoon; a German phrase surfaces from somewhere she can't place.

---

## Setting

Pfeiffer farmhouse — kitchen, Magda's bedroom, the barn. December — the farm in its deepest winter quiet, ice on the kitchen window in the mornings, the cows never leaving the barn now. The chapter stays close to the house.

---

## Goal / pressure

Hannah's goal is to hold the farm together for a day while her father is at the feed cooperative in Rockland and her mother is not functioning. The pressure is Magda — not a crisis, because Hannah has learned to manage around these spells, but a sustained weight. The chapter uses the ordinary day to create space for the interior question Hannah has been circling: what is Magda carrying that the war didn't start?

---

## Action beats

**Beat 1 — Morning without Albrecht:**
1. Albrecht leaves before dawn for the feed cooperative run — he goes twice a year, stays overnight. Hannah does the full milking alone. 5 a.m. Twenty-two cows. Cold barn, the lamp swinging. She is efficient; this is the work her body knows.
2. She checks on Magda before she leaves for school: Magda is in bed, eyes open, looking at the ceiling. Not asleep. Not distressed. Elsewhere. Hannah puts a cup of water on the nightstand and says: "Ich bin gleich wieder da." (I'll be right back.) Magda doesn't respond.

**Beat 2 — School:**
3. She gets through the school day — Latin with Marcus (she notices his precision, the way he pauses before correcting), her other classes, lunch. Nothing dramatic. But she is doing a second inventory the whole time: is Magda still in bed, has she eaten, has she moved.
4. At lunch she can't finish her sandwich. She wraps the rest and puts it in her coat for Magda.

**Beat 3 — Afternoon with Magda:**
5. She comes home at four, does the afternoon milking — the prisoners are in the icehouse, Lonzo has already gone for the day, she is alone in the barn — then goes inside.
6. Magda has not moved from the bed. The water cup is untouched.
7. Hannah does not panic. She has been here before. She heats broth on the stove and brings it up. She sits on the edge of the bed and holds the bowl.
8. Magda doesn't eat, but she turns her head toward Hannah. Progress of a kind.
9. Hannah reads aloud from her school textbook — not to teach her mother, not to reach her, just to fill the room with the sound of language. The text is a chapter of American history (the Constitutional Convention, a subject of no particular relevance). She reads two pages.
10. Then, without planning it, she switches to German. She begins reading the same text, translating it as she goes — imperfectly, the vocabulary getting away from her. Magda's eyes are tracking, slightly.

**Beat 4 — The phrase:**
11. Later, in the kitchen, making dinner alone, Hannah finds herself saying a phrase under her breath: *"Wenn das Wasser steigt, verlässt man das Dorf."* (When the water rises, you leave the village.)
12. She doesn't know where she learned this. It is not from her father — his sayings are about work and weather. She thinks it might be her grandmother, Magda's mother, whom she met once when she was five. Or perhaps Magda said it before the silences started. The origin is genuinely lost.
13. She says it again, testing it. It is specifically about water and villages and leaving; it has the shape of advice, or of justification.
14. She thinks: her mother left. Her father left. They left Bavaria before the water rose — or after it started rising, or while it was rising. She doesn't know when exactly the leaving was. She has been raised in Maine by people who left and never talked about the leaving.

**Beat 5 — Evening:**
15. Albrecht returns around 9 p.m. He checks on Magda briefly — Hannah hears his voice from upstairs, a low murmur in German — then comes down and eats the dinner she saved for him.
16. He says: "She had these spells before we came here too." That is all he says about it. Hannah does not ask.
17. She does the dishes. He sits at the table. The radio is off.

---

## Outcome / literary-fade

The chapter ends in the winter dark of the kitchen after the dishes, with her father sitting and Hannah about to go upstairs. The phrase is still in her head. The lamp on the table. The sound of Holsteins from the barn, distant and regular. The chapter dissolves into that texture without resolution — the spell has not broken, the question about Magda's history has not been answered, but the day has been survived.

---

## New question raised

What happened before they left Bavaria that Magda carries in her body and Albrecht will not talk about? Is what Hannah sees in Magda a version of what she will become, or is it something she can refuse?

---

## Continuity notes

- Magda's silence is cyclic: bad spells and partial recoveries. This is her worst spell so far in the narrative.
- The phrase *"Wenn das Wasser steigt, verlässt man das Dorf"*: Hannah's, from an unlocatable source. It surfaces again later.
- Albrecht's line "She had these spells before we came here too" is load-bearing: whatever Magda carries, it predates the war. This must not be labored; it arrives as one sentence from Albrecht and nothing more.
- This chapter should not pathologize Magda. Her silence is rendered as a condition of the person, not a plot mechanism or a symbol. She turns her head toward Hannah; she has not vanished.
- The chapter is interiority-heavy: most of its action is internal to Hannah. The physical beats (milking, cooking, reading aloud) are anchors, not plot points.
