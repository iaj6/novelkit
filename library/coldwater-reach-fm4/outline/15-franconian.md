# Chapter 15 — Franconian

**POV**: Marcus Klein  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Season / time**: April 1944  
**Target words**: ~2,800  
**Chapter shape**: Single scene (the after-school exchange, extending into Marcus's private assembly)  
**Ending mode**: Turn  
**Opening texture**: Dialogue

---

## One-line summary

Hannah brings Marcus a phrase she heard Karl say; his translation pinpoints the regional dialect; Marcus privately assembles the probable picture of who Jan Václav Horak was and why he came to Coldwater Reach.

---

## Setting

Marcus's classroom at Coldwater High School, after the last class of the day. April — the light lasting later now, the classroom warm enough that he has opened the window a few inches. Hannah comes to him after the building is mostly empty.

---

## Goal / pressure

Hannah's goal is simple: she wants to understand a phrase. Marcus's goal — ostensibly to answer her question accurately — is shadowed by what the accurate answer will tell him. The pressure is the assembly he has been half-avoiding: if the Franconian dialect confirms Karl's geographic origin at the precision he suspects, the coincidence with Horak's Czech nationality and the Franconian-Czech borderlands becomes something closer to a probable shape.

---

## Action beats

1. **After school**: Hannah stays behind. She is carrying her Latin textbook and something she's been turning over for a few days. She says: "I heard Karl say something to his friend in the icehouse. I was close enough to hear — I wasn't eavesdropping, I was just at the fence." She describes hearing it through a window, late afternoon.

2. **The phrase**: She gives it to him as best she can reproduce it — a phrase about harvest, something about a field and a reckoning. She renders it phonetically; her German is good enough that she has the sounds mostly right.

3. **He translates**: The phrase is a regional harvest idiom — he knows the type; it is specific to the Upper Main River communities, the Franconian countryside. In standard German the phrase would be rendered differently. This is not Munich German, not Berlin German. This is someone from the specific country between Nuremberg and the Czech border.

4. **He asks her**: "Where in Franconia is he from?" She says she doesn't know — she knows only that he's from somewhere near Ansbach, she thinks. He says: "This phrase is from closer to the Czech border than Ansbach. Maybe twenty, thirty kilometers east of Ansbach. Feuchtwangen direction, or further toward Dinkelsbühl."

5. **She absorbs this.** She says: "That's close to where my father is from." He says: "Yes." (He knew this from Chapter 6.)

6. **She leaves.** He closes the window. He is now alone.

7. **His assembly**: He stands at the window for a while. He has, without intending to, assembled the following:
   - Karl Reinmar is from the Franconian-Czech borderland region
   - Albrecht Pfeiffer is from the same region (Feuchtwangen, roughly)
   - The dead man — he has heard the name Horak in town now; the Coast Guard letter has circulated through official channels and the name is known — is Czech
   - Czechoslovakia's border with Germany ran through and near Franconia; the communities on both sides knew each other's dialects, shared centuries of crossover, had displaced-person networks that ran both ways during the 1930s and 40s
   
8. **What he constructs**: A man like Horak — Czech, displaced, in Halifax in 1942 — would, if he were trying to reach someone in coastal New England, seek the émigré communities he could identify through regional connection. Not German-Americans generically; specifically those whose families came from the Franconian-Czech borderland, where the communities were intertwined.

9. **The Pfeiffer farm**: He doesn't conclude that Albrecht Pfeiffer knew Horak. He doesn't have evidence for that. But the shape of the thing — the geography, the dialect, the border communities — is a shape he recognizes from his own years of movement. The person who knows someone who knows someone.

10. **He does not state this to anyone.** He is aware that he is constructing a probable picture from inadequate evidence. He also knows he has been good at constructing probable pictures from inadequate evidence, because he has had to be. He closes his lesson book. He puts on his coat.

---

## Outcome / turn

The turn is the full assembly landing in Marcus's mind. He now has what he will carry to Eira in Chapter 18 — the linguistic precision that identifies Horak as probably Czech (confirmed by the name), and the probable network logic that might explain what Horak was doing in Coldwater Reach. He doesn't tell Hannah what he's assembled. She gave him a piece and got back a translation, which is what she asked for.

---

## New question raised

If Horak was trying to reach someone through an émigré network — and the Pfeiffer farm is the most probable node of that network in Coldwater Reach — does Albrecht Pfeiffer know? Did he know? Did he know the man was coming and not come to meet him? And what happened at the dock?

---

## Continuity notes

- The Franconian-Czech borderland is real geography: the communities of Upper Franconia (the *Regnitz* and *Main* valleys, extending toward the Bohemian Forest) had centuries of intertwined German-Czech-Jewish community life before the Nazi period. Marcus's knowledge of this is historically grounded.
- Karl's dialect: eastern Franconian, closer to the Czech border than to Ansbach. The drafter should not specify the exact town; "Dinkelsbühl direction" is enough precision.
- Marcus does not confront Albrecht, does not contact anyone, does not tell Hannah what he's assembled. He sits with it. This is his mode.
- The name Horak: Marcus has heard it through town talk (the Coast Guard letter came through official channels); it confirms the Czech national origin he suspected.
- Do not have Marcus state his assembled picture explicitly. The reader follows the assembly; the chapter ends before Marcus has decided what to do with it.
