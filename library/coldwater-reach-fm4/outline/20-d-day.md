# Chapter 20 — D-Day

**POV**: Eira Bowman  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Season / time**: June 6, 1944  
**Target words**: ~2,900  
**Chapter shape**: Multi-beat (the 3 a.m. call; the dawn harbor; Beal's at noon; the evening tide)  
**Ending mode**: Literary-fade  
**Opening texture**: Summons

---

## One-line summary

D-Day arrives on the field telephone at 3 a.m.; Eira does her harbor work through it; two Coldwater men are in the landing.

---

## Setting

The harbormaster's office, the commercial wharf, Beal's General Store (at noon), the breakwater (evening). June 6, 1944 — the finest early-June morning in years, which is unbearable and also just weather.

---

## Goal / pressure

This is a chapter in which the world's largest event so far in the narrative arrives and Eira's job is the harbor. The pressure is not dramatic crisis — it is the sustained weight of knowing that two people from this town, whose boats she can name, whose fathers she knew, are on those beaches, and doing her professional work anyway.

---

## Action beats

**Beat 1 — 3 a.m.:**
1. The field telephone rings. She is not asleep — she sometimes isn't, at this hour. She picks up. It is Lawrence Cary, harbormaster at Rockland, who has been on the wire from Portland since midnight relaying military coordination traffic. He says: "Eira. It's on. The landing." He doesn't need to say more.
2. She says: "When?" He says: "Hours ago. By now they're ashore."
3. She thanks him. She hangs up. She is in the office; she walked down at midnight from force of habit, the way she does sometimes in high-pressure seasons. The harbor is dark and quiet.
4. She enters a notation in the log. Time, source of information, content. Professional. Then she closes the log and sits with it for a while.

**Beat 2 — Dawn:**
5. The dawn harbor in early June: the light coming early, the fishing boats beginning to move. She does the tide reading. Six-foot ebb, right on schedule. The boats go out.
6. Two Coldwater men are in the landing. She knows this from their families — Galen Fuller, twenty-two, Army infantry, and Robert Cates, twenty-four, also infantry. She has known their fathers' boats since before the boys were born. She does not know where in France they are. She does not know if they are alive.
7. She does the mooring check. She logs the morning departures. She fixes a cleat that has been loose on dock 3 since April.

**Beat 3 — Beal's at noon:**
8. The radio at Beal's General Store is on and has been since early morning. A small crowd: Bertrand Hatch (from Western Union, on his lunch break, looking grey), two women from the canning cooperative, a fisherman she doesn't know by name. The BBC broadcast is playing through static on WLBZ.
9. Eisenhower's recorded voice, formal and flat: the great crusade, etc. She listens to the whole thing. She does not feel what she expects to feel during it. She feels the staticness, the flatness, the distance between that voice and this town.
10. Agnes Vane — the school board member from Chapter 12 — says: "God willing they'll come home by Christmas." Nobody answers this.
11. Eira buys her ration items and leaves.

**Beat 4 — The afternoon:**
12. She works through the afternoon. A fishing boat has a bent prop shaft from an unmarked shallow-water rock — she deals with this, calls the repair yard, manages the documentation. This is the afternoon. The prop shaft exists; the landing exists; she manages the prop shaft.

**Beat 5 — Evening:**
13. At the end of the work day she walks to the end of the breakwater. She does this sometimes — it is not a habit but it is not unusual. The June evening light on the harbor.
14. She stands there for a few minutes. The fishing boats are coming in on the evening tide. She watches them. Each one is someone's boat, and behind it is someone's family, and behind that is the specific calculation that some families have made for generations: that the water is a reasonable risk for a working life. She has stood at this breakwater for eight years and watched this calculation.
15. Galen Fuller might be dead. Robert Cates might be dead. She doesn't know.

---

## Outcome / literary-fade

The chapter ends at the breakwater in the June evening light. The boats coming in. The sound of the harbor. Whatever she's carrying — the landing, the two men, the prop shaft, the dock knowledge she's been carrying for eight months — doesn't resolve. The literary-fade is the harbor at evening, the ordinary day having absorbed the largest event of the war so far. This is not a comment. This is what the day was.

---

## New question raised

When will she know about Galen Fuller and Robert Cates? And: having carried the dock knowledge for eight months, with the war now visibly turning toward its end — does she feel the weight of it differently?

---

## Continuity notes

- D-Day is June 6, 1944 — continuity fact #12. The landing has begun; Eira hears about it a few hours in.
- Lawrence Cary (Rockland harbormaster): introduced here, functional only.
- Galen Fuller (22) and Robert Cates (24): the two Coldwater men in the Normandy landing. Galen Fuller will be confirmed dead in Chapter 23 (killed June 17th; telegram arrives months late). Robert Cates survives, but this is not known until later.
- The breakwater ending: do not make it explicitly elegiac. She is standing on the breakwater. The harbor. The boats. This is her place of work. The weight is there but unnamed.
- D-Day arrives on the field telephone, not on the Beal's radio — this is deliberately different from the town's experience. She hears it first, alone, at 3 a.m. The Beal's scene is how the town gets it at noon.
