# Chapter 21 — Karl Speaks

**POV**: Hannah Pfeiffer  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Season / time**: June 6–7, 1944  
**Target words**: ~2,700  
**Chapter shape**: Multi-beat (D-Day news arrives on the farm; Karl's response; the sentence)  
**Ending mode**: Resolved-beat  
**Opening texture**: Summons

---

## One-line summary

Lonzo reads the D-Day headline aloud on the farm; Karl gives Hannah his first full English sentence — "I did not think I would live to see it" — and something between them shifts into a different register.

---

## Setting

Pfeiffer farm, June 6 and the morning of June 7. The barn, the near field, the fence. June — the farm at its most active, the days long, the soil smelling of growth.

---

## Goal / pressure

The war's most significant news of the year arrives at the farm obliquely — through Lonzo's newspaper, not through a radio or a telegram. The pressure is what it means for each person present: for Karl, the landing is the beginning of the end of the war that has defined his entire adult life. For Hannah, watching him receive this news is the moment their connection changes from proximity to something she has to name.

---

## Action beats

**Beat 1 — The morning news:**
1. June 7 — the landing was yesterday. Lonzo comes in the morning carrying the Bangor Daily News, which has a full-page headline: ALLIED ARMIES LAND IN FRANCE. He reads it in the morning before the prisoners come out.
2. When the prisoners come to the field, Lonzo reads the headline aloud — not performatively, just as information. His voice is flat: the fact, the landing, the number of divisions. He doesn't editorialize.
3. The older prisoners — Brunner, Fischer, Krebs — exchange looks. Brunner makes a sound that could be resignation or relief. Fischer turns back to his work. Krebs says nothing.
4. Karl is near the fence, setting stakes for the bean rows. He doesn't look up.

**Beat 2 — Albrecht:**
5. Albrecht has heard WLBZ the night before and knows already. He comes out of the barn with the usual morning work but he is not his usual self — he is somewhere more internal. He doesn't look at the prisoners.
6. Hannah notices this: her father knows, has known since last night, and has said nothing at breakfast, not a word in any language. He is holding this privately.

**Beat 3 — At the fence, afternoon:**
7. In the afternoon, Hannah is working near the fence — the bean stakes need straightening on her side. Karl is on the other side, near the same section.
8. She says, without planning to: "Did you hear the news?" She has not said this many words to him in sequence before.
9. He looks at her. He nods.
10. She waits. He says, in English — slow, deliberately formed, the words he has been constructing from Lonzo's newspapers for seven months: "I did not think I would live to see it." His English is careful and slightly formal, like someone who has earned each word one at a time.
11. She says nothing for a moment. Then: "Are you glad?"
12. He thinks about this — she can see him thinking about it, the way you see someone consulting a real answer rather than a social one. He says: "I think I am tired."
13. She says: "Yes." That's all.

**Beat 4 — The sentence:**
14. She carries those two sentences through the rest of the day. "I did not think I would live to see it." "I think I am tired." Not what she expected. Not relief, not triumph, not grief — just a person who has been in a war for two years saying what is actually true.
15. At dinner, Albrecht says grace in German. Magda is present and quiet. Hannah eats her food.
16. She thinks: Karl is nineteen and has been in the war since he was seventeen. The war is going to end. He will go back to somewhere. She will stay here. These are facts in the order they exist.

---

## Outcome / resolved-beat

The resolve is the sentence — it changes what Hannah understands about Karl and about herself in relation to him. She has been watching him for eight months; now she has heard him speak in his second language, carefully, and what he said was true. Their exchanges have been nonverbal or minimal; this is the first time the space between them has language in it. The resolution is not romantic — it is relational. She now has a person on the other side of that fence, not just a presence.

---

## New question raised

What is she doing with this? The war is going to end; Karl will be repatriated; she is eighteen and has never been further from the farm than Portland. What are the terms of this connection, and what does she owe it?

## Continuity notes

- Karl's first full English sentence to Hannah: "I did not think I would live to see it." His English is careful, earned, not fluent but functional — he has been learning from newspapers.
- Hannah's "Are you glad?" and Karl's "I think I am tired": these are the two sentences the chapter turns on. The prose should not comment on what they mean.
- Albrecht's silence about D-Day: he heard the news, said nothing at home. He is holding this privately. This is consistent with his character and also carries weight (the assembled picture Marcus has built suggests Albrecht might have a more complicated relation to the Czech network than just geographic coincidence).
- This chapter does not advance the mystery. It advances Hannah's arc: she is beginning to understand that the connection she has been forming with Karl is real and will have to be navigated, not just observed.
- Lonzo reading the headline aloud: a small, unremarkable act that he performs matter-of-factly. His version of this moment is pragmatic; Hannah's version is different.
