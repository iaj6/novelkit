# Chapter 22 — The Unfinished Letter

**POV**: Marcus Klein  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Season / time**: July 1944  
**Target words**: ~2,800  
**Chapter shape**: Interiority-heavy (a summer day in his room; the letter; the decision not to send it)  
**Ending mode**: Elliptical  
**Opening texture**: Interior

---

## One-line summary

Marcus drafts a letter to his Cambridge contact about Horak; writes four careful paragraphs; cannot send it without implicating more than he has the right to implicate; puts it in the drawer.

---

## Setting

Marcus's room at the Crow house — the second bedroom, Elm Street. July: the school is out; he has time he does not know what to do with. The room is warm; the window faces the street. It is a different kind of pressure — the pressure of unstructured time.

---

## Goal / pressure

Marcus has no external obligation today. He has had this day to himself and he has been at his desk since morning. The pressure is the question he has been circling since May: whether he owes something to what he knows — to Horak's memory, to the unnamed people in the network, to the possibility that someone somewhere is still wondering what happened to this man. The letter is his attempt to answer that question. The inability to send it is also an answer.

---

## Action beats

**Beat 1 — The morning:**
1. School is out. Henry has gone to work at the school for the summer maintenance schedule. Lillian is doing her own things. Marcus is in his room with a block of time.
2. He has a contact in Cambridge: Professor Emil Stern, a colleague from the Emergency Committee, one of the people who helped him move from Sherbrooke to Cambridge. They exchange letters occasionally. The last letter was in November; Stern wrote that the displaced scholars network was still processing applications.

3. He begins the letter. Not about himself — he is not writing to ask for anything. He is writing to ask whether Jan Václav Horak — a Czechoslovak national, displaced, last known location Halifax October 1942, found dead in coastal Maine October 1943 — was known to any of the networks that moved people through those channels.

**Beat 2 — The drafting:**
4. He writes four paragraphs. His handwriting is small and precise; the letter fills one and a half pages of his composition paper.
   - Paragraph 1: the official record (Horak's name, the Coast Guard classification, the location and date).
   - Paragraph 2: what he knows from his own understanding of the networks (not sourced, just offered as context — this is where his knowledge of displacement channels comes in, without naming specific people or places).
   - Paragraph 3: the question — was Horak known? Did anyone expect him? Is there a family that has not been notified?
   - Paragraph 4: his own position (he is writing as a private person, not in any official capacity; he understands the sensitivity; he asks only for whatever can be shared).

5. He reads what he has written.

**Beat 3 — The problem:**
6. He reads it again. He is looking at paragraph 2 — the one that explains how he knows what he knows about the displacement networks. To write that paragraph with any precision, he has had to gesture toward things he knows from his own experience. Which means he has gestured toward Vienna, Paris, London, the unofficial crossings. He has not been specific about people. But to a reader like Stern, the specificity of the framework would reveal that Marcus was not an observer of these networks but a participant.

7. He is not afraid of this — Stern knows him, knows his history. But there is more: paragraph 3 asks about Horak's known contacts in coastal Maine. If he sends this letter, and if Stern inquires, and if that inquiry leads back to the Pfeiffer farm, the Pfeiffer family — already in a precarious social position as a German-American family with German POWs on their property — could find themselves in a more official spotlight than they want or can handle.

8. **And Eira**: she told him, in May, that she was on the dock that night. She didn't tell him what she saw or didn't see. She trusted him with a piece of her knowledge. He doesn't know how a letter to Cambridge would affect her, if at all. But he doesn't know that it wouldn't.

9. He could redraft the letter without those implications. He tries a fifth paragraph. It doesn't work — removing the precision removes the usefulness.

10. **He puts the letter in his desk drawer.** He does not burn it. He does not revise it further. He puts it in the drawer and closes the drawer.

**Beat 4 — The afternoon:**
11. He goes for a walk. The July town, the harbor, the smell of summer. He walks past the breakwater without stopping.
12. He returns. Lillian has made a cold supper — tomatoes from the garden, bread, cheese. He eats it. He reads a page of Virgil. He goes to bed.

---

## Outcome / elliptical

The letter is in the drawer. It is not sent and not destroyed. This is where it lives — in the space between decision and action, which is also where most of what Marcus knows about this matter lives. The chapter ends without resolution, without even the motion toward resolution. The question of what he owes to Horak's memory is present and unanswered. This is the novel's first explicitly elliptical ending.

---

## New question raised

What would it mean to send the letter — for Eira, for the Pfeiffers, for Marcus himself? And: is keeping things in the drawer a form of wisdom or a form of the same evasion he has been examining in himself since October?

---

## Continuity notes

- Professor Emil Stern: a Cambridge contact, Emergency Committee. He does not appear; the letter is unaddressed to him in the prose, but his presence orients what Marcus is trying to reach.
- The letter's four paragraphs: the drafter can render these as summary or as partial quotation, but should not have Marcus write the full letter out in prose. The chapter is interiority-heavy, not epistolary.
- The implications Marcus is protecting: Eira's dock knowledge; the Pfeiffer family's social position; the unnamed persons in the network. He is not protecting them heroically — he is making a careful calculation about what his letter would stir up.
- The drawer: this physical object recurs only by implication. The letter stays in the drawer through the rest of the novel. It is never sent.
- July in the Crow house: the kitchen garden is producing; Lillian's tomatoes are real. The summer life of the house is a textural counterpoint to Marcus's interiority.
