# Chapter 06 — The Half-Question

**POV**: Marcus Klein  
**Tense**: Third-person limited past  
**Season / time**: November 1943  
**Target words**: ~2,700  
**Chapter shape**: Single scene (classroom / after-class exchange, extending into Marcus's private thinking)  
**Ending mode**: Turn  
**Opening texture**: Routine-procedure

---

## One-line summary

Hannah asks Marcus what *Landsman* means; his answer sends her back to the farm with a word; her question sends him forward into an assembly he'd been avoiding.

---

## Setting

Coldwater High School — Marcus's classroom, then the empty classroom after students leave, then a brief walk to the teachers' room. Late November — the school's furnaces working hard, the windows fogging where the cold meets the warm glass.

---

## Goal / pressure

Marcus teaches; Hannah asks an apparently simple vocabulary question; the question is not simple. His goal is to answer her question accurately without showing what the accuracy costs him — the word *Landsman* carries freight in more than one direction, and her context (a man on her family's farm) makes the question specific in ways he needs to sit with.

---

## Action beats

1. **The Latin class**: Marcus is drilling the irregular verbs — a subject he can teach in his sleep, and sometimes feels he does. The class is fine. One student, Michael Bates (present for texture, not plot), is struggling with *ferre* and Marcus stays patient.

2. **After class**: the students leave. Hannah Pfeiffer remains — she does this occasionally for Latin help, occasionally for German questions. She is one of his better students. He is gathering his papers.

3. **Her question**: "What does *Landsman* mean?" He looks up. He asks where she heard it. She says: "One of the men at our farm. I think he said it to himself."

4. **He answers the word's meaning**: literally, a person from the same region or the same land. A term of affiliation — it implies shared origin, not just nationality. In German contexts, *Landsmann* (he notes the full form) specifically invokes regional identity, not just national. Someone would use it of another person from their *Gegend* — their home district.

5. **He asks**: "Said it to himself, or to someone?" She says: she doesn't know. Maybe to himself. He asks: "Which farm?" She says: Pfeiffer. He knows the Pfeiffer farm from town talk — the only German-POW placement in the area. He knows the Pfeiffer name and that they are Bavarian-German by origin.

6. **He says carefully**: "It's an interesting word to use in that context." He doesn't elaborate. She looks at him for a moment and says: "He's from Franconia." She says this as a piece of information she has assembled recently and is testing.

7. **He says**: "I see." He does.

8. **She leaves.** He stands by the window.

9. **Marcus alone:** He looks out at the November schoolyard. He has been avoiding a line of thought since the body was found last month. The town's theory — U-boat agent — is wrong; he knew that from the description of the clothing and the calluses. A man in European civilian clothes with wrong-place calluses is not military; he is a person in motion.

10. **He has not asked himself**: what kind of person in motion? He has not asked this because the answer would require him to know things he has spent two years trying not to be the person who knows. He knows the civilian networks of wartime displacement. He moved through them. Not all of them were official.

11. **Now**: a German prisoner from Franconia, on the Maine coast, using the word *Landsmann* in earshot of a man whose family is from the same region. And a Czech body on the breakwater. These are not connected by spy-novel logic. But there are other logics. Refugees. Contacts. The long chain of people trying to reach people.

12. He thinks: he doesn't know this. He is constructing a probable shape from inadequate evidence. He should not do this.

---

## Outcome / turn

The turn is internal: Marcus has now consciously acknowledged the line of thinking he'd been suppressing. He has not reached a conclusion. But the question has been opened from both ends: what was that man doing on the dock, and what do the Pfeiffer farm's coincidences mean? He doesn't know. He turns away from the window.

---

## New question raised

What does Marcus know — from his own history — about the kind of networks that move people through wartime Europe and onto unlikely coastlines? And what obligation does that knowledge carry?

---

## Continuity notes

- Hannah and Marcus have an existing student-teacher relationship; this is not their first interaction, but it is the first one the novel renders.
- *Landsmann* / *Landsman*: Marcus gives the full German form and meaning. This is Hannah's first piece of explicit vocabulary connecting Karl and Albrecht.
- Marcus's knowledge of displacement networks is established here as background — his own movement through them — without elaboration. Do not explain what the networks were; let the fact of his knowledge exist.
- The connection Marcus is half-assembling: Czech body on the breakwater + Franconian prisoner + Bavarian-German farm family = a probable shape he cannot verify. Do not have him state this explicitly. The turn is his internal acknowledgment that the question exists.
- Hannah's contribution to this chapter is functional: she has no idea she's given Marcus anything significant. She's asking about a word.
