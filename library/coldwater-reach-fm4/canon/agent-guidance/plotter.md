# Plotter guidance — Coldwater Reach

## POV count and rotation

Three primary POV characters: Eira Bowman, Hannah Pfeiffer, Marcus Klein. Each carries approximately 10 chapters. The rotation is **asymmetric** — it follows narrative necessity, not a mechanical cycle. When one thread has momentum, two or three consecutive chapters in that POV is correct. When a thread needs space to develop before the next confrontation with its stakes, that POV may go quiet for several chapters.

Rules:
- Do not mechanically alternate (Eira, Hannah, Marcus, Eira, Hannah, Marcus).
- Do not allow any primary POV to go more than 4–5 consecutive chapters without an appearance.
- The opening chapter should be Eira's — she holds the inciting object (she was at the dock the night before the body came in).
- The closing chapter should also be Eira's, or a chapter that ends in her register.

**Fourth-POV chapters** (Henry Crow, Lonzo Reed, or Karl Reinmar) may be used 2–3 times across the manuscript. They should be shorter than main POV chapters (~1,500–2,000 words vs. ~2,500–3,000 for primary POVs). Assign them where:
- The reader needs information or a vantage point that the main three genuinely cannot provide
- A thematic echo needs to land from outside the main three's perspective

Suggested fourth-POV placements:
- One Lonzo Reed chapter, perhaps mid-novel, showing what the farm looks like from the outside of Hannah's interiority
- One Henry Crow chapter, late in the novel, showing what Marcus's displacement looks like to the man who has housed him
- Karl Reinmar if the structure calls for it, but use sparingly — his interiority is culturally remote and should not be over-explained

## Chapter-shape preferences

Standard shape for this novel: a chapter follows one POV character through a unit of time (typically a day or a portion of a day) in which something shifts — an external event, a new piece of knowledge, a decision made or not made, a relationship that changes by one degree. The shift does not need to be dramatic. Accumulation is the structural method.

**Single-scene chapters** (one continuous scene) are appropriate for high-pressure moments: Eira finding the body, a confrontation between Hannah and her father, a scene between Marcus and the school board. Use them for the chapters where compression and intensity are needed.

**Multi-beat chapters** (two or three short scenes in the same POV, same day or across a short span) are the novel's workhorse. They allow the POV character's day to cover more ground and let the mystery-pieces assemble gradually.

**Interiority-heavy chapters** are correct for this register and should appear regularly — especially in Marcus's and Hannah's chapters. These are not slow; they are the novel's mode of action. A chapter in which Marcus works through a piece of knowledge across a long walk home is not an "interior chapter" as a failure mode; it is a chapter.

**Short interstitial chapters** (fourth-POV) function as reframing devices. They should not advance plot mechanically but should shift how the reader understands what has already happened or what is about to happen.

## Episode-vs-arc structure

This is an **arc structure**, not episodic. Chapters do not resolve into self-contained units. The accumulation of what each character knows and carries is the structure — each chapter adds a weight, adjusts a relationship, or shifts the angle of a question. The three arcs are:

- **Eira**: authority and its costs → what obligation her knowledge of that night creates → a resolution that is hers alone, not the war's
- **Hannah**: accumulation of small choices → the choice about Karl Reinmar and what it means → what she is becoming on the other side of it
- **Marcus**: whether displacement ends → what the displaced person becomes when the ground stops moving → the Crow household as its own answer

The mystery of the body threads through all three arcs without resolving them. None of the three arcs concludes **because** the mystery is solved. They conclude because the characters have changed.

## Ending-mode distribution

For 30 chapters, target this approximate distribution:

- **Literary-fade** (quiet sensory or atmospheric close, no action resolution): 10 chapters
- **Resolved-beat** (the chapter's central question or pressure resolves): 7 chapters
- **Turn** (reveal or pivot that recontextualizes): 5 chapters (used at genuine inflection points — not as default tension)
- **Coda** (brief aftermath or reflective beat after main close): 4 chapters
- **Cliffhanger or mid-action**: 2–3 chapters (maximum; used with intention)
- **Elliptical** (deliberate ambiguity, open question): 1–2 chapters (especially useful at the end)

**Distribution rules**:
- No more than 2 consecutive chapters with the same ending mode
- Cliffhangers are not the default for mid-novel momentum; literary-fade or turn are
- The final chapter should end elliptically or with coda — the story ends before VE Day, in the register of lives that have shifted but not concluded

## Chapter target words

Primary POV chapters: **~2,500–3,200 words** (approximately 8–10 pages). The variance is intentional — high-pressure chapters may compress to 2,000; set-piece chapters may run to 3,500.

Fourth-POV chapters: **~1,500–2,000 words**. Shorter by design — these are lenses, not arcs.

Total target: ~80,000 words across 30 chapters. With ~27 primary-POV chapters averaging ~2,700 words plus ~3 fourth-POV chapters averaging ~1,700 words: ~77,900 words. The math works with normal variance.

## Genre-specific structural conventions

This is not a genre mystery, but the mystery of the body provides structural spine. These structural requirements follow from the brief:

1. **The body arrives in Chapter 1 or 2** — Eira finds it (or hears about it; she saw the dock the night before). This is the inciting object. Delay weakens the structure.

2. **Each POV character's relationship to the body must be established in their first chapter** — not through their explicit awareness of the mystery, but through the fact that they hold a piece of it, even if they don't know it yet.

3. **The reader's knowledge of the full picture should assemble across the middle third** of the novel — chapters 10–20 — through juxtaposition of the three POVs, not through any single character's investigation.

4. **The body is not resolved with a clean answer**. By the novel's end, the reader should have a probable picture of who the man was and what happened, assembled from the three partial views. But this is implied, not announced. The final chapter does not explain.

5. **D-Day (chapter ~20, roughly)** should arrive as a chapter-beat — the kitchen radio, the school, the harbor — and mark the turn toward the novel's final third without redirecting any of the three arcs toward war-drama.

6. **The three characters must never be in a scene together**. Plot crossings are pairwise only. If the outline ever places all three in one location at one time, that is a structural error.

7. **At least one thread should be deliberately unresolved at novel's close**. The breakwater question itself is the primary candidate. The characters' relationships to it should resolve; the underlying fact may stay in shadow.

8. **No chapter should function as spy-thriller mechanics**: no coded messages, no submarine landings, no chapter driven by the question "who is the enemy." The war is background. The body is a human mystery, not a national-security mystery.
