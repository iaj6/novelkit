# Reader guidance — Coldwater Reach

## Intended audience

Adult readers of literary fiction with patience for accumulation and restraint. The shelf comps are Strout (*Olive Kitteridge*, *Olive, Again*), Richard Russo (*Empire Falls*), and Ron Hansen — multi-character American novels set in specific communities where the weight of a place and a period is the subject as much as any individual arc. Readers who expect plot-driven mysteries should be warned early; this is not that book. Readers who trust restrained, precise prose to carry emotional weight without declaration will be rewarded.

Secondary audience: readers of WWII home-front fiction (Susan Choi's *American Woman*, Jesmyn Ward's *Sing, Unburied, Sing* in its temporal weight, Ian MacEwan's *Atonement* in its partial-information structure) — readers who want the war as texture and pressure, not as event.

---

## Brief-adherence checks to perform

These are the yes/no questions the Reader must answer for this manuscript, beyond general craft review:

1. **Are all three POV voices distinct at the sentence level?** Eira's chapters should have short declaratives and professional vocabulary. Hannah's should have interior permission and German threading. Marcus's should have formal precision and subordinate qualification. If all three sound like the same narrator, this is a critical failure.

2. **Does any chapter behave like a genre mystery?** (Investigation scenes, code-breaking, the protagonist "digging into" the body's identity as if running a plot.) If yes, flag it — this is structural drift from the brief's explicit constraint.

3. **Does the war appear as foreground in any chapter?** (A chapter whose dramatic center is a war event rather than a character's life.) D-Day and the Bulge may appear as chapter-beats; they must not be chapter-centers.

4. **Does the body on the breakwater resolve too cleanly?** The brief requires the breakwater question to stay at least partially in shadow. A chapter or ending that provides a clear, explained answer is a breach of the brief's explicit instruction.

5. **Do the three POV characters ever appear in the same scene together?** This is a hard structural rule. If it happens, it must be flagged as a critical error.

6. **Is the historical texture embedded or decorative?** Period-accurate terms (V-Mail, ration books, BC-1206) should appear because characters need them, not as atmospheric props. If a chapter reads like a period-detail insertion pass, flag it.

7. **Is race a fact of life, rendered specifically, not lectured?** Henry and Lillian Crow should be specific people whose specific lives in 1943 coastal Maine are rendered through behavior and silence. Any passage that reads as the author explaining race relations to the reader is a breach of the brief.

8. **Do the three individual arcs (Eira/authority, Hannah/becoming, Marcus/displacement) each reach their own resolution** that is independent of whether the mystery resolves? The brief requires these to be character resolutions, not mystery-plot resolutions.

9. **Does the ending arrive before VE Day?** The story closes in spring 1945, before May 8. Any chapter that describes VE Day itself has overrun the ending.

10. **Is the dialect handling restrained?** Albrecht's English should not be phonetically rendered as stage-German. Marcus's English should feel formally overcorrect without being burlesqued. German phrases in Hannah's chapters should be untranslated. If any of this has become caricature, flag it.

## What "good" looks like for this book

A good chapter in this manuscript:
- Establishes its POV within the first sentence through vocabulary and perceptual mode (Eira notices the tide; Hannah notices the cold in her father's voice; Marcus notices the grammar of a student's mistake)
- Advances its character's internal arc by one small increment without announcing the advancement
- Contains at least one moment where the reader understands more than the POV character — because of the juxtaposition with another chapter, or because the POV character's language reveals what they are not letting themselves think
- Ends in a way that does not require the next chapter to recap it

A good Eira chapter has the compression and procedural calm of a harbormaster's log that turns out to be a grief document.

A good Hannah chapter has the quality of a seventeen-year-old's awareness — acutely observant, slightly more honest than an adult would allow, and willing to follow a thought further than it's comfortable to follow.

A good Marcus chapter has the quality of a letter in a second language by someone who is very good at it — but the reader can see, under the precision, what the writer is working not to say.

## What to flag explicitly if the manuscript drifts

**Voice homogenization**: If Eira, Hannah, and Marcus sound like the same careful literary narrator, name it explicitly and cite examples from each POV. This is the primary drift risk for this register. The brief specifies three different prose registers; if they have converged, the drafter has defaulted to a single literary voice.

**Mystery-genre drift**: If any chapter reads like an investigation, a thriller scene, or a plot-driven mystery (protagonist searches for information, information reveals a key clue, momentum is driven by "what happened"), name it explicitly. The brief prohibits spy-thriller mechanics. A chapter that functions as investigation has misread the brief, even if the prose is excellent.

**The body as symbol**: If any chapter or passage treats the unknown man as a symbol of the war, of displacement, or of any theme (rather than as a dead human being), flag it. The brief is explicit that the body is not symbolic machinery.

**Period-lecture**: If a character explains what ration books are, or the novel pauses to describe the V-Mail system as if the reader needs orientation, flag it as register failure. The period texture should be invisible infrastructure, not taught content.

**Over-articulated emotion**: If a passage explicitly names the architecture of a character's feeling ("Her grief had the quality of..."; "What he felt was not quite fear but..."), flag it. The brief's register carries feeling through what characters notice and do, not through taxonomy of states.

**The war as drama**: If D-Day or the Bulge are staged as dramatic scenes (the town gathers, characters emote, the chapter turns on the event), flag it. These should arrive as weather — a sentence, a paragraph at most, then the character's ordinary life continues under new pressure.

## What to applaud

**When the three POV voices are genuinely distinct**: Eira's short declaratives, Hannah's German threading and interior recursion, Marcus's formal precision — if these are working at the sentence level throughout, this is the book's central craft achievement and should be named and celebrated. Do not flatten it in editorial notes.

**When the mystery assembles without announcing itself**: The best moments in this manuscript will be chapters where the reader's picture of what happened on the breakwater shifts without any character investigating anything — because a detail in Eira's professional log intersects with something Hannah noticed at the farm, or because Marcus's knowledge of a certain type of displacement gives a name to something Eira won't let herself name. When this works, defend it. Do not suggest the author "clarify" connections the reader should assemble themselves.

**When Henry and Lillian Crow are fully present without the prose explaining them**: When the Crow household scenes carry their full social weight through dialogue, domestic detail, and the texture of a warm kitchen in a cold-to-them town — without the narrator stepping in to contextualize — that is the brief being honored. Defend those scenes.

**When a chapter earns its emotion through specificity**: A chapter that makes the reader feel something through a specific tool (the harbormaster's log entry, the German word that surfaces in Hannah's interior, the sentence Marcus almost-writes and doesn't) should be identified and defended, not subjected to "more emotion" suggestions.
