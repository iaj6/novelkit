# World

## Coldwater Reach, Maine — 1943–1945

Coldwater Reach is an invented coastal Maine town, positioned on a rocky tidal inlet south of Bangor, north of Portland — somewhere on the Penobscot Bay periphery, close enough to the sardine canneries and lobster wharves that its economy makes sense, far enough from the naval installations that it is not strategic, only adjacent. Population roughly 800. Not a summer colony. A working town.

The harbor is the organizing fact of Coldwater Reach. The breakwater runs two hundred feet out from the north shore, granite blocks laid in the 1890s, algae-striped from the waterline down. The tidal range is eleven feet. The fishing fleet — already shrunken by enlistments and fuel rationing — moors on the inside. The harbormaster's office occupies a cedar-shingled building at the foot of the commercial wharf, with a chart table, a tide log, a field telephone, and a kerosene stove.

## Physical texture

- **The harbor in November**: black water, spray over the breakwater, fog that sits in the channel until noon. The lobster smacks go out in the dark and come back in the dark. Diesel smoke and brine and the cold that is wet rather than dry.
- **The Pfeiffer farm**: three miles inland on Route 9, a working dairy operation. Red barn, icehouse beside it (now whitewashed inside and partitioned into a sleeping room for the prisoners). Twenty-two Holsteins. Corn stubble in October. The smell of silage. Stone walls the first Pfeiffers built in 1903.
- **The town itself**: one main street, a general store (Beal's), the post office, a Congregational church, a Legion hall that doubles as the rationing board offices, the school. The school is a WPA-era brick building; it handles grades one through twelve, a total enrollment of about 130 children.
- **Henry and Lillian Crow's house**: Elm Street, three blocks from the harbor, a white clapboard house with a good vegetable garden that Lillian tends. The house is warm, kept. Marcus rents the second bedroom. The kitchen is the social center.

## Historical layer

### The POW labor program
Under the Emergency Farm Labor program, German prisoners of war from the Houlton internment camp (Camp Houlton, a real and large facility in Aroostook County) were assigned to satellite work camps and individual farms to supply agricultural labor for the duration. The prisoners were housed under the Geneva Convention terms: adequate food, shelter, pay in scrip redeemable at the PX, and armed escort during work. The escort at the Pfeiffer farm is a single aging Coastal Patrol private, Lonzo Reed, who sits on a stool at the barn door with a Springfield rifle he has not fired in two years.

### Home-front rationing and communications
Ration books govern sugar, meat, coffee, fuel oil, rubber, and shoes. Fishermen receive a fuel-oil allocation; civilian vessels are prohibited from certain waters after dark. The Coastal Patrol (volunteers, mostly older men) patrols the beaches and harbor entrances for signs of enemy activity — their authority is real but their means are limited.

V-Mail (microfilmed letters photographed and printed at destination) is the dominant form of communication with men overseas. Casualty telegrams arrive at the Western Union office on Main Street; the Western Union clerk is Bertrand Hatch, seventy, who has delivered forty-three since December 1941 and has not gotten used to it.

The BC-1206 radio set is the standard wartime communications radio; civilian shortwave is restricted, but commercial broadcast (WLBZ out of Bangor) carries the nightly war news. Families gather at the kitchen radio at six o'clock.

### The U-boat context
By autumn 1943, the acute U-boat threat to the Maine coast that characterized 1942 has subsided (the Battle of the Atlantic having largely turned). Submarines are present in the Atlantic but are no longer surfacing near the coast or sinking tankers in visible range. The town remembers 1942 — the dim-outs, the floating debris, the one body that washed up at Pemaquid that summer — but 1943 is quieter. The body on the breakwater is not a U-boat story. The town will reach for that explanation first. It is wrong.

### German-American community
The Pfeiffers are not unique in coastal Maine; there were German-speaking communities throughout New England, established in the 19th-century waves of immigration. After Pearl Harbor and the subsequent declaration of war on Germany, these families occupied an uncomfortable national position. German-born noncitizens were classified as enemy aliens (required to register, restricted from coastal areas in some jurisdictions). The Pfeiffers are naturalized citizens; this protects them legally and does very little socially.

### Race in Coldwater Reach, 1943
Henry and Lillian Crow are Black Americans in a nearly all-white small Maine town. Henry's family came north from Norfolk, Virginia, in 1924 as part of the Great Migration's northern edge. He works as chief custodian of the school — a secure, respected, low-paying position that puts him in contact with almost everyone in town. The town's relationship to the Crows is specific: they are known, long-established, and still not quite unquestioned. The prose should render this without monologue.

## Seasonal arc

The story runs autumn 1943 through spring 1945:
- **Autumn 1943**: the body. The first accumulation of winter. Silage, deer hunting season.
- **Winter 1943–44**: the longest and most interior season. Ice on the harbor shallows. The school's furnace. Ration book renewal.
- **Spring 1944**: mud season. D-Day (June 6, 1944) falls within this narrative period — it arrives on the radio at Beal's store and at the Crows' kitchen.
- **Summer and autumn 1944**: the farm at full labor. The harvest. The war that seemed to be ending isn't ending yet.
- **Winter 1944–45**: Ardennes offensive (Battle of the Bulge, December 1944). The town loses two more men.
- **Spring 1945**: VE Day approaches. The story ends before it arrives.

## Tone

Not pastoral. Not elegiac as a sustained note — the prose does not linger for beauty. The world is cold and concrete: wood stoves, diesel, the particular smell of brine-soaked rope, the weight of a ledger that must balance. What is moving comes from specificity, not from atmosphere declared.
