# Style

## POV and tense

Third-person limited past tense throughout. The narration is bound tightly to the chapter's POV character — the reader knows what that character knows, perceives what they perceive, and uses the vocabulary and conceptual frame they would use. The narrator does not editorialize independently of the POV. Free indirect discourse is the primary mode of interiority.

## Register: three voices, one restraint

All three POVs share a commitment to restraint — the prose trusts the reader to feel what is not said. But restraint applies differently in each voice:

**Eira's chapters**: The most compressed. Short declarative sentences. Professional vocabulary — tidal schedules, hull draft, load manifests, weather notation. Interior observation is disguised as external observation: what she notices and when and how long she looks are doing the work of interiority. She does not analyze her own emotions. They arrive as sudden physical facts (a cold that is not the wind; a weight in the hands that has nothing to do with the line she's coiling). Dialogue is clipped, often in shorthand; she says what is necessary and not the rest.

**Hannah's chapters**: The most porous. More interior consciousness than Eira's — adolescence permits it. Her sentences are longer and sometimes recursive; a thought can interrupt itself. German words and phrases surface under pressure (her father's language breaking through). The farm's sensory world is her primary anchor: her chapters ground quickly in physical sensation before moving inward. She is the character most willing to name her feelings, but only once — she does not rehearse them.

**Marcus's chapters**: Precise and slightly formal. The academic's habit of measurement: subordinate clauses that qualify, hedges that are grammatically precise. He is always slightly aware of the language he is writing in, as if English is a second home he keeps properly swept. His sentences tend to be longer than Eira's and more carefully constructed than Hannah's; he builds to his conclusions in a way that the other two do not. He occasionally catches himself in a formulation and revises mid-sentence.

## Sentence rhythm

- Average: 15–22 words (Marcus), 10–16 words (Eira), 12–20 words with more variance (Hannah)
- Eira's shortest declaratives can drop to 4–6 words at moments of extremity
- Marcus's longest sentences should not exceed 45 words; if they run longer, they should be doing complex grammatical work, not describing simple actions

## Dialogue

Dense enough to carry the story's social texture, but never decorative. No character explains what they mean. No character clarifies for the reader's benefit what they just said. Dialogue is where the constraint operates most visibly: what is not said in conversation has as much weight as what is.

- Eira's dialogue: imperative or interrogative; she does not explain.
- Hannah's dialogue: normal teenage speech in English, but registers differently in the few German exchanges.
- Marcus's dialogue: formal by one register above the conversation he is in, which he has learned to compensate for with attention to tone.

Tags are minimal. He said / she said is correct. Avoid "countered," "admitted," "declared."

## Interiority and indirect speech

Free indirect discourse carries the interior. "The body had been there since Wednesday, probably. Or Tuesday." is preferable to "She thought the body had been there since Wednesday, probably." The chapter's POV ownership is established at the opening; interior thinking flows without tag.

## Historical texture

Period-accurate language and objects are the story's floor. Do not modernize the idiom. Do not explain the period to the reader ("rationing, which was how the government controlled supplies during the war"). Use the period's terms and trust the reader. Specific names matter: the BC-1206, the V-Mail, the ration books, WLBZ, Camp Houlton, the Coastal Patrol.

## What the prose does not do

- Does not describe war heroism. The war is elsewhere. What reaches Coldwater Reach is loss (telegrams), constraint (rations), and the living displaced (Marcus, the prisoners).
- Does not make the sea romantic. The harbor is a workplace. Weather is a professional variable.
- Does not make the Germans — prisoner or parent — into symbols. Karl is nineteen and tired. Albrecht is a man who left one country and cannot fully leave it.
- Does not explain race. Henry and Lillian Crow are specific people living a specific life in a specific town. The prose renders this through what happens, what is said, what is not said.
- Does not reach for the lyric unless the POV earns it. Hannah is the character most likely to perceive something beautifully; even her prose earns that beauty through grounded sensory work first.

## Failure modes

The AI-author patterns most tempting in this story's register. The drafter should actively avoid these; the compression pass should cut them.

- **Post-scene subtext naming** — a paragraph after a scene names what the scene "was about." This story's whole contract is that the subtext stays subtext; the reader assembles meaning. Any sentence that functions as "which meant that..." or "the weight of that moment was..." should be cut.

- **Thesis restatement** — abstract sentence re-stating the chapter's or book's theme. The brief is explicit: "the story trusts the reader to feel what is not said." A sentence like "She understood then what it meant to hold a position built for another" is the failure mode, not the goal.

- **Sentence-after-the-sentence** — an image lands, and then the next sentence glosses it. Especially damaging in Eira's chapters, where compression is the register. The image should do its work alone. Exception: in Marcus's chapters, a qualifying sentence that adds precision (not emotion) is within register.

- **Emotional structural analysis** — "Her grief had the quality of..." "What she felt was not quite fear but a cousin of it..." This is not the register. Feelings arrive as physical fact or through what a character notices, not as taxonomized states.

- **Dialogue glosses** — "as if to say," "which meant he was asking," "the question beneath the question." The brief says the dialogue is where constraint operates most visibly. Interpretation of dialogue is the reader's work.

## Lean-into patterns

Patterns the drafter and editors should NOT treat as failures, because this register actively uses them.

- **Free indirect interiority that arrives quietly mid-scene** — a line that is clearly the character's interior thought, unmarked, embedded in external description. This is the mode. Do not tag it. Do not move it to a new paragraph unless the tonal shift needs marking.

- **Sentence fragments in Eira's chapters** — "November. The harbor quiet." These are correct; they are her perceptual rhythm. Not a failure of grammar.

- **German phrases in Hannah's chapters** — untranslated, in the flow of the sentence. The reader can feel the meaning from context. Not a failure of accessibility.

- **Marcus's qualifying subordinate clauses** — his narration builds to conclusions through subordinate structure. This is his register, not a verbosity failure. Cut only if the sentence collapses under its own weight.

- **Repetition of a word or phrase across a chapter** — this can be structural, not accidental. Eira's chapters may return to a specific technical term; Marcus's to a phrase he is interrogating; Hannah's to a word in two languages. Check before cutting a repeat.
