# Drafter guidance — Coldwater Reach

## Voice register

This is literary adult fiction — the pipeline's native register. Restrained, precise, third-person limited past, trusting the reader. The closest shelf analogues are Elizabeth Strout's *Olive Kitteridge* (tightly bound multi-POV, coastal Maine, the weight of what is not said between people who share a small world) and Ron Hansen's *The Assassination of Jesse James* in its procedural calm — the world rendered through its work and its weather, not its feelings about itself. The prose earns its emotional weight through specificity, not declaration.

The critical drafter challenge here is not register — this is the default — it is **maintaining three distinct voices within that shared register**. Eira, Hannah, and Marcus must sound like themselves at the sentence level, not like variants of the same narrator. See details below.

## Sentence-length expectations

These vary by POV character:

**Eira**: 
- Average: 10–16 words
- Maximum: 28 words; anything longer is register drift
- Short declaratives (4–7 words) at moments of extremity are correct — do not pad them

**Hannah**:
- Average: 12–20 words with substantial variance
- Her sentences can be longer and can interrupt themselves; a clause that trails off or pivots is within register
- Maximum: 35 words; her interiority is allowed but not sprawling

**Marcus**:
- Average: 15–22 words
- Subordinate clauses and careful qualification are correct; they are his register
- Maximum: 45 words; if longer, the sentence must be doing grammatical work, not simply being verbose

## Chapter-opening conventions

Establish which POV character you are in within the first sentence. Drop into their world immediately — sensory or professional, not abstract. 

- **Eira's chapters** should open on work or observation: tide log, the harbor, weather, the specific task of the morning. No meditation in the first paragraph. She is already doing something when we meet her.
- **Hannah's chapters** may open on sensation or domestic detail (the cold of the barn, the smell of silage, the sound of her father's German from the kitchen), then move inward. Adolescent interiority is permitted, but establish the physical world first.
- **Marcus's chapters** may open mid-observation or mid-thought, but the thought should be grounded in something specific: a class he is teaching, a student's face, the walk to school, something Henry said. Not in abstract rumination.

Do not open any chapter with weather description as scene-setting. Weather belongs inside a scene, noticed by a character who has a professional or practical reason to notice it.

## Chapter-ending conventions

This register leans heavily toward literary endings: **literary-fade**, **resolved-beat**, **turn**, and **coda**. Cliffhangers and mid-action cuts are rare and should be used deliberately, not as defaults.

For 30 chapters, a rough distribution:
- Literary-fade: ~10 chapters (especially Eira's)
- Resolved-beat: ~8 chapters
- Turn (reveal or pivot): ~5 chapters (sparingly; used when a POV's knowledge shifts)
- Coda: ~4 chapters (the brief aftermath-beat)
- Cliffhanger or mid-action: 2–3 chapters maximum (and these should feel earned, not genre-default)

Do not end consecutive chapters the same way. Check the craft log.

## POV strategy

Three co-leads with roughly equal weight (~10 chapters each). The rotation is **asymmetric** — do not mechanically alternate. Follow the story's logic: if Eira's thread has momentum, two consecutive Eira chapters is correct. If Marcus needs a long development, give it to him.

The **three leads never appear in the same scene simultaneously**. They cross in pairs (Eira and Marcus may speak; Marcus and Hannah have the teacher-student channel; Eira and Albrecht have the milk-delivery contact) but never as a trio.

Occasional **fourth-POV chapters** (Henry Crow, Lonzo Reed, or Karl Reinmar) are permitted but should be exceptional — use them when a piece of the breakwater mystery or a piece of the thematic weight is only visible from outside the main three. These chapters should be noticeably shorter than the main-POV chapters. Do not use them to resolve plot questions that the main three cannot see — use them to reframe a question the main three are already carrying.

## Dialogue density

Dialogue carries social texture and subtext, not exposition. A chapter's dialogue load should feel natural to the setting: the harbormaster's office has different rhythms than the school or the Crow kitchen. Hannah's chapters may have less dialogue (more interior), Eira's more (she transacts with people), Marcus's is mid-range.

Rules:
- No character explains what they mean for the reader's benefit.
- No character repeats information the POV character already knows.
- Tags: "said" and "asked" are correct. Avoid "countered," "admitted," "ventured," and all other gloss-tags.
- Do not follow a line of dialogue with a sentence explaining what the speaker meant by it (dialogue gloss failure mode).

**German in Hannah's chapters**: when Albrecht speaks to Hannah in German, or when a German phrase surfaces in Hannah's interior, render it in German without translation. The meaning should be inferable from context. Do not supply the English in brackets or in a following clause.

**Marcus's English**: formally correct, one register above the conversation he is in. He does not use contractions freely; he chooses his words deliberately. This should feel natural by the middle of his first chapter, not like a speech impediment.

## Comic timing and tonal balance

This is not a comic register. The tonal signature is gravity: the weight of loss, of partial knowledge, of lives proceeding under constraint. There is warmth (especially in the Crow household scenes) and there is dry observation (especially in Eira's narration of the town's behavior toward her), but these are within a serious frame. Do not deflect serious scenes with humor. Do not deflect humor with sentimentality.

The closest thing to comic timing in this novel is **Eira's dry observational mode** — her narration of what the town thinks about her, what the Coast Guard lieutenant does not ask, what the ledger records and what it does not. This is wry, not comic. It is the humor of competence in an incompetent situation.

## The breakwater mystery: structural, not genre

The body on the breakwater is a structural spine — it is what the three characters separately carry, what orients each arc, what the reader uses to track how the three threads relate. It is **not a mystery novel**. The drafter must not let it generate genre-mechanical scenes: no interrogation sequences, no code-breaking, no chapter that reads as investigation. 

What is correct:
- A character notices something that recontextualizes what we already know
- A character is forced to act (or not act) on their piece of knowledge
- Two characters' knowledge proximity is almost visible to us but not to them

What is wrong:
- A character actively investigates (goes to the Coast Guard, asks around town)
- The mystery is "solved" mid-novel
- Any chapter whose function is primarily exposition of the mystery

## Anti-defaults

Because this is the pipeline's native register, the usual anti-drift warnings don't apply. The specific drift risks for this book are:

1. **Voice homogenization**: Eira, Hannah, and Marcus all running in the same prose register — spare literary — without the specific differentiation the brief demands. Check: does Eira's chapter have the short declarative rhythm? Does Marcus have the subordinate clause pattern? Does Hannah have German vocabulary and internal interruption?

2. **The mystery becoming a plot**: Any scene that functions as "investigation" rather than "character in their world encountering their piece of what happened" is drift. The body is a load the characters carry; it does not drive their actions.

3. **The war becoming foreground**: Radio news, casualty telegrams, D-Day — these should arrive as weather, not as event. A sentence, not a scene. The characters' responses to them are interior (usually) or brief external (Eira, who notes things in ledgers).

4. **Period texture as set decoration**: Ration books and V-Mail should not appear as atmospheric props. They should appear because a character needs them, waits for them, is angered or relieved by them. If a period detail has no function in the POV character's immediate life, cut it.

5. **The body becoming a symbol**: The unknown man on the breakwater is a dead person. He is not a symbol of the war, or of displacement, or of anything else. The themes arise from the characters' lives — not from the body as symbol. Let him be a dead man.
